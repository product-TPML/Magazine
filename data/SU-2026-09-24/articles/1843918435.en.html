When we think of a fox, the first thing that usually comes to mind is the jackal. However, there are four animals belonging to the fox family in India: the jackal, the Indian fox, the desert fox, and the Tibetan sand fox. Among these, apart from the jackal, another fox species found in our state is the Indian fox, or the small fox.

Although the Indian fox looks somewhat like a jackal, there is a clear difference in its coat color and size. Its distinctive features include a light brown body, a thick bushy tail with a black tip, and wide, long ears. These large ears help regulate body temperature for this animal, which lives in hot, dry grassland areas. Overall, it is a very cute little animal.

An adult male Indian fox usually weighs less than 3.5 kg. It is the smallest wild animal belonging to the dog family in India.

The diet of the Indian fox is also diverse. It eats small mammals like rodents and rabbits, birds, reptiles, bird eggs, and various insects. It also consumes fruits such as Chotte Mullu, Sulli, Bore, Nerale, Kakke, and Amte. By eating these fruits, their seeds are spread to distant areas through their droppings, playing a significant role in the seed dispersal of these plants. In addition, they play an important role in controlling the population of rodents and insects that cause damage to agriculture. Rodents are one of the main foods of the Indian fox, and studies conducted in various regions of India have shown that 40-80% of their diet consists of rodent remains. For this reason, Indian foxes can be called farmers' friends.

In grasslands, Indian foxes raise their young in burrows that are two to three feet deep and have several entrances. They reuse the burrows they build again and again.

For Indian foxes to be able to spot enemies from a distance, the height of the grass must be lower than their eye level. Since livestock graze in grasslands, the grass does not grow excessively tall. For this reason, the coexistence of Indian foxes and livestock is essential for this ecosystem.

The habitats of the Indian fox are rapidly being destroyed by agricultural expansion, tree planting programs, urbanization, industrial development, and other human activities. In addition, vehicle accidents, poaching, disease spread by stray dogs, and excessive pesticide use have contributed to their population decline.

About twenty years ago, I saw an Indian fox near what is now Sahakarnagar in Bengaluru. At that time, the area was covered with shrubs and open fields. If I go there now, it is difficult to even imagine that I once saw an Indian fox there. This change is a clear example of how rapidly the grassland environment around us is disappearing.g
