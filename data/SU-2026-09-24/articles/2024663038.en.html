Drosera; Insect Hunter

Some plants catch insects and obtain the nutrients they need from them. Drosera is one such carnivorous plant. This plant has another name, 'Sundew'. Many species of this plant, belonging to the Droseraceae family, are found in India. They mostly grow in moist grasslands, marshy areas, around water sources, and humid regions. It has been recorded that these species are found in many districts of the state.

The basic characteristic of this plant is to catch and eat small insects. Thus, it got the name carnivorous plant. The delicate pink flowers attract. There are many microscopic glandular filaments like hairs on the leaves. At the tip of these, shiny, sticky liquid droplets look like dewdrops, hence the name 'Sundew'. As soon as insects get stuck in this sticky liquid, the filaments on the leaf slowly bend towards the insect. In some species, the leaf part also folds around the insect. Then digestive enzymes are released from special glands and digest the insect's body. The plant absorbs the nutrients obtained from it.

–Gayathri S.N., Shivamogga

Crimson Forest Roselle Flower

When a crimson flower suddenly bloomed on a plant that had sprouted by the roadside, the whole plant looked attractive. The flower of that very familiar plant resembles a hibiscus flower. The green leaf has a similarity to the okra plant. When I searched on Google, I found out that it is indeed a plant of the okra family. Its botanical name is 'Hibiscus cannabinus'. The leaves taste sour. Later I learned that it is 'Pundi Palle' or 'Wild variety of Gongura greens'. It has names like 'Mullu Pundi', 'Holada Pundi', 'Kadu Pundi'. Most people mistakenly interpret the leaves of this plant as bhang leaves. There is no connection between this and bhang leaves. Because of the abundant fiber, wild pundi is used instead of jute.

–Vishwanath Bhat, Dharwad

The disappearing wooden 'Manth'

The picture shows an ancient wooden churner. This is a wooden device for churning curd. In North Karnataka, it is called 'Manthani', and in Tulu Nadu of the coastal region, it is called 'Manth'. These are carved from hard woods like jackfruit or teak. There is also a belief that food items do not spoil because these trees naturally have medicinal properties. The lower part of the churner is shaped like flower petals and has a special design. This helps to churn the curd quickly and easily lift the butter.

In ancient times, curd and water were put into it, and a long rope was coiled around the churner. These can still be seen in some rural houses. Two people or even one person would churn rhythmically by pulling both ends of the rope back and forth. Nowadays, mixers have replaced these, and heat is generated in them, destroying the microorganisms in the curd. Churning curd in a wooden device provides cool and naturally beneficial buttermilk.

–Raman Shetty Renjala, Mumbai

Yali Sculpture on the Pillar

Under the group 'Great Living Chola Temples', the Brihadeeswara Temple of Thanjavur, the Gangaikonda Choleswara Temple of Gangaikonda Cholapuram, and the Airavateswara Temple of Darasuram are included. These have been recognized as World Heritage Sites by UNESCO. These sites, filled with a peaceful atmosphere and artistic richness, can be seen as a cultural treasure of South India.

Intricate stone carvings and elegant sculptures can be seen on the outer walls of the temple. The outer mandapa has 108 different pillars. On them, one can see the architecture and sculpture of South Indian Hindu temples, referred to as 'acrobats' and 'Yali'. The art carved on the pillar symbolizes the mythical/fictional animal Yali. It is also called 'Vyala'. It is believed to be a very powerful creature that embodies the strength and characteristics of several animals like lions, elephants, horses, and snakes.

The architecture and paintings of this temple reflect the rich cultural heritage of the Chola dynasty. Numerous paintings, as well as sculptures from the Vedas and Puranas, are carved here. The Airavateswara Temple is located about 40 km from Thanjavur in Tamil Nadu.

–S. Prabhavathi, Bengaluru

The Foundation of Vessels, this 'Shimbe'

This is a 'Shimbe' or 'Simbe' made by weaving coconut coir rope into a circular shape. In olden days, this 'Shimbe' was commonly seen on women's heads. Back then, women would draw water from the well and fill it into pots, then place the 'Shimbe' on their heads and carry the pot on top of it. It was also used in the kitchen to prevent vessels from toppling over. Steel stands have now taken its place, and 'Shimbe's have disappeared. Recently, seeing one in a house in Goa reminded me of old times.

–Lakshman Gorlakatte, Mysuru

Hanuman's Vow Tree

The vow tree seen in the picture is located in front of the Anjaneya temple on Anjanadri hill (Kishkinda). Devotees tie coconuts wrapped in saffron cloth to this tree and make vows. It is believed that doing so fulfills their wishes. According to the temple priests, this vow has been a tradition for centuries, and the tree itself is a witness to hundreds of years of history.

–Shashirekha Nagendra, Tumakuru

Remnants of the Palegars' Rule

The 'Jarimale Palegar family' is one of the prominent families that ruled Karnataka. This family, which ruled for more than 200 years with Jarimale in Kudligi taluk of Vijayanagara district as its capital, still resides in Jarimale. The four or five temples, wells, forts, fort gates, and lakes built during the Palegars' rule can now be seen in a place called 'Hāḷūru' (ruined village). Some monuments have been damaged by the ravages of time. The gateway we are seeing in the picture reminds us of the past glory of the Palegars' rule. This area was also a settlement of early humans, and tourists can admire the drawings made by the people of that era here.

–Shekharayya T.H.M. Geddalagatte
