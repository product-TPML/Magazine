Devu Pattar

Who is this Gawan? The answer to this seemingly simple question is not so simple. Broadly speaking, he was a person who lived in Deccan - Bidar in the fifteenth century. Saying just that doesn't say anything about Gawan. Gawan, who was a poet, writer, and scholar, made extraordinary, rare, and unique contributions to the social and cultural fields, including education. Gawan, who was associated with four Bahmani Sultans, had the full and real name Khwaja Imaduddin Mahmud Gilani. Mahmud's family elders, who were residents of the city of Rasht, belonging to the Gilan province in northern Iran, held the reins of administration there.

Gawan's uncle, Khwaja Shamsuddin, was the Prime Minister of Gilan. Gawan, who was trained by his uncle in administrative matters, and his family members faced difficulties due to changed political power and conspiracies. Realizing the impending danger, Gawan and his brother (Shamsuddin's son Muhammad) had to leave the country. Gawan's mother also supported this. While Muhammad traveled towards Mecca, Gawan went towards Iraq - Khorasan. Gawan, who wanted to establish himself in trade, rejected the high position of Prime Minister offered by the rulers of Iraq during his travels.

From his childhood, Gawan had a special interest in reading and studies and wished to be in the company of scholars. There is no definite information about which countries Gawan visited during this period. However, he visited almost all the regions where traders used to go at that time and also achieved success in trade. Gawan, being a trader, did not limit his travels to commercial purposes during his wanderings. He spent time with experts from various fields, including poets, scholars, Sufis, and saints in the areas he visited, and exchanged ideas.

Mahmud Gawan, born in Khawan near Rasht in 1410, thought of coming to India at the age of forty-three. India, rich for commercial and cultural reasons, was a center of attraction. In 1453, he landed at the Dabhol port on the west coast. He had thought of going to Delhi for trade. Before going to Delhi, he changed the direction of his journey towards Bidar with the intention of sitting at the feet of Muhibullah Kirmani, the grandson of the saint-poet Niyamatullah Kirmani, who was in the Deccan. That changed the course of Gawan's and Deccan's history.

When Gawan came to Bidar, Alauddin Bahmani II (1437-1457) was the ruler. The meeting with Shah Muhibullah also led to a confrontation between Gawan and Alauddin. Alauddin, who had a special interest in literature and culture, could not fail to realize Gawan's importance. At Alauddin's insistence, Gawan settled in Bidar. He also got a high position. Along with that, Gawan also got an opportunity to prove his ability. Jalal Khan, the Subedar of the Telangana province, rebelled. Jalal Khan, who was also the Sultan's brother-in-law, also had the support of Mahmud Shah Khalji of the neighboring Malwa country. The responsibility of the military operation fell on Gawan's shoulders. Gawan besieged Jalal Khan's Nalgonda fort and ensured that Jalal Khan was forced to surrender. Along with accepting tribute from the defeated and surrendered Jalal Khan, he returned the authority of the province with a warning policy. Both Gawan's success and statesmanship were dear to the Sultan.

Before Gawan came to Bidar, the administration of the Bahmani Empire was divided into two groups. Sultan Firoz Shah's (1371-1422) policy of giving preference to newly arrived Persians (Afaqis) had caused dissatisfaction among the local Deccanis who had been there from the beginning. At that time, Gulbarga was the capital of the Bahmanis. After Firoz Shah, Ahmad Shah Ali 'Wali' (1422-1457) who came to power, shifted the capital to Bidar. But he continued the policy of giving recognition to the newly arrived Persians. That caused resentment among the local Deccanis. The Deccanis had the support of the Negro Habshis. These two groups had reached a level where they harbored mutual hatred. Not only that, they did not even stay together when fighting against enemy armies. This dissatisfaction and hatred had reached such a stage that the leadership of the war on Khelna in Konkan was with Khalaf Hasan Basri of the Afaqi group. When Basri's army was fighting with enemies in the dense forest, the Deccani soldiers, far from providing assistance, were silent spectators to the mass slaughter of their own army. Thousands of people from the Afaqi group of the Bahmani army, including Basri, were brutally killed. To make matters worse, the Deccani soldiers had lodged a complaint against the Afaqis. The Sultan, believing it, ordered action against the Afaqis. Later, after realizing the reality of the events that took place on the battlefield, action also had to be taken against the Deccanis. Overall, mutual distrust and conspiracies were common in Bidar. This led to the administrative machinery reaching the point of collapse. The dissatisfaction that began during Firoz's time continued during Ahmad Ali's time and grew during Alauddin II's reign. Annoyed by this, Alauddin ordered that the Deccanis be on the left side and the Afaqis on the right side in the court and the army. As a result, the factions were officially divided into left and right. Gawan tried to unite these two factions. However, the Deccanis, who considered Gawan an Afaqi, viewed all of Gawan's efforts with suspicion.

Humayun Shah (1458–1461), who came to power after Alauddin, was a strange personality recorded in the pages of history. Old historians like Ferishta and Syed Ali Tabatabai, who wrote that Humayun was extremely cruel 'Zalim', colorfully narrated stories of his cruelty. They recorded it as a bloody chapter. However, modern historians like Sherwani do not agree with this. A few days after Humayun came to power, a rebellion broke out again in Telangana. Humayun himself led it. Gawan was also with him in the military operation. A conspiracy in Bidar made Humayun furious. In his absence, his brother Sikandar was enthroned. Humayun, who instructed Gawan to continue the war, returned to Bidar and severely punished everyone involved in the conspiracy. As if Humayun had a premonition of his assassination, he formed a regency council comprising his Maharani Makdum-e-Jahan (Mistress of the World) Nargis Begum, Khwaja Jahan Turk, and Mahmud Gawan. After Humayun, his son, the eight-year-old Nizamuddin Shah (1461–1463), came to power. Although Nizamuddin was on the throne, the real administration was that of the regency council. During those days, Gawan's foreign policy helped protect the interests of the empire. When Mahmud Shah Khalji of Malwa attacked, Gawan sought military assistance from Mahmud Shah of Gujarat. This cordial relationship with Gujarat continued in later days as well.

Nizamuddin Shah breathed his last on the first night of his marriage. Then his brother, the nine-year-old Mahmud III (1463–1481), took power. Even then, the regency council was in power. The administration run by Nargis Begum with the help of both and the decisions she took were significant. Nargis Begum, realizing Khwaja Jahan Turk's behavior and ambition for power, did not hesitate to order his assassination (1465) in a full assembly. After Turk's assassination, Nargis Begum took charge of the administration with Gawan's help.

To prevent the menace of pirates, which was a major obstacle to trade, Gawan led an expedition against the forts of Konkan. It was not easy to capture these forts located in the dense forests of the Western Ghats. However, Gawan's war strategy and shrewdness came to his aid. After three years of continuous effort, all the forts of Konkan, including the important port city of Goa, came under Gawan's control. The victory of Goa (1471) is recognized as a significant milestone in Gawan's military achievements. The celebration of this victory lasted for 30 days in Bidar. The greatest victory achieved by the Bahmanis led to a triumphant celebration.

The Maharani and the Sultans themselves visited Gawan's house. Nargis Begum and Gawan met face-to-face for the first time. (Until then, they had been communicating through an assistant named Ma Banu). The Maharani addressed Gawan as 'brother'. It was an emotional moment. As a mark of respect, the Sultan placed his royal robe on Gawan. Gawan, taken aback by this, felt embarrassed. He made excuses and sent them away. Then, dressed in simple white clothes like a dervish, he sat on a mat spread in a corner and began to cry. His companions were surprised. Gawan's behavior at this most joyous moment caused wonder. When asked about it, Gawan lamented, 'For what mistake? For a few moments, the royal robe fell on me.' This incident is like a testament to Gawan's honesty and goodness.

The battle against Belgaum witnessed the first use of gunpowder in India. This led to changes in the construction of forts and military tactics in India. Nargis Begum died while returning from this war. From then on, the responsibility of administration fell on Gawan.

On one hand, Gawan expanded the state's borders through military operations, and at the same time, he also focused on administrative reforms. The empire stretched from the eastern coast to the western coast. Initially, the empire was divided into four provinces for administration. Even after expansion, the same system continued. Realizing its shortcomings, Gawan undertook reforms. He divided the provinces of Gulbarga, Daulatabad, Telangana, and Berar into 1) Bijapur, 2) Gulbarga, 3) Daulatabad, 4) Junnar, 5) Rajamahendri, 6) Warangal, 7) Gavil, and 8) Mahur. At the same time, he introduced a system of land measurement to fix definite land revenue. This system initiated by Gawan was later reformed by Malik Ambar in Ahmednagar and Raja Todarmal in Delhi. Two hundred years later, when the British started measuring land, only 80% of the Deccan region had been measured in the entire country.

Gawan, who sought to free the administration from corruption, interpreted the lack of funds to meet essential needs as the cause of corruption and doubled the salaries of soldiers and staff. His reforms gained public approval but also caused dissatisfaction among those with vested interests.

Gawan, himself a poet and scholar, was also an extraordinary lover of education. The 'Madrasa' started by Gawan in Bidar is a significant contribution to the history of medieval India. It had a library with more than three thousand books. Many subjects including mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, literary criticism, linguistics, and medicine were taught there. Gawan, who liked to spend his free time in the company of scholars, also taught at the Madrasa. Ferishta recorded that among his contemporaries, few were equal to him in mathematics. Gawan, who was interested in botany, brought the grafting technology from Iran to India. This led to the development of various varieties of mangoes. Gawan is also credited with cultivating saffron in Bidar.

Gawan's collection of poems 'Diwan-e-Arsh' is lost. However, parts of poems mentioned elsewhere remain as examples of his poetic skill. The work 'Manazir-ul-Insha', a treatise on language and literary criticism, remains a testament to Gawan's scholarship and his ability to interpret and analyze. The collection of letters 'Riyaz-ul-Insha', written to family members, rulers, administrators, scholars, Sufis, and poets, is a historical work of great importance. The letters inviting people to Bidar bear witness to his concern and interest in education. The Persian poet Abdurrahman Jami, who was close to Gawan, mentioned in a letter to Gawan about 'creating a Deccan that Rome would envy'. Jami's Qasida (eulogy) about Gawan is one of the rare recognitions Gawan received.

Gawan, who was a confluence of honesty, scholarship, and goodness, became a victim of a conspiracy fueled by dissatisfaction and jealousy. The innocent life that fought against corruption succumbed to the corruption of his seal-bearing assistant. The conspirators, who lured the assistant and succeeded in getting a blank paper sealed, added a request to the ruler of Orissa, Dali Mau, to attack. This was presented to the Sultan, who was intoxicated. An inquiry was conducted on April 4, 1481, in Kondapalli. Gawan said, 'The seal is mine, but I know nothing about the content of the letter.' The Sultan was not in a state to listen. The ruler got up and left without saying anything. Gawan turned towards the Kaaba and sat down to pray. At the same time, Johar's sword separated his head from his body. The innocent Gawan was assassinated. By the time the Sultan woke up the next day, it was too late. Gawan's body was sent to Bidar with all honors. He was cremated in a mango grove near Khwaja Jahan Talab (lake), built by Gawan in Goranahalli, on the outskirts of Bidar.

The news of Gawan's assassination caused a stir in Mecca, a fact recorded by the contemporary scholar and historian Al-Sakhawi. It also mentions a mourning assembly held in Mecca. The Sultan realized his mistake. The Sultan, suffering from mental derangement, died before completing a year. A few years later, the Bahmani Empire broke up into five new kingdoms. The Deccan region was ruled by five different dynasties, including the Adil Shahi, Qutb Shahi, Nizam Shahi, and Barid Shahi.

A folk tale about Gawan's personality tells how he did not eat the mango given by his mother alone, but brought it along, planted it as a sapling, and grew a tree, thus spreading the mango lineage. It is also a metaphor.
