May victory always be theirs

K. Nirmalamaradihalli, Chitradurga
Art: Manjunath Hulakoppa

Is this the rainy season? It poured and poured at the beginning. After the planting was all done, there was a scorching sun like summer. Who listens to their constant grumbling? She was immersed in that world, weeding in the water-filled paddy field, her saree tucked up above her knees. There was no difference in the leaves between the paddy and these weeds. Only when the ears of grain appeared would one know which was paddy and which was a weed. Until then, the water and fertilizer given to the paddy would be brazenly absorbed by the weeds. When two or three more leaves appeared, one should look for the red base and pull it out with the root. If the base of the paddy is white, the weed plant that looks like paddy has a red base. From above, everything looks lush green.

Suresh, breathing heavily, swaying on the bund, ran without noticing the water in the field, hugged his mother, and started crying, "Ho...". As he said, "Mother... all three buffaloes are dead," she threw the chair aside and stood up. They both untucked their sarees and walked towards the house. What happened to the buffaloes? I left them to graze in Gowda's backyard and came for work. I was about to sit down for lunch when this news came!

Jayasheela was from Bilki Kanhalli. Her father raised four daughters through daily wage labor and married off his eldest daughter, Jayasheela, to Kappanahalli. Chenna had a tiled house in front of the backyard and two acres of dry land. If they worked and ate, there would be no trouble in life. Chenna also had no one. His father had died when he was young. Last year, his mother also died from a snake bite. Then relatives gathered and arranged his marriage with Jayasheela. She planted a Sampige tree on the platform in front of the house, visible from where they sat. Both were good workers. They grew horse gram and jowar on their dry land and worked as laborers in paddy fields.

In ten years of marriage, they had a son and a daughter. The elder one was Suresh. The girl was Sukeshini. Jayasheela had sent her to her mother's house in Sigematti, where she was studying in the third grade with her grandmother. In a village of one hundred and fifty houses, she never refused anyone who called her for work. She would look everywhere, without a moment to breathe, saying, "I'll come tomorrow. This and that," managing to not miss any work she could get. The Sampige tree in front of the house grew, full of flowers, smiling. Sometimes, both Jayasheela and Chenna would go for paddy planting. While planting side by side on the same sheet, everyone would laugh heartily at Chenna and Jayasheela's conversations. They would laugh until they were tired. People would praise their enthusiasm and love, their longing to be inseparable.

When Chenna said, "This dark-skinned Jayasheela was married to me by her father," she would say, "You wouldn't find a beauty like me even in heaven." In the water-filled paddy field, holding a bundle of saplings in her left hand, she would take two or three saplings with her right hand, plant them in the mud, and move forward without looking back, wanting to be ahead. Chenna would often splash water from the field onto Jayasheela's face instead of planting saplings with his right hand. Wiping her face with her saree, she would fall behind for a moment, but then, showing she was no less, she would put down the bundle of saplings in her left hand and splash water from the field with both hands, drenching Chenna. Before he could say "Hoo ha...", she would be ahead in planting. Their mischief was famous throughout the village, and people called them Muddanna and Manorame.

In life, the burden of love was greater than the burden of work. Others eagerly sought to work with them, saying that when the two of them were together, they wouldn't even notice the work getting done. In addition to all this, Chenna never missed going to the wrestling arena at least twice a week. Every year, on the evening of the Ranganatha Swamy fair, all the villagers would gather to watch the lifting of the stone weights in front of the wrestling arena. However, women were not allowed to watch, being told that their hair would fall out if they did. Nevertheless, some would secretly stand here and there, without telling their family, to watch. They wouldn't miss the feat of lifting the wrestling arena's stone weights, placing them on their shoulders, rolling them over their necks, and then lowering them again with the help of their left side. This feat happened every year.

The first five or six weights were lifted by the boys training in the wrestling arena. Then, the weights were larger in size, round, and oiled to make them slippery. This was a challenging task. Chenna's calloused hands, hardened by hard work, gripped the stones firmly like a monitor lizard. What good fortune it was, no cruel eye had fallen upon Chenna and Jayasheela. Their lives progressed in tune with summer, monsoon, and winter. So did the Sampige tree.

They had sheep at home, some of which were pregnant, and some had given birth to lambs. These lambs roamed around the house like kings. Jayasheela had raised more than fifty chickens. For that, she had built a chicken coop next to the veranda. The chickens roamed freely in the courtyard, on the steps, and inside the central room. Chicken droppings were common everywhere. Just last summer, she was happily setting aside eggs, intending to put five hens to brood. As the hens came to brood, she put dry straw in a basket, arranged twenty-one eggs widely in each basket, and then gently placed the brooding hen in four directions. She didn't forget to place the basket on a stand. If it was placed on the ground, it would be over. The menace of mongooses. Earlier, when chickens were set to brood in the corner of the central room, oh my... chicken lice, lice everywhere. It was only when the itching started on the bodies of family members and visitors that they realized. They were smaller than ants and went unnoticed. They had to endure this due to lack of space to keep chickens. Now, they had built a roof adjoining the house wall for tying sheep and cattle, plastered the ground, and leveled it properly. This provided more space for other tasks, and it was as if Jayasheela had four hands.

There was plenty of space around the house. In the backyard, they grew beans, pigeon peas, bananas, papayas, guavas—not just one or two, but everything. Their son, as soon as he came from school, would throw his bag and run to the backyard. In front of the house, on one side, were full of double petaled flowers. White, red, blue, not just one or two colors. The large double petaled flowers shone like the chakra in Lord Krishna's hand. The Sampige tree, planted when she got married, exuded fragrance and seemed to stretch its branches wide. That day was Mannettin Amavasya, and though the rainy season had begun, the sky was clear. After dinner that night, Chenna and Jayasheela were sitting on the platform outside. The Sampige flowers and buds sparkled like stars in the courtyard. Chenna looked steadily at Jayasheela, who had lifted her face towards the glittering stars, and said, "How many more years will we keep working like this? If our strength diminishes and diseases come..." As he spoke, she interrupted, "Phoo, let it be, don't say such things!" Seeing them look at each other, their son Suresh jumped in, clapped his hands, and started laughing. They would tell themselves, "We must work hard, we must prosper. Our children's lives should be better than ours."

I don't want to end Jayasheela's laughter here. I'll continue a little. When she went to Shanthamma's house to prepare green gram, two other women had also come. They were drinking buttermilk in between, joking and laughing. "We bought new clothes for the festival, sister... 'old husband even for a new festival'," they would laugh so hard it felt like the sky would tear. This time, they planned to use the society's money to dig a borewell in their dry land. They dreamed of planting areca nuts, and inside, betel leaves, pepper, cardamom, to be a support for their son's future. They worked for daily life, saved what was left, and merged with the soil. The belief that Mother Earth would never abandon them was ingrained in their blood. It was her habit to apply a pinch of soil below her forehead kumkum.

Jayasheela, who had run over, and her son Suresh saw the three dead buffaloes, and her grief was so overwhelming that she cried out, "Hayya... hayya..." and shook them. She shook their horns and looked. Stroking their bodies, she cried out, "Manaka... get up!" The big buffalo, the white buffalo, and Manaka were life within her life. The white buffalo was the daughter of the big buffalo, and Manaka was her daughter. They provided a continuous flow of milk throughout the year. There was never a shortage of milk, curd, butter, or ghee at home, and she would send it not only to her husband and son but also to her daughter's maternal home. Chenna, who heard the news, also came. The depth of their sorrow was indescribable.

People gathered and consoled them. Jayasheela used to let the buffaloes graze in Gowda's backyard when she went for work. No one saw them eating the dried linseed plants spread out in the nearby threshing floor. If they had seen, they would have chased them away. They had eaten the thorny dried linseed, their stomachs had bloated, and they had died unable to breathe. Unable to digest this shock, the house felt like a cremation ground, and looking at the cattle shed brought pain. The sun seemed to lean towards the setting direction, and a fearful anxiety enveloped Jayasheela, as if her smile was dying. After the buffaloes died, a feeling of helplessness enveloped Chenna, and he wished for their children to stay with them, so he called their daughter, who was at her grandmother's house. She forgot to water the Sampige tree. The tree's leaves silently withered and drooped. Whenever Chenna and Jayasheela, who believed life would go on beautifully, wondered why this happened, they would look at the sky. Falling and rising are part of life. There are no questions for those who take it lightly. For those who wish things to be a certain way, there is a shower of troubles. To forget by working more, Chenna prepared to plant coconut saplings along the fence in the backyard and front yard. He also talked about making a garden by combining Jayasheela's society money and his chit fund money, trying to bring cheerfulness back into the house.

The son and daughter danced and played, getting drenched in the water gushing from the borewell, forgetting themselves. In this situation, it was also imperative for Chenna to make a garden. The paddy field owners around them had already started gardens, and the shade of their areca nut trees fell on his crops, blocking the sunlight, and the yield was drastically reduced. Now, Chenna's family had no leisure. The sheep, chickens, and their combined wages kept their lives going without much trouble. Shindmuga Aiyanna told them, while they were sitting and talking in front of the Hanuman temple, that there was a business opportunity for all the fields upstream of the Kumudhwati river, about ten kilometers from the village.

Big shots from Bangalore, coming in big cars, had started the work of tempting people by quoting prices per acre. For this, they had made their own people agents and fixed commissions for those who arranged the deals. Listening to their buttery words, these charmers would strip them bare without them even realizing what they were losing. Some, enchanted by this, pursued landowners and troubled them. Now, everyone was occasionally losing sleep over the suspicion of what danger awaited their village, what hawk's eye would fall upon it. What kind of trouble is this? No one can live peacefully. Mountains and hills cannot exist; there's a hurry to cut them down and make sites or something else. Lakes cannot exist, forests, even trees, are destroyed with a hundred thoughts. Nature also tolerates and waits to take revenge. Did they forget how they hid in corners during the Corona period? The beautiful hill that looked so good when coming from Bangalore has now been disfigured like Shurpanakha with her nose cut off. And when coming from Davangere, the beautiful stone hill has been carved and carved, made to look like Ravana with his ten heads cut off. Wherever you go, such deformities greet you. Nowadays, it's not just fields and gardens, but evil forces are invading homes. If life becomes too commercialized, the words of values that cannot be matched by price will cease to exist.

Although Chenna did not know the motto, 'Don't make property for children, make children into property,' his thought was exactly that. He educated his son in a good college. But for two years, he struggled to find a proper job. His daughter, in her final year of studies, married the one she loved and left. Jayasheela now found it difficult to go for daily wage work and was doing small tasks in her own garden. Now, the income from the garden was her support. The flowers of the Sampige tree in front of the house, with no one to pluck and wear them, fell to the ground. She would pluck as many as she could reach and with a long stick, offer them to God. Many changes had occurred in the village. The wrestling arena rarely opened its doors. Chenna would go, sit on the platform, and come back. The youngsters of today, lacking the patience and interest to follow the rules of the wrestling arena, were leaning towards gyms. It was as if they were stumbling, unable to stop the tsunami of changes. Government cars came and went to Mudavaladavara's house, where all the important village matters were discussed. Even though people thought this was not related to them and remained silent, it stood at their roots like a haunting ghost. Even though the lands in the southern part of the village had no irrigation facilities, they had dug borewells and started gardens. A notice from the government reached the houses, indicating a warning of danger to more than five hundred acres of land, and it felt as if the ground beneath them had collapsed. They cried out, unable to speak or move. How could they be told to abandon the gardens they had made by pouring in hard-earned money, which hadn't existed until now? If they say water will fill everywhere here, what kind of justice is this? They should have been told when they were making the gardens. How can they say this after the harvest is in hand? This felt like greater oppression than the British. No one was asked when the dam's height was raised. If a system that disregards ordinary citizens still exists in this era, what kind of advanced era is this? There is no doubt that the cruel history of using humans in insensitive times is repeating itself.

Vasanth, Bangaranna's son who studied law, gathered all the garden owners and encouraged them, saying, "Let's fight legally. Let's get a stay order from the court." They vowed, "Let's protest and submit a petition to the District Collector. Let's inform all the media and get the support of all organizations and common people. We will never abandon our land." The trees in the garden swayed in the wind, as if waving their hands and crying out, "Save us, save us!"

Chenna and Jayasheela hugged the trees and shed tears. They loved the trees more than their children. The spreading betel leaves and cardamom vines seemed to be saying, "Mother... Mother." They didn't feel like going home. To snatch away the bond with one's own children and count money, phoo! In which country am I? What karmic deeds are happening in this land, the very foundation of humanity! If the fence itself eats the field? If the protectors become the killers? What will be the fate of the country? Thinking all this, people struggled like fish taken out of water.

Jayasheela, sitting on the platform of the house and looking at the Sampige tree, was leaning towards emptiness. Even if Chenna spoke to her, she would remain silent. Her attention was elsewhere. When she understood, there were words, anger. Why is life like this? No matter how much we struggle, it doesn't turn out as we wish. How much effort to make ourselves, our place, our lives beautiful. But everything changes due to others' decisions and their roles. It feels like there is no language for the pain of being outsiders in our own place. There seemed to be no awareness about the relationships of villages many miles away from the dam's height. No one informed the people. Some knew about this. But without informing everyone, they remained in their own state. Even if they ate from the same plate, these people would not stop cheating. No matter how much they thought, the outcome would not change. Could trees and plants be seen in the compensation money that would come? There was no strength to go for daily wage work as before. There were no secure jobs for the children in the cities. What to do, this was the worry...

Jayasheela, as usual, went to Mudavaladavara's house and said, "Sister... I'm going to ask you something, you shouldn't lie." "Oh... why would I lie at this age?" she said, and both went to the backyard and stood near the hibiscus plant.

Sister... did you know beforehand that all the gardens would be submerged by this dam? Lokamma's face turned pale at this sudden question, and she moved her eyeballs as if looking this way and that. "Why all that now..." she said, "Yes, dear, your brother was also asked about this. But for some reason, he remained silent. He was also the Taluk Board President, wasn't he... If we had known about this then, we could have made rules so that all these troubles wouldn't exist. 'Which snake is in which anthill,' people think everyone is their own." And she left.

People raised their voices for the daily struggles. Life can only be sustained by the humanity in one's heart. This truth, though late, was understood by everyone. Jayasheela had sent word to her daughter to come with her husband. She reassured her son, "If work is difficult in the city, just come back, son, you can be happy here." After a long time, her mind felt relieved. They cooked a festive meal at home and spent the entire day in the garden. They draped sarees on the trees and performed puja. They hugged and kissed the trees as if embracing their own mother. Tears flowed unknowingly. The birds that had left their nests returned, chirping and celebrating. Even though the dam's height was increased, a good decision came from above that water would be released after a certain level to save hundreds of acres of gardens. Everyone splashed colors, beat drums, and danced. They offered butter puja to Hanumanthadeva. That night, Chenna and Jayasheela sat on the platform, looking at the Sampige tree. The flowers, buds, and tiny buds laughed in clusters.
