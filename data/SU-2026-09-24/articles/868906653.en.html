Saroja Prakash

On the two-day weekend in Stockholm, the availability of buses and trains also decreases. Not just for hospitals and offices, but all staff in the system need a holiday, right?

That day, we had planned a visit to the organic farm of one of our Punjabi friends. But there was no suitable bus until then. When informed, the farm owner, Paras, said he would come and pick us up himself. We took the train to Märsta, then a bus, got off at the designated spot, and walked.

It was the end of July. In European countries, which are submerged in snow and darkness for six months of the year (usually from October to March), when summer arrives, every particle of the soil awakens. Wild plants sprout and bloom in every inch of space along the roadside, celebrating. As the days lengthen, the sun, which lasts until nine at night, drinks and the bees dance, sucking nectar.

In a field on one side of the road, the ears of a drying grain glittered like wheat. Nearby, four or five horses were grazing. I slowly stepped aside, plucked an ear of grain, and a tiny, soft wheat kernel peeked out. The ground there was moist.
After walking about one kilometer, Paras came in his car and picked us up.

The tall, well-built Paras was born and raised here. His father, Chandraprakash, came to Sweden in the eighties as a technician for employment and settled down. He bought land on the outskirts of Stockholm, built a house, and commuted to the city for work every day. As the family grew, on sunny days, he took coriander from a spice box from his hometown and planted it in the yard. He planted tomatoes and mint. It started growing beautifully. The black soil, a place that was once a forest, began to grow crops without much need for fertilizer. As the children grew up, along with their studies and jobs, they began experimenting with growing vegetables in their kitchen garden. Whenever Punjabi and Hindi relatives settled in Stockholm visited, they would ask them to grow more, to let their people taste the vegetables and greens from their hometown.

Chandraprakash's eldest son, Dr. Paras. Although he had a medical degree and was in his profession, he joined his father, and the three acres of land have now expanded to thirty-four acres. They started growing organic vegetables in about two acres of the yard. But not in a completely open space. Because Indian vegetables need plenty of sun. A minimum of twenty degrees Celsius heat is essential. This family, who visits their hometown once every two or three years, started bringing seeds with a license. Every packet of seeds brought from the Punjab Horticulture Department began to be tested. (We also took a handful of wheat to teach microgreens to our grandchildren.) Permission is given to carry 200 grams of seeds; if more is needed, a license must be obtained. The license fee is expensive. Therefore, if different types of seeds weighing several kilograms are brought once, they are grown again and again, and the seeds are preserved.

"These have now tasted Swedish water and soil and have become local crops," said Paras, smiling, as he showed us the taste of coriander sprouts that his father had been growing using the same seeds for 25 years.

Eight thin-walled polyhouses were built for Indian vegetables that require sun. We saw the flourishing vegetables in these polyhouses. Gourds, eggplants, bitter gourds, peppers, greens... When we entered the green chili cave, the pungent aroma filled our noses. Cucumbers, squash, mint, coriander, etc., were also grown directly in the ground.

Now, with the special care of Paras and his father, the vegetable growing program starts from March and April. In July, when the crop is ready, messages are broadcast in WhatsApp and Facebook groups. Usually, on Sundays from ten to three o'clock, interested people can visit the garden, harvest the desired crop themselves, pay for it, and take it away. This continues until the end of the harvest in August and September. After that, agricultural activities completely cease. Excess vegetables and greens go into the kitchen freezer and are used for winter cooking.

"Look how beautiful this salad leaf is," said Paras, handing us red amaranth leaves! We ate them, and they were very delicious. Although red amaranth always grew in our hometown, we had never eaten it raw. Various types of basil, various types of mint, red salad leaves resembling beetroot leaves...

"Oh, this is spreading like a weed and going beyond its boundaries," he said, pulling out a couple of roots. He warned those who were stepping nearby, "Oh, move aside a little, there's coriander growing there."

The vegetables are fertilized with manure made from horse dung. The neighbors have 40 horses. If manure is bought from them once, the needs of three summer crops are met. He explained that if it rains in between, the plants don't need water for three or four days, as the soil has the property of retaining water.

Outside one polyhouse, there were plants in grow bags that we had never seen before! "These are walnut saplings, and these are Kashmiri almond plants. We are preparing them to plant on the border of our farm. They will start bearing fruit after eight years of growth when planted outside. For now, they will all sit inside the polyhouse during winter," said Paras.

After a while, Chandraprakash appeared from somewhere. After introductions, he said, "Look at these bathua leaves," and handed us the leaves of a plant that had grown like a weed there. These are nutritious leaves that grow abundantly in North India, just like timare grows in our region. "This is also a free crop here; we distribute it for free to those who come to us. These leaves are rich in iron," he said. In a row nearby, beautiful tulip-like flower plants were visible.

"These are opium plants; we have a license to grow them. If you put their leaves in boiling water and make tea, you get good sleep, and blood pressure also decreases," Chandraprakash laughed. Immediately, Paras added, "But we are supplying it to others for research."
That day, the vegetables from our homeland filled not only our eyes and minds but also our bags.
