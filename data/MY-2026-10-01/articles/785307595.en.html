Raghu Bhat couldn't figure out where he was, no matter how much he calculated. He felt like he was stuck somewhere high up in the hills, without knowing the direction. But he wasn't gripped by the fear that the story was over. After all, Cheenkra was with him.

Hunting in the hills was one of Raghu's hobbies. The hill standing opposite his house seemed to constantly challenge him. Raghu would get furious at the way all the animals that raided the garden would descend from there and then return to the hill. Amidst the daily garden work, the society's squabbles, and the household chores, the hill that stood tall as night fell would provoke him.

At such times, he would take his gun and climb the hill. As soon as the workers left in the evening, Raghu would get ready. It was a purely English habit. He needed a gun, a headlamp, and a knife. Not just that, a jerkin, boots, an air pillow, a monkey cap, and a hat... In a small backpack, some beaten rice and a water bottle... His wife would laugh just looking at him. Cheenkra, with a blanket tied to his back, would say, 'You look very stylish, Anna,' as he praised him. This is how their hunting expedition would begin.

If they climbed the hill in the evening, they would descend into its inner valleys, climb again, and sit on the peak of the hill. Cheenkra knew about one or two ravines below. He could bet that some animal would definitely come there. If they found something there, they would shoot it, pick it up if it fell, come to the top of the hill, sleep, and start descending when the light of dawn appeared.

The story of the hunt was for Raghu, the hunt was for Cheenkra.

This time, as they were climbing, Cheenkra coaxed him, 'Anna, what if we go to the other hill? It's a bit less steep. There are more water sources, and probably more animals.' Raghu also felt a surge of excitement, and they descended and climbed the banana plantation hill beyond it.

Raghu felt that the water ravine was wider than his hill. Sitting on a rock some distance from the ravine, with his whole body turned into ears, he heard so many sounds. The gurgling of water was constant, while the wind, changing direction, tickled the branches of the trees, rustling them as if shaking braided hair, and moved forward. Various insects would chirp incessantly. Amidst that, there was a sudden rustling sound on the ground. Was it a mongoose, or a monitor lizard...? Based on how far the sound moved, one could identify whether it was a snake or a monitor lizard. Suddenly, a pangolin would leap from a high branch of a tree and land on a branch of some other tree below. Based on what it might have come down to eat, one had to identify the fruit of that tree.

Raghu was not very familiar with all this. Cheenkra, however, even if he didn't breathe there, would explain the details without missing a beat when the hunting ritual was over and they had reached the peak and rested. What happened at every moment, what kind of animal it was, why it flew or crawled, which tree it was, whether it had fruits or raw ones... This is how his description would go.

His description had the capacity to continue for any length of time. After midnight on the peak, the howling wind and cold would induce sleep. If Cheenkra brought some large logs and set them on fire, they would burn with crackling sparks. Their smoky smell would dissipate as the wind blew. The hunted animal was comfortably far away, and there were no tigers or wild dogs coming for its prey. If Raghu fell asleep, it meant Cheenkra also fell asleep. What was the point of him talking alone?

This time too, it was the same.

Raghu, sitting far from the ravine, knew from the sound that the bush beyond the water ravine had rustled, and something had come to the water ravine. As he estimated its height from the sound of it drinking water, Raghu pressed the headlamp, and as if by fate, the animal stopped abruptly, startled by the bright light. It was just another second to pull the trigger. The booming sound of the gun left the water ravine, hit leaf after leaf, pushed through the trees beyond, spread across the width of the hill, and faded somewhere in the distant mountain's hollow.

Cheenkra suddenly got up, ran to where the animal had fallen, and shook it. It still had a flicker of life. It kicked its leg a little, showing a moment of protest in its dying throes. Cheenkra gave it some water and sat there. It slowly breathed its last.

After sitting like that for about five minutes, Cheenkra said, 'Anna, let's go,' as he put the animal into a plastic sack. After all, it wasn't a big game. If it were a pig, there would be more work.

Raghu, loosening his legs to get down from the rock, slipped as if his grip had failed. Perhaps because he had pressed his foot down, he stumbled and sat down.

'What happened, Anna?' Cheenkra asked anxiously.

Groaning in pain, Raghu moaned, 'My leg seems to have twisted. I can't take a step, man...'

'Oh, Lord, this is difficult. Don't worry, let's limp our way up somehow. Then in the morning, we can go home slowly. Let's see if the leg gets better by then...' Cheenkra consoled him.

Raghu had never limped or sat like this all these years... He didn't remember even a single thorn pricking him. A couple of times, thorns had pricked Cheenkra's foot, and Raghu had removed them and bandaged it.

Now, his leg seemed to refuse to move at all. The pain was unbearable. Taking one step would cause a spasm up to his waist, making it unbearable.

Seeing him groan, Cheenkra said, 'Bear it, no matter how difficult it is, we'll climb up. It's not far, just a hundred or two hundred yards... It seems difficult because it's steep. Give me the torch here,' and putting his shoulder under Raghu's armpit, he made him take one step at a time, swaying... They climbed up. Because Raghu was swaying, Cheenkra's left shoulder seemed completely bruised.

Raghu had never touched Cheenkra until now. Not even when they went hunting. Just like when they carried loads in the garden, he would just lift the hunting gear a little. Today, as he took one step at a time on Cheenkra's shoulder, the excruciating pain settled deep into Raghu's core.

By the time they reached the peak, Raghu felt like he had been walking for ages. It must have been the twelfth day of the moon. The moon was shedding abundant light on the peak and moving slowly. A series of clouds would hide it, then the moon would emerge again... The light would dim, then brighten again... Raghu, lying on his back and groaning, looked at the sky as if for the first time.

Raghu had studied in Mysore. Then Delhi. While studying, even if the fads of the time and the influence of his studies entered him a little, it wasn't his nature to delve deeply into anything. What had deeply entered him was an interest in women. He had also returned to his village by his own decision. In fact, he didn't have the courage to believe he would get a lecturer's post. Since there was plenty of land, and his father had prepared it like a ready-made dish, Raghu didn't have much work. He just had to get the daily, seasonal work done. Since his father had bought a jeep, there was no problem with transportation. What was his daily work? Cheenkra would come in the morning to milk the cows. He would take the milk with him, go to Kootadka in the jeep, deliver the milk, have a tea at Ayyappa's hotel, pick up the newspaper, and return. Then, supervise the garden.

When his parents told him to get married, he did. She was a good girl. If he praised her a little or showed some affection, she would respond. If Raghu took the jeep once a week, she would come back the next day. Besides the money his father made, Raghu's skill was that he learned to save some money himself. From lending money at interest in Sullia or Puttur to doing small-scale areca nut trading.

He wasn't a big-time businessman in anything. He would naturally engage in small deceptions, threats, and intimidation. Because he was educated, he would also use some English.

Once, while talking to Dr. Shenoy opposite the Highway Bar, he had startled him by saying, 'Doctor, I am immoral.' He loved his wife very much. Yet, he had said at some point, 'It gets boring quickly if everything is fine, man.' This boredom meant nothing else. He would spend a night with an acquaintance in a distant lodge outside the village and return. He didn't tell all this himself. It was a rumor that floated in the air for some people in the village. If they asked him repeatedly, he would startle them by asking, 'Why do you eat hotel food?'

He would only talk some philosophy with Dr. Shenoy. 'I need a Teflon coating, Doctor, nothing should stick to me. I should experience it when I want to, and I shouldn't crave it again and again,' he had explained his philosophy. It was the same in the village society's politics. He would join whoever benefited him more. However, he wasn't someone who plunged into big politics. He would maintain enough contacts to go and negotiate when he needed to, and then retreat into his shell. No one clearly knew any of his private affairs. Once, when his wife heard a rumor and cried bitterly, he took her around to temples and other places for three days. She had returned to her usual self.

When someone asked if it was a temple puja, he winked and said, 'Temple? Isn't it a celebration to stay in a good lodge for three days?' His wife, who was used to daily home and cooking, returned satisfied because she didn't have the pressure of doing anything while staying in a lodge and eating in a hotel.

Cheenkra was his only assistant; he had even bought him a mobile phone. Neither of them had calculated his salary or if it was a weekly payment.

Raghu, lying there, gritting his teeth and groaning, felt his eyes heavy with sleep as he watched the clouds covering the night and the moon peeking through, but the pain wouldn't let him sleep.

'Anna, you sleep. Here, take this blanket,' Cheenkra said, untying the blanket from his back.

The smell of that blanket made Raghu feel dizzy.

'No, man...!' Raghu said, turning his head away where he lay.

Cheenkra bent near his face and said, 'Anna, this is my wife's blanket, Anna...'

Raghu shuddered where he lay.

Cheenkra again said, 'Anna, she studied up to ninth grade, she even reads newspapers. She rolls beedis, you know she doesn't come for garden work, right...?'

Raghu changed his side and groaned, as if uninterested in talking.

Cheenkra came back to that side and asked, 'Anna, you gave this blanket, didn't you? Did you forget?'

His voice seemed a little sarcastic and harsh to Raghu.

Cheenkra put his hand into his shorts pocket, took out a beedi, bit it, took the lighter from Raghu's jacket, and lit the beedi. The yellow light of the fire that spread on his face stung Raghu's eyes.

Cheenkra took two puffs of the beedi and said, 'Anna, you go to Ullal and stay in hotels, don't you...? Someone told me...'

Raghu just groaned, closed his eyes, and moaned where he lay. He was afraid to even move his feet.

Cheenkra again said, 'Anna, I know you came home... I know you were coming...'

Raghu stared at his face. A distorted sarcastic smile floated across Cheenkra's face and then returned to a serene expression.

'Anna, I won't say she's wrong. What to do, you're the master, aren't you? You come wearing perfume, that too... But she has a nature. She won't keep anything from me. No matter what soap I use to bathe, it's the same. Still, she loves me.'

'She shows me everything you gave her. If it's money, she puts it in my shirt pocket...'

Raghu trembled in fear and groaned. He sat there looking at Cheenkra, terrified of what would happen if he said anything more.

'She remembers you said that if you cover yourself with this blanket, you won't feel cold,' Cheenkra said.

Raghu, helpless like a mouse lying on its back, expecting a cat's blow, pulled himself up and sat.

'Cheenkra... that...' he tried to say something but fell silent, unable to speak.

Cheenkra took the water bottle from his bag, gave it to Raghu, and said, 'Anna, you can go anywhere, but don't come home. I'm not saying please, please... What happened, happened. Nothing will affect you. But if you come towards home again, the news will definitely come that you went hunting and fell into a tiger's mouth and died.'

Raghu tried to hold Cheenkra's hand, but he shook it off and said, 'Anna, I remembered my sister's face. Poor thing... Sleep until morning, I'll sleep here. As soon as it's morning, we'll manage to get home somehow... Don't worry.'

Raghu, wondering if he was dizzy, or if his eyes were heavy with sleep, couldn't understand anything and slid back down, lying on his back as if in a trance. Cheenkra lit another beedi and poked the fire in the log. Raghu watched him and started sobbing uncontrollably.l
