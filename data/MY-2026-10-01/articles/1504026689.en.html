Our Gail Omvedt, one with everyone

Bharathi Devi P.

It was the time when Covid had invaded all our lives. A time when direct human contact was lost, and people reached out to each other through phone or internet, striving to stay alive. The long-lived elder soul, Gail Omvedt, passed away on August 25, 2021, at the age of 80, in Kashegaon, a village in Sangli, Maharashtra. When one witnessed the deluge of tributes from the local people, activists of Maharashtra and India, and social thinkers, it became clear how Gail, who came from America and settled in our country, had found a place in everyone's hearts. Gail, who dedicated her life to the liberation of the oppressed, conducting studies and struggles, deeply analyzed the social structure of India. The way she engaged in struggles and studies as a companion to the people, and her perspective on the historical intertwining of caste, class, and gender, has provided a path for all those who study the social structure of India.

Important writings about Gail's life that emerged after her passing have been collected in the book 'Gail Omvedt and Her Legacy' by her companions Nagamani Rao, Prachi Patankar, Dileep Chavan, Pramod Majumdar, and Anosh Malekar. Another important Indian thinker and activist, Vandana Sonalkar, has written the foreword to this work.

Born in Minneapolis, America, in 1941, Gail was a student of sociologist Eleanor Zelliot, who conducted in-depth studies on the Dalit movement and Ambedkar in India, during her studies at Carleton College. It was also a time when the American student community was awakening to new kinds of awareness. When young people raised their voices against America's war-mongering policy and capitalism during the Vietnam War, Gail was a part of it. She stood for the rights of black people and women's liberation. In 1963, Gail first came to India for her dissertation research. Later, for her Ph.D., she chose the non-Brahmin movement in India and conducted special studies on the social work of Jyotiba Phule. During this period, meeting activists and thinkers of people's movements in Maharashtra, and understanding the pulse of the region, Gail decided to settle in India. She married Bharat Patankar, a social activist whom she met during her studies. They walked together in life, studies, and struggles.

With a Marxist understanding of the world, Gail, in the light of Phule and Ambedkar, tried to understand the conditions of Dalits, farmers, laborers, and women in India, by being with them. This was not a theorization done from a distance, treating them as objects of study. Therefore, Gail's struggles and studies have special significance.

Prior to this, when one examines studies on caste in India, they appear as monolithic perceptions from a Vedic perspective. Scholars like Louis Dumont, M.N. Srinivas, who conducted studies until then, viewed things from a top-down model. Concepts like purity-pollution, Sanskritization, etc., arose from this perspective. In contrast, Gail, with her deep understanding of society, identified the 'Caste mode of Production' in Indian society. Here, those in the upper strata have been continuously and unrestrictedly appropriating the fruits of the labor of the socially lower-strata workers. Thus, she analyzes how, as Ambedkar said, it is not just the division of labor, but the division of laborers that has continued.

When Gail came to India, two types of perceptions dominated our social and academic spheres. One was a nationalist perception influenced by a colonial viewpoint, and the other was a superficial understanding that applied Marxism to India without a deep understanding of society. Neither of these helped in understanding the stratified inequality and the complex intertwining of caste, gender, and class in our society. Therefore, Gail moved forward by combining the analytical method of Marxism with the thoughts of Phule and Ambedkar. Her perspective on women also emerged from this foundation.

In Indian history, Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj has been easily grouped with 'reformist movements'. However, Gail recognized that it was different from other reformist movements operating under the Vedic shadow. She identified it as a 'cultural revolution', truly a movement of the working class. Her Ph.D. thesis, 'Cultural Revolt in a Colonial Society: The Non-Brahman Movement in Western India, 1873 to 1930', documents how voices were raised against oppression and discrimination during colonial rule. This thread expanded in her later writings.

To understand stratified inequality in society, it is important to understand how caste, gender, and class are intertwined (Intersectionality). Gail was one of those who wrote and spoke with deep insight on this topic long before such studies appeared. When Western feminism revolved around the interests of white women, and Indian feminism did not move beyond the sphere of upper-caste, upper-class women, Gail spoke for peasant, Dalit, and tribal women. She analyzed how patriarchy pervades at different levels. Having witnessed violence and atrocities against women in homes and society up close, Gail wrote with anguish. Her 'We will Smash the Prison: Indian Women in Struggle', a narrative of anti-caste and women's movements in Maharashtra in the 70s, is not only an important work that closely observed and narrated feminism and labor movements, but also a work that shows a new direction for Indian feminism.

For Gail, study and struggle were inseparable. Whether it was sun or rain, whatever the region, Gail participated in movements alongside the people without any hesitation. Her close associates have documented in this work how she, her husband Bharat, and his mother Indu, along with their daughter Prachi when she was a small child, would all get involved. Gail, who mingled with people in this way, never brought herself to the forefront. It is a great lesson that she adhered to the principle that those who are affected should be at the forefront of the movement, and everyone else should join them. There are many instances where powerful individuals have appropriated movements, taken the lead, and pushed community leaders aside. By not doing so, and by joining as an ally, Gail became dear to everyone and also became a model for how those with privileges and status should negate them and become one with everyone. Her question about why there was no tribal leadership in the 'Narmada Bachao' movement arose from this very foundation.

The way the academic community responded to Gail's writings, who had found a place in the hearts of activists and people, needs to be noted. The book records that the academic sphere did not respond in the same way as everyone else did at the time of her passing. This silence still reveals the reality that the Indian academic sphere has not been able to come out of the Vedic perspective and confront society in its true form. Whatever the case, Gail Omvedt was the one who took the discourse on caste to an international level and created a platform for discussion. Today, no one studying Indian society can move forward without considering her writings. Gail gave a vocabulary to the oppressed communities to understand themselves. It becomes clear when reading Gail's writings that academic study can bring about change only when it reaches the people instead of remaining an unattainable dream. Today, many like Suraj Yengde, Yashica Dutt, Shailaja Paik are working to expand these ideas.

Gail, who renounced her American citizenship and became an Indian, witnessed the various ups and downs of India. She saw the dark days of the Emergency up close. She was concerned about the religious frenzy after the demolition of the Babri Masjid and its deep potential consequences. She believed that communalism does not stop merely at Hindu vs Muslim; it ultimately leads to the oppression of Dalits, women, and marginalized communities through the strengthening of dominant castes, and many incidents bear witness to this before our eyes.

Gail and Bharat, realizing that political freedom alone does not transform into social liberation, understood the importance of social movements and were involved with many such movements. Not only that, they themselves started an organization called 'Shramik Mukti Dal' as the voice of laborers, farmers, and women. Gail, who was continuously with the people until travel became difficult due to Covid, sowed the dream of 'Begumpura' among us and pointed the way towards it. Her work 'Seeking Begumpura: The Social Vision of Anticaste Intellectuals' introduces the path of anti-caste thinkers and spiritual practitioners in India. It introduces the thoughts of saints like Chokhamela, Kabir, Janabai, Ravidas, Tukaram, Phule, who lived among the people for five decades and dreamed of equality. Ravidas, a saint of the 15th century, born into a marginalized community, tried to break down the walls between people. He dreamed of a 'Begumpura' without sorrow and hardship. This is a place where everyone can live happily, without hierarchy, oppression, exploitation, or taxes. The welfare state we all wish to build under the support of the constitution is similar to this. Many among us have worked to guide us towards such a pain-free destination. Gail has held our hand and led us a few steps in that direction. She believes that merely economic movements are not enough to move forward on this path; instead, a new kind of social, intellectual, and cultural movement needs to be built.
