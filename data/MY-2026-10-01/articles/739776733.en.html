Like every Sunday, that week too, they had all gathered at Sarojamma's house. But the usual cheer was missing. Sarojamma's husband Venkatesh was sitting cross-legged on a sofa in the spacious hall, with both his feet up and his chin resting on his knees. On the divan cots opposite, Krishnegowda, Sarojamma's uncle's son, and his wife Kamala sat on one, while Surabhi, Sarojamma's aunt's granddaughter, and her husband Umesh sat on the other, looking glum. Sarojamma's two middle school children sat in one corner, while Kamala's two children and Surabhi's one daughter sat around them,
staring wide-eyed at their elders.

* * *

All of them, who had come to Bangalore city in search of a livelihood, were now quite well-off. Umesh and Surabhi, a couple, were software engineers in an MNC in Electronic City. Krishnegowda and Kamala, who initially sold vada and bonda at the Ring Road circle, recently started making biryani at home and selling it at the Ring Road circle in a Maruti van. People flock for their dum biryani in the evenings.

Sarojamma and Venkatesh, on the other hand, held good positions in the government insurance department, earning a handsome salary, along with perks! They had bought a plot early on and built their own house in the city. Being in a well-connected department, they had contacts with government employees and officials, giving them some influence. Moreover, Sarojamma, with her sharp tongue like TV serial heroines, held sway among the other family members.

Once a month,

on a Sunday,

they would all go to a distant

homestay or resort

in their own Malnad region

and have a good time. On other Sundays, they would gather at Sarojamma's house for a party. There would be good mutton, chicken, or fish dishes, along with brandy for the men and wine or beer for the women. And what about the children?! They would naturally have
ice cream with Mirinda or
7Up.

Now, with the monster called Covid invading the city and raging, their peaceful lives have been disrupted. Initially, the government and people dismissed this coronavirus as nothing serious, but gradually, as patients coughed and sneezed, the virus spread through the air, infecting everyone it encountered, and people panicked. When patients started having difficulty breathing and were hospitalized, governments were at a loss, unable to provide pure oxygen for them to breathe. If people were hospitalized in such numbers due to Covid, how much oxygen could governments possibly fill in cylinders and bring in trucks for breathing!

To prevent the spread of the disease, the only way was to keep people from interacting with each other. Offices closed, commercial centers closed, temples and mosques shut down. Finally, a lockdown was declared for the entire society. As a result, daily necessities became unavailable. Liquor stores also closed their doors.
Whether it was food or alcohol, when even the life-giving air became scarce, the people of the metropolis were distraught. People who had left their villages and built lives in the city, with no other option, left the city and began returning to their villages in search of oxygen for breathing.

It was for this purpose that the meeting was held at Sarojamma's house. The men, who were accustomed to having a peg or two of alcohol before dinner every night, sat silently, and Sarojamma had to take action.

She took out her mobile and called
her brother in the village. On the other end, her brother's wife answered the phone,
turned on the mic, and said, 'It's your sister's call, talk to her,' and handed the phone to her husband.

Sarojamma, after explaining all the troubles of Covid, began to talk about their intention to come to the village, when her sister-in-law on the other end said, 'Don't let that loud-mouthed sister of yours come here with her baggage. Will she bring the disease and spread it everywhere? Tell her we want to live peacefully here for a few days.' It was clear that there was no point in talking to them anymore. Helpless, she hung up the phone and looked at Surabhi and her husband, who were sitting on the opposite sofa.

Surabhi understood the implication of Sarojamma's gaze. Just last week, Sarojamma had called Surabhi and suggested that she go to her grandmother Shanthamma's house on the edge of the forest and stay there for some time.

* * *

That isolated house, located in the middle of the open field near the forest, a mile away from the village, though small, was perfect for their purpose. Behind the house was a paddy field, and beyond that, the forest. Adjacent to it were the Western Ghats mountain ranges, and the cool breeze blowing from there, carrying life-giving air, brought joy to everyone.

Shanthamma's husband was the eldest in the family. He had two younger brothers. As the proverb goes, 'Brothers at birth, rivals as they grow,' disputes began after they all got married. The property was divided, and the paddy field and plantation land on the forest side fell to Shanthamma's husband. Three families lived in the one house. One brother on the front veranda, another brother in the middle part, having broken a wall from the outside and put a door, and the rice pounding area for Shanthamma's husband.

Shanthamma's husband was naturally gentle. So, harassment from the other brothers' families increased. Shanthamma would wake up early, send her only daughter to school, and go to the farm on the forest side with her husband.
They had planted cardamom in their share of the abandoned forest. When there was no work in the paddy field, they would always be in the cardamom forest. A stream flowed through the middle of the forest. During their lunch break, they would catch crabs and fish there, cook them, and eat. During the monsoon season, they would set up traps in the stream and catch plenty of small fish. The remaining fish would be dried and stored. They had dug an agricultural pond at the edge of the paddy field and made arrangements for two crops.

Their relatives, who came to visit, would grumble seeing their prosperity. Due to their envious words, Shanthamma's family could not even sleep peacefully in that rice pounding area at night.

Shanthamma pestered her husband. Both of them walked five miles to the Panchayat office every market day. A Janatha house was sanctioned. A small house was built far from the village, near the paddy field, on the edge of the open field which was government wasteland. They built a spacious shed next to the house for cattle and firewood, collecting forest timber, bamboo, and mountain stones.

In that vast open field at the edge of the paddy field, there was no one to question them. All day long, they would let their cattle graze on the mountain slopes and work tirelessly in the fields. In the evening, they would come home, finish the work in the cowshed, bathe in hot water, and sit in the open area in front of the house, exposing themselves to the pleasant breeze blowing from the mountain ranges, caressing the top of the forest. Shanthamma would ferment fruits and berries with jaggery and distill enough arrack to last two or three months.

With fish curry, they would sit for hours, sipping arrack from small finger-sized glasses, sharing their joys and sorrows. On moonlit nights, it was as if heaven descended to earth. Thus, they lived a peaceful life, 'without the king's control, and without the torment of ghosts.'

Perhaps even fate could not tolerate their beautiful life. One day, during the monsoon, her husband, who had gone to the forest to set up a trap in the stream, did not return. Shanthamma, who had waited for her husband all night in the pouring rain and was disappointed, went to look for him at dawn. It was unclear whether he had fallen victim to a relative's jealousy, or if he had slipped from the steps while crossing the stream and fallen into the current. As a sign that he had been swept away by the overflowing stream, his rubber sandals were caught on a small tree branch in the hollow.

Shanthamma did not lose heart. She managed the farm work with the help of others. She sold all the cattle in the cowshed, keeping one buffalo calf for herself. After working hard all day, she would drink arrack from her small glass, eat, secure the door, and fall asleep. Two dogs, Kariya and Bulli, were her companions in her lonely life.

As a sign that difficult days had begun in her life, she started having stomach pains occasionally. But there was no one to whom she could confide her troubles. Her only daughter had been married off to a poor family in a village on the outskirts of a distant city when they were still in the village.

Since her daughter's husband worked as a mason in the city, the responsibility of the small piece of land in the village, two Sindhi cows, and two children fell to Shanthamma's daughter. Both children were bright. The elder daughter, Surabhi, had completed engineering and started working. She was also married to her colleague Umesh. Her son was still in college. Amidst all these pressures, her daughter could not come to the village to inquire about her mother's well-being. She had once asked her mother to come to her village and given her a small keypad mobile, saying, 'Keep this, you can talk about your joys and sorrows.'

As days and months passed, the occasional stomach pain that Shanthamma experienced became constant. The doctors at the hobli hospital diagnosed it as uterine cancer.

Panicked, Shanthamma called her daughter. The daughter, upon hearing the news, was distraught. She fretted, wondering what to do, as she couldn't leave her family responsibilities and go to her parents' house to help her mother. She thought that if her daughter Surabhi and son-in-law in Bangalore wished, they could get her treated by good doctors. However, they had kept their distance from her because she had been an obstacle when they decided to marry for love.

She told her mother to call Umesh. Umesh, after listening to Shanthamma, said, 'It's not such a big deal. Get admitted to the district hospital there and have the operation. We are both busy with work, so we can't come and go. If you have financial trouble, let us know, we'll send money.'

She did not like Umesh's indifferent tone. She remained silent. Shanthamma continued to suffer for a year or two. An Asha worker, who knew of her plight, took her to the taluk hospital. After checking everything, the doctors advised, 'The cancer is worsening. You should take her to a big hospital in the city before it's too late.'

It was known that Shanthamma's brother-in-law's daughter Sarojamma and son-in-law worked in the Bangalore government insurance department and were acquainted with the doctors at the cancer hospital. When the lady surgeon there was buying a site, Sarojamma had helped her get a loan on her insurance policy, and for that reason, the surgeon was grateful to Sarojamma. All this was known to the Asha worker. The whole village knew that Sarojamma had boasted about it when she came to the village for the garden festival last time.
The Asha worker sat her down on a stone bench

in front of the hospital

and said, 'Your Sarojamma might be able to help. Call her and talk to her right now.'

Shanthamma, knowing Sarojamma's nature, was reluctant to call. She just sat with her head bowed.

'Will it do to just sit like that? Give me her number. I'll talk to her myself. If she doesn't help in this difficulty, then what's the point?' she said, snatching Shanthamma's phone, taking out the number, and calling. Before she could explain fully, Sarojamma said, 'I'm busy with work right now. I'll call her back in a while.'

Later, Sarojamma called Shanthamma and said, 'What, auntie? You make everyone call me. Don't you know I'm busy with office work here? The cancer hospital surgeons are not unknown to me. You find someone to accompany you, come to Bangalore, and get admitted. I'll talk to them on the phone. If needed, I'll come for a short while on the day of the operation.'

Shanthamma did not seem to like Sarojamma's words. 'Let it be, let's see,' she said, and walked towards the village with the Asha worker.

* * *

Sarojamma said to Surabhi, who was hesitant to call her grandmother, 'Hmm, why are you sitting like that? Will it do to just sit? How will our problems be solved if we don't tell them?'

'What problem should I tell? We haven't been there for their problems, have we? You call,' Surabhi said, looking at her husband.

'What will you talk about? Did we say we wouldn't be there for their problems? Didn't I tell you in the beginning? Go to the district hospital and get the operation done. Whatever the cost, I'll pay for it. If she herself is negligent, what can we do? If it had been done then, the uterus would have been cut out and thrown away. Everything would have been fine,' Umesh said, turning to Surabhi with red eyes.

'Umesh is right, Surabhi. When they said the disease had worsened, didn't I tell you to come to the cancer hospital here in Bangalore and get admitted? I'll call the surgeons and tell them. What more than that... Now, if you keep dragging it, it won't work... Give me the phone,' Sarojamma said, snatching the phone from Surabhi's hand and dialing.

Shanthamma, who had listened patiently to everything, said, 'That's all fine. If so many of you, with children and grandchildren, come, how will you stay in this palm-sized house?' Just as she was saying this, Sarojamma snatched the phone from Surabhi's hand and said, 'Don't worry about that, auntie. If the children get a place to sleep with you in the house, that's enough. Anyway, the spacious cowshed is empty, isn't it? We'll all adjust there.' As she said this, Shanthamma said, 'Oh, I don't know what it is, I don't understand.' and fell silent.

'Oh, auntie, don't worry. When we come, we'll bring enough rations for a month, and a couple of sacks of jaggery in the car. We'll do all the cooking ourselves. Our men are used to drinking every day. You just make a little arrack for them,' Sarojamma said, comforting her and silencing Shanthamma.

* * *

The next evening, Sarojamma's eleven-member squad arrived in two cars and got out in front of the house. The men unloaded the sacks of rice, provisions, and jaggery from the car trunks and carried them inside. Shanthamma stood leaning against a wall, watching all this with curiosity.

Sarojamma left Surabhi and the children with their grandmother and took the rest to the cowshed. They swept and cleaned the entire area, leaving the corner where the buffalo calf was tied, and spread mats.

Meanwhile, Shanthamma, with Surabhi's help, had prepared dried fish curry and rice for the rare guests. The men who had followed Sarojamma stood there, looking eagerly for the arrack Shanthamma might offer.

Sarojamma sent them all out to the cowshed and said, 'Auntie, they are all waiting to see if they can get something to drink. They haven't had anything to drink for two days. If you have any, give it to them.'

Shanthamma brought a can from the attic and said, 'Look, there's this much. I made it for the house. The doctor told me not to drink because the pain has increased. I haven't touched it for a month.' Seeing that, Surabhi said, 'Grandma, don't show them all this at once. They've been sitting here frustrated for two days without anything to drink. They'll finish it today or tomorrow. Just give them one bottle, that's enough. And it will take at least a week for you to melt this jaggery, let it ferment, and distill the arrack. Until then, let them adjust with what's available now.'

Then Shanthamma brought a large old cauldron from the attic, poured a sack of jaggery into it, added water, and set it to melt. She called Sarojamma and asked her to send someone in the car tomorrow to bring some grapes and rock candy. 'I'll bring pineapple and cashews from the garden. If we add everything to this and let it ferment, we can make good arrack,' she said.

Every day, they had vegetable curry and rice, and finished a bottle of arrack at night, sleeping in a row in the cowshed. The pleasant breeze blowing from the mountain ranges brought joy to their minds. They would fall into a peaceful sleep until dawn.

Thus, a week passed. That night, Shanthamma said, 'The fermentation is ready. We can distill the arrack tomorrow night.'

Everyone, who had been wetting their throats with one bottle every day, was happy.

'Then tomorrow morning, let's all go with Shanthamma to the paddy field and catch crabs and fish. We can have a good feast,' said Sarojamma.

The next evening, on the kitchen stove, crab curry was boiling in one pot and fish curry in another. On the stove in the bathing room, the jaggery, fruit, and berry mixture was boiling, evaporating, and transforming into arrack due to the coolness of the cold water pot above, collecting in another pot.

After cooking one batch of the fermented mixture, Shanthamma set the pot filled with arrack aside to cool, and began preparing the next batch for distillation. The pungent smell of the hot distilled arrack spread throughout the environment, and its aroma pleasantly reached the noses of everyone sitting in the cowshed, doubling their enthusiasm. The men and women, with the newly prepared arrack pot and the crab and fish curry pots in front of them, began talking animatedly. Even the women, who were accustomed to drinking beer, partook in the arrack and participated in the joyful conversation.

It was already past eleven. All the children had eaten and gone to sleep in the house. The clamor of conversations from those sitting in the cowshed reached Shanthamma's ears, who was sitting in the bathing room, distilling arrack.

It was clear from their conversations that all of them, who had experienced difficulty breathing in the city and had come out of it distraught, were now exhilarated and floating in joy with the pure inhalation and exhalation of life-giving air.

'Our Shanthamma's skill is truly skill! Look, what she made, they call it arrack! It's like good Scotch...' Sarojamma, who had never spoken well of her aunt, was now praising her to the skies.

'Why call it arrack? Wouldn't 'Shanthamma's Malnad Scotch' be better?' others chimed in.

Their cheers and exaggerated words did not please Shanthamma. Her stomach pain began to surge. She felt that none of their superficial words could save her from this demon called cancer, which had reached its final stage.

'If I become bedridden, will any of them come to see me? If I become a corpse in a corner, who will even sniff me?' she thought, poking the embers in the stove.

As if she had made a decision, she filled an empty bottle with arrack, took her small glass, opened the back door, and began walking on the embankment of the agricultural pond. She sat down in one place and poured arrack into her glass. The cool breeze blowing from the edge of the forest created ripples in the pond. She felt that no life-giving air could save her.
She raised the glass to her lips...

***

They had gone to bed late at night and were not awake even when the sun shone on them. The bellowing of the buffalo and calf
woke them up.

'Shanthamma, who used to milk the buffalo, make coffee, and wake us all up every day, why hasn't she come yet?' they thought, rushing towards the house and pushing the door open. The children were in a deep sleep. The stove in the kitchen was not lit. The arrack distilling equipment was still on the stove in the bathroom. The collected arrack was in a pot in the stove pit.

The back door was open. They rushed out from there. On the bank of the pond, a partially empty arrack bottle and a small glass sat abandoned. Kariya and Bulli whimpered, running back and forth on the embankment. Everyone's hearts began to pound. They looked around. On the other side of the pond, the red saree Shanthamma had worn the previous day was floating.

They began to realize that a terrible tragedy might have occurred.

They stood looking at each other's faces,

like lost souls.
