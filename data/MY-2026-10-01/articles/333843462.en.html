A slender body, a constant smile on his face, soft-spoken. Even though he had good camaraderie with the star cast at one time, he was a gentleman who never gossiped about anyone. His words were measured and thoughtful. A simplicity that leaves the question, 'What great thing have I done?' Even at the age of eighty, he easily takes down and shows his father's hundred-year-old camera, kept on top of the showcase, with great care.

The house of 'Pragati' Ashwathanarayana in Basaveshwara Nagar, Bengaluru, was quiet. The atmosphere was so silent that you could hear a needle drop. As Ashwathanarayana opened his laptop and then each folder within it, old movie memories seemed to come alive.

Rajkumar and Bharathi immersed in water, cheek to cheek (film: Hrudaya Sangama), the serious dialogue moment from the film 'Paduvaralli Pandavaru', the joyous faces of brothers Rajkumar and Varadaraj, the old memory of Bharathi and Vishnuvardhan holding their little daughter Keerthi, Puttanna Kanagal standing like a disciplined soldier on the shooting set, the occasion when all the stalwarts of the film industry gathered at the 'Gandhada Gudi' event... Thousands of such speaking pictures are hidden in Ashwathanarayana's small laptop. All of these are 'speaking pictures' taken by him, carefully preserved memories.

Beyond the cricket craze...
to the world of cinema

Ashwathanarayana was born in Belakawadi, Mandya district. His father, Krishnamurthy, was a farmer. In the 1920s, he had imported a 'Kodak' camera from America. With film in it, it was only possible to take eight photos at a time. He took group photos of people around him, in interesting costumes. Ashwathanarayana is the son of such a father. He lost his parents at a young age. He moved to his uncle's house in Bangalore. From there, his connection with the city began. He studied there until SSLC. Ashwathanarayana, who was also crazy about cricket at the same time as G.R. Viswanath played club cricket, also played the game. Even during this conversation, his eyes were glued to the TV showing the live broadcast of the second Test match between India and Sri Lanka.

His uncle's son was Nagesh Babu. Nagesh had worked as an assistant director with R. Nagendraraya in Madras for films like 'Premada Putri'. It was a time when the film industry needed still photographs. So, Nagesh Babu started 'Three Stars Studio' there. After SSLC, Ashwathanarayana, who did not continue his studies, went to his elder brother. He traveled from Bangalore to Madras in 1965 and learned the first lessons at the studio.

Due to his elder brother's active involvement in the South Indian Film Chamber, he developed contacts with people in the film industry. There was a 'Raw Film Committee' specifically for distributing film for shooting in turns. Nagesh Babu, who was also its superintendent, had a name in the film industry.

Ashwathanarayana first learned the work of washing film in the darkroom. A formula for mixing chemicals in the right proportion was used to process film in studios. It was a secret. Five chemicals had to be mixed one after another in specific quantities to prepare the mixture. The order in which the chemicals were to be mixed was crucial. Even a slight variation in this sequence would render the mixture useless. Ashwathanarayana learned all these things in his basic training and gradually became skilled enough to take photos independently. Usually, no one was allowed to touch a camera for five or six months. Since it was his brother's studio, he got some advantage.

After honing his skills by directing two Malayalam films, Puttanna Kanagal ventured into directing Kannada films. That film was 'Belli Moda' (1967). Ashwathanarayana was appointed as the still photographer for it. He went to the set filled with fear.

Balakrishna, Dwarakish and Jaya were acting in a comedy scene. He first captured its still image with his camera. That photo is preserved in the Pune archives. It was an unforgettable recognition for the first photo he took.

Before Ashwathanarayana, apart from Vrishabhendrayya and M.R.K. Murthy, there were no Kannadigas who took this profession seriously.

Whenever the shooting of the film 'Belli Moda' took place (producers did not always provide money for continuous shooting then), he would take photos and come back. Later, Puttanna Kanagal relied on this still photographer for most of his films.

This is the prelude to how the son of a father who brought a camera from abroad and captured the emotions of his village people, Ashwathanarayana, went a step further and engaged in the work of taking film stills. For this reason, he says, 'Perhaps photography was in my blood.'

He was only nineteen years old when he took the still photographs for his first film in the presence of Puttanna Kanagal.

One movie after another

Compared to the pace at which Kannada films were being produced, the Malayalam film industry was far ahead at that time. Prem Nazir was a hero who acted in 700 films. There is an example of thirty-nine of his films being released in a single year. The release of sixteen films starring Rajkumar in 1968 can also be seen as a similar example. However, no one could match the speed at which Prem Nazir was shooting. He would be involved in the shooting of three films in three shifts simultaneously. He would quickly change his costumes and get ready according to the roles. Ashwathanarayana got opportunities to do still photography for such Prem Nazir's films.

As he worked with different film industries, his world of experience also expanded.

Ashwath Narayana has closely observed the working style of producers who made Kannada films. It was the nature of many producers to shoot when they got money, and then stop. They would continue shooting when they got money again.

On the film set, they would first shoot video using film. The cinematographer would do that work. After they shot a scene, they would place the camera in the same spot and take still photographs with the lighting that had already been set up. Even when taking still photographs after acting in a specific scene, the actors and actresses would act again. The task was to capture their emotions neatly.

Unlike now, there were no smart lights then. Lighting was done with the limited facilities available. It was a challenging task to take effective still photographs with lighting suitable for videography. Over time, Ashwathanarayana mastered it.

Origin of 'Pragati Studio'

In the 1970s, the Kannada film industry shifted from Madras to Bangalore. During that period, there was a shortage of technicians to work in Kannada films. It was necessary to bring technicians from Madras for the films being produced here. This required more time and money.

Realizing this, Ashwathanarayana and his brother Nagesh Babu established 'Pragati Studio' in Gandhinagar, Bangalore, in 1972, dedicated to Kannada films. This studio was established on the road where the 'Movieland' cinema hall is located. The brothers sold their studio in Madras and moved here.

In addition to working as still photographers for films, Ashwathanarayana and his brother also trained newcomers who joined the studio. Over time, this benefited the Kannada film industry. This was because bringing still photographers from Madras was expensive. Moreover, if still photographs were not available on time, films would not get the necessary publicity. 'Pragati Studio' filled that gap.

The studio expanded its business by not only taking film stills but also by taking event photos and passport photos.

Back then, still photographs were like fuel for film promotion. Most state-level newspapers and magazines published film stills. Looking at them, the audience would become curious about what kind of film it might be. Details of the shooting, concise information about the chosen story, and experiences of the actors and actresses were all published in abundance. Still photographs appeared to enhance their value.

Filmmakers also looked at still photographs to compare whether the costumes and makeup of the characters were perfect. For example: a still photograph clearly shows how wide a bindi an actress has on her forehead in a particular scene or what style of saree she is wearing. In situations where continuous shooting was not possible, when they came back for shooting after a month or two, the still photographs helped maintain the costumes and makeup of the characters. Especially in mythological films, from what kind of jewelry they wore to how their hairstyle was, makeup artists would prepare the actors for shooting again by looking at the photos.

Now the frenzy of film promotion has shifted towards social media. Back then, such facilities were non-existent, let alone electronic media. Thus, still photographs were the lifeblood of promotion. Such still photographs have not only remained as promotional tools but also as historical documents. Each photograph taken by Ashwathanarayana holds a story within it.

Memories of Premier Studio

Ashwathanarayana recalls how Premier Studio, owned by M.N. Basavarajaiah, nurtured the entire film industry at one time. When shooting was going on there, the entire team worked diligently. Some producers who said they would come for lunch would never show up.

Everyone who was busy working would start feeling hungry. Those in charge of production would go to Basavarajaiah and ask if he could arrange for food if he gave them some money. He would then take out ₹400 from his pocket and give it to them. That amount was enough to feed the entire film crew at that time.

After the shooting, all the reels would remain with him. Many producers misused his kindness by not paying him. However, the way he lovingly treated everyone who selflessly engaged in filmmaking was exemplary. In this era where business is paramount, people like Basavarajaiah are worthy of remembrance. The reason Ashwathanarayana says that the government should have saved Premier Studio is his love for cinema.

Puttanna, Siddalingaiah's favorites

Ashwathanarayana has worked as a still photographer in 11 films directed by Puttanna Kanagal, 15 by Siddalingaiah, and 10 by K.S.L. Swamy (Ravi). His experience expanded with prominent directors including H.L.N. Simha, N. Lakshminarayana, Hunsur Krishnamurthy, Bhargava, T.S. Nagabharana, Dwarakish, and K.V. Jayaram. H.L.N. Simha was the first Kannadiga to become an independent film director. His 'Samsara Nauka' holds the distinction of being the first social film.

Ashwathanarayana saw a disciplined soldier in Puttanna Kanagal. On set, everyone hesitated to talk back to this director. He would repeatedly change the dialogues that had already been written on set. He would hold a page in his hand, memorize it once, and then instruct the artists who had performed to change the same dialogue in a different way. That was his method to elevate the scene on the spot. Some actors and actresses would get exhausted by this entire process. However, the quality of the scene would improve because of it.

Usually, no one would go to Puttanna to give advice. This was because there were many things to learn from him. Ashwathanarayana observed that he himself had knowledge of many aspects of film technology. He witnessed everything from the use of an alap as background music in the film 'Gejje Pooje' to the first adoption of slow motion in cinema.

Puttanna would not let anyone be too familiar. Actor Ambareesh was the only exception to this. Ambareesh was often late for shoots. By then, Puttanna would be furious. Ambareesh, who arrived late, would go to his makeup artist and loudly say that the director would scold him if he delayed putting on makeup quickly, Ashwathanarayana recalls.

After getting his makeup done, Ambareesh would come to Puttanna, make him adjust his collar, and butter him up. Puttanna, however, never stopped teaching him to be on time. Only Ambareesh would behave like this in front of this strict director. Everyone else worked as if they were afraid of him.

Siddalingaiah was a people's person. He also had commitment when it came to shooting. Yet, he would occasionally speak in a lighthearted tone. Rarely, he would listen to the advice given to him, and if he found it right, he would adopt it. Ashwathanarayana has many such memories in his mind.

When K. Balachander decided to shoot the Kannada film 'Benkiyalli Aralida Hoovu', he had decided to call a still photographer from Madras. The local people advised him that it was not necessary and that Ashwathanarayana was suitable for the job. Finally, Balachander relied on Ashwathanarayana. Not only that, but after seeing his studio and the atmosphere there, he continued to work with him in the future.

'Pragati Studio' a think tank

Most of the prominent figures in the film industry visited Pragati Studio. Their purpose was not just to get their photos taken. Stalwart directors like N. Lakshminarayana held discussions there with Girish Kasaravalli, who had imbibed artistic sensibilities regarding film subjects.

Balakrishna had his photo taken at that studio two weeks before his last breath. Although his photos taken at various ages are in the collection, his last photo in life was also captured there.

For Narasimharaju, M.R. Vittal, Varadaraj, G.V. Iyer, Bhargava, Hunsur Krishnamurthy, Veeraswamy, Abbainaidu, and others, his studio served as a meeting place. Not only that, but this is also the place where Anant Nag was given his name.

If anyone needed to deliver a letter or an item, they would leave it at the studio. The concerned people would come and collect them. In this sense, the studio was also a post office.

Memories with Raj

Rajkumar's film production company was close to Pragati Studio. So, they would invite Ashwathanarayana to take photos of many of their family events. Rajkumar, seeing his professionalism, would always greet him with folded hands and speak respectfully.

When Rajkumar turned sixty, journalist V.N. Subbarao went to interview him for a film magazine. Ashwathanarayana also went along to take photos.

Subbarao asked Rajkumar, who always wore a white shirt and white dhoti, to wear a colored shirt. His son-in-law Govindaraj was wearing a colored shirt. Rajkumar borrowed it, put it on in a few moments, and posed for photos. That colored photo was a rare among rarities of Raj's photos taken at that time.

Ashwathanarayana worked as a still photographer with Raj in only a handful of films. After the shooting, Ashwathanarayana would quickly capture Raj demonstrating the same scenes again. Seeing his speed, Raj would praise him, saying, 'Is this enough? You finish your work very easily.'

When Raj was awarded the Dadasaheb Phalke Award, it was Ashwathanarayana who went to his house, persuaded him to wear the medal around his neck, and took his photo. Ashwathanarayana had an unforgettable camaraderie with Raj's brother Varadaraj. Countless people had their pictures taken in various poses at Pragati Studio. The fact that Ashwathanarayana preserved all those films to provide them whenever needed seems to be a testament to his dedication to his work.

Ashwathanarayana worked with Puttanna until the film 'Rinamuktalu'. His last work was for the film 'Prema Prema' in 2005. After digital photography became prevalent, these professionals became silent. The reason he gave up was that the technology surpassed the old emotional world. Gradually, the studio closed. However, he carefully preserved the photo memories there in digital form. 'Wherever I see a photo of Hunsur Krishnamurthy, I can confidently say that I took it. Because it is the only available photo of him,' says Ashwathanarayana.

A grand collection of
film stills

After the studio closed, Ashwathanarayana embarked on a rare project. It was a research project to collect at least one still from every film released from 1934, when 'Sati Sulochana' was released, until the year 2000. He states that 2047 films were released during that period, and he has collected all but about 40 film stills. He has prepared a robust 'archive' by noting each photo with the name of the film and the year of its release.

He has 3 lakh negatives. Some producers, unable to preserve them themselves, gave them to him, and he has protected all of them. He has a collection of photos of 50 films starring Rajkumar. He also has countless pictures of Aarathi, Manjula, Ambareesh, Vishnuvardhan, Bharathi, Balakrishna, Narasimharaju, and others.

He has taken some photos of Puttanna Kanagal when he was engrossed in shooting. All of them reflect the nuances of filmmaking. Puttanna believed that still photographs should tell the story of the film. Ashwathanarayana has diligently documented Puttanna's film work through photographs. He also organized an exhibition titled 'Behind the Screen of Puttanna'. He had also previously organized an exhibition of rare old photos taken by his father.

Film Adventure

In 1970, Ashwathanarayana's brother Nagesh Babu ventured into producing and directing a film. He chose 'Anireekshita', a film based on Krishnamurthy Puranik's literary work. It contained material similar to the theme of 'Bayaludaari'. Srinath was its hero. It was his second film. Kalpana was chosen as the heroine.

Vijay Bhaskar composed the music for the film's songs. Vijay Bhaskar had S.P. Balasubrahmanyam, who was still very young at the time, sing the songs. At that time, Balasubrahmanyam was not very well known to Kannadigas. Ashwathanarayana says that it might have been Balu's second or third song. Poet K.S. Narasimhaswamy was commissioned to write the song for that film. He came to where Vijay Bhaskar was and wrote the song according to the meter.

Despite witnessing many experiments, this film brought financial losses to Nagesh Babu. It took him a long time to recover from it. He never dared to venture into another film production. However, he continued his association with the film industry even after coming to Bangalore. He further strengthened that bond.
