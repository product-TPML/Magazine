Are you in the fish... or is the fish in you?

Prasad Shenoy R K

Prasad Shenoy R. K., Art: Gujjar

“My life is fish, my life’s light is fish,” a friend of mine sings in the tune of the old Kannada movie song ‘Nanna Jeeva Neenu’ (My life is you) whenever he sees a fish hotel. If you go to that hotel and order a fish meal, in a short while, hot fish fry with red masala on a banana leaf, showered with coriander flowers, with a small slice of lemon on the side, wafting a fragrant aroma, and along with it, rice and fish curry ready and waiting, the song ‘Meene Meene Nanagella Meene... Aase Neene Nanagella Meene’ (Fish, fish, all for me... You are my desire, all for me) spontaneously comes out of his mouth. He is such an innocent fish lover who replaces ‘you’ with ‘fish’ in all popular Kannada love songs.

As you know, we are from a coastal town, and whenever we enter the coast here, we are delighted by the smell of fish wafting in the air. Even if those who don't eat fish hold their noses, we, by deeply inhaling the fish aroma, desire it even more. When traveling somewhere, the strong aroma of fish curry that emanates for just a moment from some hotel on the bus, or the captivating aroma of tawa fry, rava fry, fish masala fry that comes daily from a neighboring house or hotel, we enjoy it like jasmine. We listen attentively to anyone who talks about fish and experience a pleasant thrill within ourselves. Such is our affection for fish.

When we enter the roads on the outskirts of our village during the day, we are sure to find people selling fish on bikes or pickup vehicles, honking 'pom... pom'. Sometimes, loaded with various kinds of fish, the pickup vehicle moves like an ant, honking in front of every house in the village. When I see this vehicle moving, calling out to everyone, 'Fish, fish, bangde, bhutayi, etti, kaane, anjal, koddayi, manji, kalluru, ade, fresh available', I stop and watch from a distance. Waiting for the interesting things that happen there, grandfathers rush to buy fish because their wives like bangde fish. A woman, laughing with the fish seller, starts bargaining for shrimp, saying her son won't eat without shrimp. An old woman from the courtyard shouts, 'Today is Tuesday, we don't eat fish today, we'll buy tomorrow.' A female voice floats from a window of a house, 'Hey, how much for bhutayi?' A housewife, holding a vessel, comes happily, saying, 'Oh, you came at the right time, relatives from Bombay have come home,' and fills it with bhutayi and kaane fish, mentally grinding fish masala, thinking, 'Bhutayi for fry, kaane for curry,' and rushes into the kitchen. While buyers gather in front of the vehicle on one side, there's a big gang of people who, even if they don't buy, stand on the road, wasting time, looking at what kind of fish has come, asking, 'Is that fresh, is this fresh?', picking up fish, saying, 'It looks like yesterday's fish, isn't it fresh?', and without buying fish or stopping talking, they ask, 'What's new, you have good business, how much do you get fish for, do you have your own boat?' and so on, talking excessively with the fish seller. On holidays, children and youngsters from every house surround the fish cart, staring with open mouths at the pink, long-whiskered, thick-tailed, silvery shining fish, their shapes, lengths, and widths. When the fish cart honks and calls, no matter what state they are in, they are not satisfied until they run first and see the fish. What's even more amusing is that even if the fish cart is a mile away, cats that were comfortably sitting in the stove or attic of the house, sensing the cart's arrival by smell, shake themselves off and come out, lining up by the roadside like people waiting to receive prasad at a temple, and sit watching the road. As soon as the fish cart arrives, the cats' agitation increases. These cats befriend the fish sellers, make various musical sounds of 'meow meow', rub against their legs and knees, flatter them, and get some free fish. Since their stomachs are not filled by one source of income, they politely act in front of acquaintances who come to buy fish and shamelessly snatch fish from them too. Some cats, with a strong belief that they will definitely be given fish again when the owners go home and clean it, follow them like ghosts, wagging their tails. When there is fish at their own house, why lose dignity by going to someone else's house? Some cats immediately rush to their own backyard and start pestering the housewife, saying, 'Give it quickly, dear.' Thus, in this daytime wonderland that unfolds due to fish in a small village, how many characters, how many stories, and how vibrant is the abundant entertainment found here. For this reason, fish carts wafting the aroma of fish on the roads of small towns are an endless wonder for me, and for fish lovers like me, seeing this is as thrilling as coming to a forest village by the sea.

For fish lovers on the coast, fish has become so natural that it's like a sea vegetable. So, the various curries of this sea vegetable, and the fries cooked in oil, are our desire, the life of our affection. In our Mangalore, Udupi, Kundapura region, fish fries cooked in coconut oil with masala, and the aroma and taste of pulimunchi, a sour and spicy curry, are captivating. If we recall the taste of bangde ubbi and fish curry from Kumta, Honnavar regions of North Karnataka, our mouths water. Whenever I meet friends from North Karnataka, I savor their fish stories from their own mouths.
I watch with wonder the craving of friends who cannot eat a meal without fish every day. Even if there is an atmosphere of dissatisfaction at home, the husband, to appease his family, occasionally brings large fish from the fish market and has his wife or mother make fish fry, temporarily calming the rebellious situation at home with fish.

In some homes, fish is still a rare and expensive delicacy. So, if fish is brought home once in a while, it's a festive occasion for everyone in the family. Everyone sits together and joyfully eats each morsel of the fish meal with enthusiasm, as if it were something new. The mother gently separates the fish from the bones for the small children and lovingly gives them only the soft part. Watching the elders carefully, the children also effortlessly learn various techniques of eating fish. They slowly understand the truth that each fish has a different kind of bone, a different aroma, and a different taste, and they too become experienced fish lovers. Eating fish is not easy; one must know how to eat it carefully so that bones do not enter the body. That's why people in our region jokingly say that eating fish is an art.

Just as the food culture of each region is different, in each coastal town, fish curry and sambar are prepared in different ways. They give it beautiful names related to the local language and geographical characteristics. Fish biryani found in Kerala is not found anywhere else on the coast of Karnataka. It has a pure, natural taste of the land of gods. The fish found on the coasts of Tamil Nadu and Puducherry is very fresh. But there, even fresh fish is fried like dried fish, with chili powder sprinkled on top. Even if this masala seems bland compared to the fish masala made in rural areas on a grinding stone, the freshness of the fish melts in the mouth. In the Konkan region of Maharashtra, there is a unique tradition of offering fish as naivedya to Ganapati during Ganesh Chaturthi. On that day, the festive meal includes fish fry, fish gasi, and a grand feast of various fish dishes. They also prepare various fish curries for Gauri Habba. See what a beautiful connection there is between the sea, fish, the people here, and Ganapati. There is no feeling or fear here that fish is impure for God. Our God accepts whatever we offer with love. What if it's fish, what if it's modak, it's Shabari's love.

In Bengal, fish holds the first place in folk rituals and traditions. Here, the moment when a grandmother or grandfather feeds fish to small children is celebrated like a festival. Furthermore, at the time of marriage, the bride's family symbolically gives large fish to the groom's family to wish them well. For the people here, fish means peace and self-satisfaction. Giving fish as a gift is like pouring out prosperity. Fish dishes are a delightful experience for festivals and religious ceremonies. Bengalis do not perform any rituals without fish. Therefore, for Bengalis, it's a feeling of 'Are you in the fish... or is the fish in you?' Fish is so intertwined with them that they cannot be separated.

Even though there is a classification of river fish and sea fish in our land, river fish is the mainstay for people in towns without seas. Even if fish trucks from the coast go to towns without seas, by the time the fish reaches there, even if ice is added, the freshness would have faded. Therefore, when a daughter or son from the coast, who has gone to distant towns without seas, comes home, they make various fish curries, eat until their stomachs are full, and immediately relieve the longing for fish that had enveloped them for so long.

During the monsoon, when deep-sea fishing is on holiday, fish markets are deserted. Except for one or two types of fish, no other fish are seen in the market. At such times, crabs and dried fish are the only options. Even those who don't like river fish eagerly wait, hoping to catch at least one fish by casting a line in the river. Fishermen constantly pray for heavy rains this time so that fish reproduction increases. For them, fish means hope, life. For us, it's a delicious commodity that satisfies our hunger from time to time. Isn't our regional food culture beautiful? And the more fish lovers learn about this fish food culture, the more peace and satisfaction they feel.

The Madmal fish, whose name itself evokes wonder, is called Madmal in Tulu, meaning bride. This fish is as beautiful as a bride, and just as a bride blushes slightly, this fish is also slightly pink. The common name for this Madmal fish is Disco. Nowadays, there are almost no fish hotels in Mangalore and Udupi where this Disco fish fry is not available.
This very soft Disco fish, when its name is heard, fish lovers are just short of doing a disco dance out of joy. It is said that this fish got the name Disco because of its shimmering and swaying movement. Some people even go to the market just to see the Thorake fish, which weighs about 10-50 kg. If you stand and look at the width, long tail, eyes, gills, and fins of the Thorake fish, which resembles a large fan, your mind is amazed by its form. Large Thorake fish sometimes fall into the nets of fishermen in the sea. At such times, Thorake fish lovers, as if determined, engage in fish trade, willing to pay any price to take the Thorake fish home and make curry.

Kalluru is a light pink fish that holds small stones from the sea within itself. When eating this fish, those small stones suddenly come into the mouth. But it has a buttery taste, so even if we encounter stones, we are not disturbed and immerse ourselves in savoring its delicious taste.

Whenever I think about fish, the sea, and fishermen, Hemingway's novel 'The Old Man and the Sea' haunts me. The old fisherman Santiago, even after returning to the sea for 84 consecutive days without catching any fish, does not give up, and on the 85th day, he catches a huge fish. But shark fish bite and eat that big fish. When the old man returns to the shore after struggling with them, only the skeleton of the fish remains. Others are also amazed to see such a large fish. Oh, the fisherman's effort, victory, hope, hard work, luck, misfortune, fate – whenever I remember this novel that conveys all these, my curiosity about the sea, fish, and fishermen increases even more.
