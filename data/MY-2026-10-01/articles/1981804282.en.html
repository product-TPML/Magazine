Yogindra Maravanthe, Art: S.V. Hoogar

He could see himself completely in the five-foot-tall mirror. A smile appeared on his face, wondering if he was born to the size of the mirror, or if this mirror was made just for him. Before leaving for work, the main thing he paid attention to was the ironed uniform. It was his argument that the lines on the clothes of those working in a clothing store should be sharp. Will I ever see myself in this uniform in the mirror again? He knew the answer was no. What if he couldn't see himself in the mirror, was it possible to disappear from memory? He thought that the mind was also a kind of mirror. That day, as usual, he folded the cuff of his left sleeve and looked at his wristwatch, gently tapped his right hand for the pen in the left pocket of his shirt. He let both hands hang down and simultaneously touched the left and right pockets of his pants. Thinking that everything was where it should be, he quickly came to the door.

After completing his high school education and reaching the age to be employed, he was in the same small clothing store in the same small town in England. His classmates had long since left the town to pursue further studies and employment. For him, the biggest migration was from home to the clothing store in the morning, and then from the store to home at night. His precious adult life was spent neatly arranging clothes from the shelves, which customers, who could change their minds at any moment, would look at, touch, try on, and then crumple and discard, only for him to put them back in order as if new. Since the only skill he knew to earn a living was working in a clothing store, the thought of changing jobs never crossed his mind. He was a loyal servant, meticulously prepared for that clothing store.

In addition to welcoming customers and assisting with purchases, he was also responsible for the clothes that were not sold on a given day and needed to be neatly prepared for the next day. This was also the task he would rush to finish just before closing the store. Not being very tall, he would stretch on his tiptoes to arrange clothes on reachable shelves. Otherwise, he would climb a ladder to reach the upper shelves. At the request of customers, he would lower shirts and pants hanging from a height with a hook and hand them over; he would point out which size and type of clothing was in which corner of which direction of the room. If necessary, he would smile and lead them closer to show them. If asked, he would instantly tell who would fit which size.

Customers who had been coming to the store for years would talk to him intimately. Sometimes they would completely forget about the clothes they came to buy and talk about their children, grandchildren, and strange incidents that happened on the road while coming there that day. A note about the day's weather was always a must. Loud chatter would ensue. He too would be happy to see customers laughing in his presence. He never failed to tell the story of his cat at home to customers who talked even a little. Many customers would ask about the cat before asking about him. However, the cat at home never knew that so much talk was happening about it behind its back. It was, after all, a purebred English pet cat. It rarely ventured out of the house's premises. In a system where food and affection were easily available, why should it wander the streets and hunt, tiring itself out? Was it a mere human? Or a clothing store employee? But that it had a life, a personality, no one would know unless he told them. Whether it thought about itself as much as others thought about it, he didn't know. Whatever it was, it was his beloved cat. His wife once scolded him, saying he loved the cat more than her. She had said she would be born as a cat in her next life. He had said that if that really happened, he would bring a beautiful shirt from the clothing store and dress her in it. This life's story was over, and in his next life too, his world revolved around the clothing store.

Even though he always worked with a cheerful face and never yawned, his enthusiasm would occasionally wane. How would anyone know this? First, his left finger would slide into his pant pocket and fumble around. It would pull out a packet of paper. In another pocket, there would be a packet of tobacco powder. When he took out a sheet of paper, held it between his fingers, filled it with tobacco powder, and rolled it into a spiral, a crude cigarette shape would come to life. Immediately, he would press the rolled paper between his fingers and walk out through the back door of the shop.

Under the canopy built for smokers, he would flick his thumb over the lighter, light it, take a deep breath. He would close his eyes, exhale. As the smoke rings rose and spread across the sky, he would look up through the gaps. Then he would cough. He would take another puff. If a colleague was there to talk to, he would discuss some serious matters. Mostly about his wife. He didn't have much affection for her. He would say that, like many shrewd English men or women in domestic life who think and decide, he too was playing the same role in the same house, enduring the days, because at this stage, if they separated, the loss would be greater than the gain. He would tell the cigarette-loving circle how much to talk at home, which of his wife's words to remain silent about, where to raise his voice, and what facial expression to adopt. After finishing his smoke break and returning to the shop, unable to suppress the train of thought he had until then, he would also share family news with the esteemed customers. The secrets of various households would be exchanged effortlessly. It seemed everyone in the world was dissatisfied. Everyone was an actor in a play. His cat's life was more enjoyable than that of humans. No matter what attire they wore or how expensive the clothes they came to buy, they were not happy. He would think that everyone who came to the shop bought and wore new clothes just to hide their dissatisfaction.

Thus, one day, his retirement day approached. Surely he must have known that such a day was coming for him? He had not told even the most intimately chatting customers about his retirement. His colleagues and neighbors had no clue. Neither did the cat.

He was the only one who had worked continuously for fifty-one years in the clothing store where he was employed. But as soon as his retirement was fixed, he had strictly instructed the manager not to tell anyone about it. The rare wish of a long-serving employee was handled carefully by the higher authorities. The news of his retirement was kept away from all colleagues and customers of the store. He never mentioned retirement in any of his conversations. Usually, it was a tradition in that store to cut a cake, give gifts, and make formal farewell speeches when an employee retired. If the topic of retirement ever came up around him, he would just laugh it off, saying that his retirement was "just one day" in the end. In reality, he wished someone would celebrate his long service farewell more specially than the usual store ceremony. In his opinion, there was no other person who remained as indelible in the memories of the customers and colleagues he had interacted with. Feeling that a proper farewell was beyond the owners' capability, he had refused the usual farewell he might have received. However, the store owners had developed respect for the rare employee's principle of not wanting to accept a farewell.

He often wondered how customers and colleagues, who had grown accustomed to his service, would react when he suddenly stopped working after so many years. The question arose in him whether the store could run smoothly even for a few hours, let alone a day, without him. It was his nature to always smile while working; if he spoke, humor would arise, and his speech would be filled with sarcasm and wit. His manager thought that this silent retirement case was a continuation of that style.

Some of his customer friends even knew where his house was. When it came time to stretch, shrink, adjust, and stitch the clothes they had bought, if they came to pick them up after the store closed, he would take the altered clothes home, and such customers would pick them up from his house late at night. Some people would specifically seek out that clothing store for his special customer service. He would thrill at the thought of the customer community being stunned by his sudden absence after long service at the ancient clothing store in their town. The void he was about to create was the farewell he deserved. To remain permanently in the memory of the familiar

community

felt like the appropriate farewell ceremony. He expected some to call and others to come looking for him at home. A month before his retirement, he would talk to his wife daily about how everything would run in the store without him. He didn't tell that customer, an old man who came to the store the day before his retirement, which he had kept secret from everyone, that he wouldn't be coming to work from the next day, even though the old man hugged him emotionally and cried, saying that seeing him reminded him of his brother who had passed away unexpectedly, and that he came to the store just to see him.

Finally, the day of retirement arrived. Pushing open the door, he stepped out of the house, got into the car, and headed towards the store.

It was another ordinary day at the store, except for that last task. Before closing the store at night, he picked up an envelope with his name on it from the manager's table, who had already gone home. It contained the bonus money that clothing store employees receive upon retirement. He put it in his pocket and drove the car towards home without looking back. Without giving any hint, as he would say in his own words, the thrilling episode of retiring secretly was proceeding as planned. He felt at peace with the clandestine farewell program that had gone as he had intended.

Retirement was not just from office work; the preparation for work also stopped. Ironing the uniform, looking in the mirror, making sure there was a pen and cigarettes in the pockets. Every morning, the car would start at the same time, the gears would shift, and the car's breathing would rise and fall as it moved out of the premises, the car and its sound disappearing from the road, then returning home the same way in the evening, stopping in front of the house, pushing open the door, and him going inside, closing the door – it was a vivid picture of his street. But from the day he retired, all this ceased. A living stream of the street's daily life suddenly dried up. His alarm clock also got a rest.

On the very first day of retirement, he woke up slowly, stretching his body and listening to the sounds outside. He turned his neck to look at the clock and thought, "Oh, the store must have opened a long time ago." "Oh, am I still in bed?" He chuckled. Retirement still needed getting used to. He carefully listened to the sounds outside. He wondered if there was any commotion outside the house, if anyone had gathered by then, or if any car had stopped in front of his house. He checked the silent phone for any missed calls. He thought that neighbors might come to inquire, seeing the car parked in front of the house all day. He would have to tell the same thing over and over again to everyone who came. Just then, there was a knock at the door. He got out of bed and ran to the door. His hair was disheveled, his clothes messy, but he thought, "Never mind," and opened the door. No one was there. The postman was standing, knocking on the neighbor's door, holding mail. He closed the door, stood behind the window, and looked at the street.

During the first week of his retirement, he stood more than he sat. Through the gap in the window curtain, he could catch a glimpse of the street. People walked by. Now and then, a car would pass. Then a passerby's
steps slowed down. He wondered who this person was and carefully observed his face pressed against the windowpane. His heart beat faster, thinking that this person would now turn towards his house. But the stranger slowly passed the house and went on.

The thought came to him of who all might have been shocked by his retirement. One day or another, they would call or come looking for the one who disappeared without a word. He had so many memorable moments with everyone he knew. Yet, it was most surprising that no one came to inquire about his whereabouts. Where did all those people go after interacting with him, what did they do? Even if a visit to the clothing store was a small event, the clothes bought and worn were capable of reminding them of everything, weren't they? It seemed impossible that they wouldn't remember him. Since it had only been a week since his retirement, he wondered how anyone could forget him. Perhaps why would they even suspect that he had retired? There had been many instances during his career when he had taken leave from work due to illness. He worried if they thought it was the same now. Did I make a mistake by leaving work without telling anyone? He thought that if he had told even one or two people, the news might have slowly spread to everyone. But he was comforted by imagining the curiosity surrounding his sudden disappearance, people inquiring about him here and there, and then searching for him.

Even though the alarm didn't ring every morning, the clock of his retirement kept ticking forward. As soon as he woke up in the morning, the faces of familiar customers who used to visit the store would come to mind. Even with his eyes closed, he would feel happy wondering how much they must be remembering him. But his face would fall, thinking that no one had come to ask about him. For him, who had never been at home at that time of day, all the neighborhood sounds were new. Every sound seemed to be for him. He would think that something new was happening on his street today. It felt like the whole world was changing because of his retirement. As a few weeks passed, all the street activities that reached his eyes and ears became commonplace. He began to suspect that these sounds would remain the same even without him. Slowly waking up to some late morning vehicle sound, eating without any hurry, reading the newspaper, eating, sleeping became his new routine. For the first few weeks, he hadn't lit a single cigarette all day. He was worried that if he went behind the house to smoke, someone might come looking for him in front of the house without him knowing. When weeks passed without anything like that happening, he had completely adjusted to the new routine. Every two or three hours, he would open the back door and light a cigarette. His wife, unaccustomed to him spending so much time at home, was always watching TV. She didn't notice her husband sitting in the hall or opening the back door to smoke. Having adjusted to a routine without him for most of the time he was awake at home, she acted as if he was mostly not at home. He too would be restless all day, unable to sit still. They used to talk only after he came home from work in the evening, so even now they would talk a little at that time. Since breakfast, lunch, and dinner still appeared on the plate as usual, the beloved cat was unlikely to notice any changes in the house.

A professional life that had spanned so many months and years had effortlessly come to a halt one day, as if nothing of the sort had ever happened. Several years had passed since his retirement. But the fact that no one remembered him was a daily torment. His face, which used to light up at the thought of people coming to look for him, was now serious. The memories of working in a store for a long time, talking to customers, and joking with colleagues had become hazy in his mind. As soon as he felt that the long period of his professional life was fading from his mind, he became afraid. He had forgotten the names of many close customers. He couldn't recall the name of a customer who had once given him a pack of Indonesian cigars as a gift, no matter how long he thought about it. He tried to guess, hoping that if he remembered even the sound of the name, the name would come to mind. No matter how much he thought, he couldn't remember.

Immediately, he got up, opened the cupboard door, and searched for his uniform among the piles of clothes. Why couldn't he find it? Oh, he must have left it at the store on the last day, he thought. Was he really working in a clothing store? It became difficult to confirm whether he was or not. He immediately went to his wife and asked if she remembered the name of the clothing store where he used to work. She burst out laughing, asking what kind of question he was asking. Her reaction did not alleviate his doubt.

He would suddenly sit up in bed. It became a big problem to figure out what he used to do, if he used to do anything, or if he had always been here like this. He felt like the cat, spending its days aimlessly. He looked around, wondering where this cat had gone. It was sitting under the bed to avoid being stepped on by him, who would wander from room to room when awake. He thought that the cat, like his memory, would stay away from him.

He lit a cigarette and went to stand behind the house. No matter how much he thought, his doubt about whether he had ever worked somewhere grew. Then, one day, he stopped smoking altogether. He wondered how he could calmly light a cigarette when such a serious, decisive question was bothering him. He chuckled, thinking that he had imagined so many people all this time and worried about them forgetting him. A sense of guilt arose. Pity arose; the faces appearing on the tablet of his mind must be imaginary. He felt like crying. He gripped the arms of the chair tightly with his hands, closed his eyes, and his face tightened, filled with wrinkles. Oh, he couldn't even cry.

He couldn't sleep properly, so he went to bed, pulled the covers over himself, and lay down. He pulled the cover off his face, turned to his wife, and asked if he used to work in a clothing store. He heard no sound other than her long inhales and exhales. She must have been asleep, he thought, and reached out to shake her. A "meow" came from the direction of the kitchen. He got out of bed and went that way. The cat ran away at the sound of his footsteps. He came back to bed and closed his eyes. He fell asleep sometime. As soon as it was morning, he put on his outdoor clothes. He came out of the house. He started walking. He thought the store should be this way. Let's ask someone. He thought that if he asked for the address of the store where he worked, they would laugh at him too. That seemed like the right path. After so many years, he felt lively walking. He walked quickly. Hey! Isn't this the way to his store, the same long road, the crossroad turn, the ups and downs. He kept walking for two hours.

He stood in front of a store. It was a clothing store. He wondered if it wasn't the place where he worked, would it have been possible to come here so easily after years? A smile flashed in his eyes. His mind felt relieved. Finally, he had proof, he felt at peace. He put both hands in his pant pockets, suppressed the rising laughter, and rushed to the store steps, imagining the surprising scene that was about to unfold. The doorman standing at the store entrance politely opened the door for him, just as he did for everyone. Oh, he thought, didn't this man recognize him? He must be new. He went inside the store and looked around. An employee there saw him and asked what kind of clothes he wanted. He pointed to another corner and said, "Your size is over there." The store was full of customers, and employees were bustling about. No one noticed him, nor did they seem to recognize him. He didn't know who any of them were either. He counted on his fingers, one, two, three, five, ten... how many years it had been since he had been here.

Sweat beads formed on his forehead. His eyes blurred. His hands, which were counting the years, trembled. "No, I never worked here," he thought and walked out. But he wondered if he had worked anywhere. He wasn't sure. This search was meaningless now. He felt as if he had no strength in his legs. "Enough, let's go home," he thought and started walking.
