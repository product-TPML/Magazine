A reading of a complex poem by Gopalakrishna Adiga, one of Kannada's most important poets, which has not been subjected to much criticism or analysis, as the year of his birth centenary is about to end.

The permanent emotion of contentment

Sripada Hegde

In silence or in speech, in humor or in song,
In love or in heroic victory, man
Seeks contentment in some way or another,
Joy is a quality of the soul.

-Mankutimma

This is a verse from D.V.G.'s Mankutimmana Kagga. As soon as 'joy is a quality of the soul' is mentioned, this poem acquires a spiritual dimension. Even apart from this spiritual dimension, looking at the first three lines, the simple meaning is that continuously seeking contentment is the goal of human external and internal actions. Gopalakrishna Adiga has explored this same subject in a complex way through the exploration of experience in his poem 'Ni Baliyoliruvagge'. He has called this fundamental internal quality of man, who seeks contentment, 'Sama Tejas'. Although the title 'Ni Baliyoliruvagge' sounds like a poem about a lover/companion, it is not addressed to any external companion. Instead, it is about the Sama Tejas that seeks contentment, which is a permanent emotion within us, always with us without our knowledge.

Many poems written by Gopalakrishna Adiga are popular. For some reason, this poem seems to have not reached many people. U.R. Ananthamurthy has mentioned it many times in his speeches. This poem, which combines psychology and spirituality, has captivated my attention. Ananthamurthy's analysis has helped me understand this poem better. In the first lines of this poem, the poet says that this is with us like our heart, breath, and blood circulation, which beat constantly without our knowledge. He has specifically referred to the heart as 'hrudaya kamala' (lotus heart). This word usage seems to indicate that the heart is not just an automatic organ that pumps blood, but also the origin and abode of all our emotions.

'When you are near, like the lotus heart,
Like the lungs, the unconscious flow of blood
In the nerves...'

Our awareness of this permanent emotion of Sama Tejas within us arises in some situations when our mind is agitated and reaches a state of tension, and the resolution of this tension is possible only through this awareness.

'...When the heat that flares up between coffee,
Snacks, and meals at regular intervals,
Causes a long wave to rise and agitate,
Blood pressure increases, heart beats rapidly,
Fever rises, and the roof tiles turn red hot,
Your awareness is only for words that pop like roasted grains,
You are the goal.'

After this transient emotion of tension subsides, both these emotions exist parallelly with the permanent emotion in the depths of our mind (subconscious), which the poem depicts through the image of two sailboats floating side by side in a calm lake.

'After the fever subsides and small leaves fall as usual
On the lake, these two sailboats side by side
Move here and there on their own, and white sails
Wait in the sun, in the evening glow, in the twists and turns of the night, on parallel lines.'

There is always a chasm between the inner self and expression. The difficulty of expressing the inner self. The next part of the poem expresses the murderous (atatai) transient emotions and the fear in the depths of the mind that kill what is within before it can come out.

'...Between words and thoughts
There are some murderers; huge, nameless, prehistoric
Twilight shadows that wander;'

Adiga's famous and most quoted lines are the following.

'New scratches of tiger claws that tear through the sleeping velvet and become vocal.'

That is, although this poem as a whole has not been discussed much, the lines have been discussed individually in many contexts. Here, the poet has effectively utilized the possibilities of language expression both in terms of imagery and sound (nāda) level. On a psychological level, these lines effectively express the fundamental human nature of animalistic tendencies through the image of a tiger, and the alliteration of 'kha' and 'gha' sounds here is more effective in evoking the image of a tiger at the sound level than William Blake's famous poem 'Tyger Tyger burning bright / In the forests of the night/' (with 'ṭa' alliteration), an opinion expressed in critical circles.

In the last part, the poet describes how the Sama Tejas within us protects us from losing the rhythm of life and falling out of balance, through three images. If we consider the lines 'At the bottom of the occasional boiling pot / The solid, undisturbed base of the daily routine', this comes from Vedic rituals. Every Brahmin must perform a regular fire ritual every day and preserve that fire (embers) in an earthen pot, and this fire must be used for special fire rituals to be performed on occasional days like Sankranti, Parva, etc. The solid, undisturbed base (of the pot) of the daily routine endures the occasional fire embers. The 'state of daily undisturbedness' is possible due to the permanent emotion of Sama Tejas within us. Mental agitation or discomfort are like occasional occurrences that come and go.

'The flute that always holds the shruti (pitch) and carries the rhythm of the original tala (beat), whether it loses the beat or jumps from one beat to another.'

This is known to everyone with basic knowledge of music. The next line says that as long as this shruti is being played, the danger of falling out of balance is impossible. The way the poet uses the images of circle, radius, and circumference of geometry is interesting. Considering the heart as a sphere, the innumerable transient emotions mixed with sattva, raja, and tama (radii) arising from its center, like radii originating from the center of a circle, find their fulfillment and culmination in the circumference, which is 'Sama Tejas'.

'The innumerable radii that take shape in the center of the heart,
The Sama Tejas of the circumference that brings fulfillment to them, salutations.'

The poem ends with a salutation to this permanent emotion that enables emotional balance in life.
