The other day, I had to visit four or five shops to buy a couple of T-shirts. The reason was that I wanted a T-shirt with pockets. Apparently, many branded companies now don't have pockets on their T-shirts. The shops replied that today's youth prefer various types of T-shirts without pockets. Finally, I found T-shirts with pockets in a couple of shops and bought them. Shirts with pockets are available everywhere. But T-shirts with pockets need to be searched for. Our generation cannot wear shirts or T-shirts without pockets on the chest. Earlier, pockets were mainly needed to keep pens and pencils. Now, they are essential for keeping mobile phones.

While I was searching for T-shirts with pockets, an interesting news started appearing in newspapers. It was about the theft of the hundi (donation box) at the Shri Ram Mandir in Ayodhya. As days passed, its dark aspects began to be revealed as 'Chanda Chori' (donation theft). It came to light that a large amount of hundi money was stolen by the staff appointed to count the money in the Shri Ram Temple hundi. The lapse of the administration committee also came to light. After all this, the news was reported that the temple administration committee imposed a condition that the staff coming to count the temple hundis should wear pocketless clothes. After the theft of the hundis of the Shri Ram Mandir temple gained nationwide fame, the administration committee proceeded to make changes in the hundi counting process, and as part of that, a pocketless dress code was implemented in the temple. Some staff members reportedly left their temple jobs, refusing to accept the administration committee's condition. Even though the hundi counting process takes place under CCTV camera surveillance, reports are still coming in all media that the counters stand in such a way that the camera is obscured, steal the hundi money, first hide it in toilets, then transport it outside, and build their empire extensively. Some money and valuables might have been transported by the counting staff in pockets and inner pockets, or they might have fitted some special pockets to their clothes specifically for the purpose of stealing. The temple administration committee, like 'they locked the fort gate after looting the palace', has made some changes in the security measures of the hundis after the continuous looting of Shri Ram's hundi. One of them is to come for counting wearing pocketless clothes. In some places in our state and other states, a dress code is in force where one has to remove shirts, banians, and pants to enter temples. Its purpose might be different. But behind the condition of coming for hundi counting wearing pocketless clothes, the administration committee's intention is the safety of the hundi money and that the counters should not be distracted in any way by the heap of hundi money, wealth, and valuables. For now, they have not imposed a condition that counters should come only in innerwear!

Even in the past, there have been instances where those involved in hundi counting in many temples have stolen some money, escaping everyone's notice. This might have happened in the famous Tirupati temple or the Sabarimala Ayyappa temple. How many hundi thefts have gone unnoticed! We often read in newspapers about thieves carrying away temple hundi boxes. The theft by insiders is one kind. The theft by outsiders is professional theft. When the Ayodhya hundi theft occurred, a similar theft was reported in Badrinath. In such hundi theft cases, God remains a silent witness. Those who are in daily contact with God are aware of God's inactivity. Such acts are committed by such people. There is a Manokamana Hundi (wish-fulfilling donation box) in the Badrinath temple premises. It is said that if you pray there and put money in the hundi, offer wealth and valuables, your wishes will be fulfilled. Whether your wishes are fulfilled or not, the wishes of the temple staff there are definitely fulfilled. This is the story of all small and big temples. For devotees, devotion is an easy way to please God. Devotees try to please God by offering various kinds of temptations. The benefit of that goes to someone else. Allamaprabhu said, 'Devotion is a profit shown and eaten.' As long as there are devotees, hundis will continue to fill. Rituals will continue to happen in the name of devotion.

Excuse me, I started to talk about pockets and ended up entering temples. Financial transactions also take place in government offices. There are no rules that one should come wearing pocketless clothes in offices where money transactions take place. But in some offices, there is a rule that staff should come to the office with only a certain amount of money in their pockets and should record the money in their pockets as soon as they arrive at the office. There is a restriction that such staff should not demand bribes from the public, either on the table or under it. Bribery, corruption, and illicit earnings are always present in the government system. The earnings of the big shots exceed the size of their pockets.

Our generation cannot imagine shirts and pants without pockets. Especially, a shirt must have a pocket on the chest. What's more? Even innerwear has pockets. They are an integral part of the attire. Tailors have known their importance from the beginning. Thus, innerwear like underwear, mundas, or banians, which were called mundas, were adorned with pockets and were used like lockers. Even now, along with pant pockets, there is also a secure locker pocket. Earlier, the chest pocket on a shirt was mostly used to keep pens and paper. Now it is a mobile home. Now even lungis have pockets. There is no shortage of pockets in coats and jackets. The lack of pockets in women's attire, especially sarees, is a drawback. It does not suit the saree design. Some women use their blouses as a small safe place. Still, wherever they go, they invariably carry a small or large vanity bag. It is a part of women's adornment. It is a rival to men's pockets. The items it contains are astonishing. Their vanity bags are larger than the size of four or five men's pockets. Now, mobile phones are an integral part of every person. Women's vanity bags are safer than men's pockets for keeping them.

When we were in primary and secondary school, the two side pockets of our shorts and the pockets of our shirts used to be mini warehouses for storing various items and snacks. On one side, there would be a collection of peanuts, jaggery, roasted gram, and various kinds of fruits. On the other side, there would be marbles, gajugu (a type of seed), tops, and other things for playing. Many times, fruits like jamun and kavali would leak in the pockets, leaving permanent stains. The shirt pocket on the chest would often get stained with pen ink and look ugly. Once in a while, the one-anna, two-anna, or four-anna coins found at home would become our pocket money and increase our joy. They would be carefully kept in our pockets. If they were lost, there would be an uproar, just like the Ayodhya hundi theft today.

In our ancient scriptures, a person who had mastered sixty-four arts was considered a scholar. Among them, the art of thievery was one. Theft is an art. The sleight-of-hand theft, now called pickpocketing, is a part of the art of thievery. If you have ever friendly pickpocketed a friend or been pickpocketed, you would have experienced it. To master the art of stealing money unnoticed with sleight of hand amidst noise and commotion requires immense effort and dexterity. It requires the cleverness to escape. It is not an art that everyone can achieve. It is a theft superior to plagiarism or any other kind of cunning. Sometimes, small-scale pickpocketing also happens inside homes without the owner's knowledge. Governments continue to pickpocket the public every year by imposing various taxes and increasing prices.

Siddheshwar Sri of Vijayapura, who passed away two years ago, did not have pockets on his shirts. A white lungi below and a white shirt without pockets above were his attire. These were his clothes throughout. But he had an immense amount of devotee wealth across the country. Isn't that how saints should be? Some swamis and pontiffs used to carry a jholi (begging bag) instead of pockets and ask people to put their bad habits into the jholi. Some sris carried jholis for dasoha (charitable feeding). Jholis are a magnified form of pockets. Still, some pontiffs have built empires in the name of monasteries and temples. Their jholis are very large. Many of our pontiffs have moved away from the vachana of Ayadakki Lakkamma, 'Is desire for Shiva devotees, not just for kings, O Lord...' The desire of our elected rulers in today's democracy is limitless. Thus, their pockets are 'as wide as the world, as high as the sky, even wider...' The depth and width of those pockets are very large. The range of resources flowing into them is also large. Gandhiji's attire also did not have pockets. His life was transparent.

It is not enough if only the clothes of the staff coming for hundi counting in temples are pocketless. The administrators of the temple administration committees should also not have pockets. It is not enough if only the clothes worn do not have pockets; no person in public life or public service should first have pockets in their mind and body. The tendency to accumulate should not enter them. If the thought of stealing arises in the mind, then the pockets on the clothes worn will be filled. Temple hundis are like God's pockets. Devotion and faith are offered there. To break into them is like pickpocketing God's pocket. It is like pickpocketing all devotees. It is like holding a mirror to the moral bankruptcy of society.l
