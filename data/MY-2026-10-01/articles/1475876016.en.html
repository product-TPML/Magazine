Vasumathi Udupa, Muralidhar Rathod

When Manorama was young, she was an ordinary girl, like one among many in a crowd. She had a fair complexion. Her eyes and nose were well-defined. A village girl, she grew up according to the customs and traditions of the village. Her family wasn't overflowing with wealth, but they weren't poor either. They were a middle-class family with enough to eat and wear. There were two children in the house. The elder one was Manorama's brother, Mukunda. The second and last was this girl. Whenever Devaki thought of her, she would picture Manorama wearing a faded, loose frock, with her hair oiled and neatly braided, adorned with a string of jasmine or a garland of flowers, holding her aunt's sari pallu and moving around the house. What was the reason for her to remember her, anyway? Nothing. Manorama was the daughter of Devaki's closest friend, Manjula, and that was the only connection she had with her.

Devaki and Manjula were born and raised in the same village. They were of the same age. They were classmates until they finished SSLC, walking two miles to school and back together. Devaki was from a well-to-do family. Her father was a respected and influential man in the village, a big landlord. Compared to them, Manjula's father was an ordinary farmer. He had enough resources to provide for his family. It's not that Devaki didn't have her father's pride and prestige. But Devaki never showed it to her friend, who walked with her every day. No matter how close their friendship was, Manjula, understanding her friend's status, behaved with humility and deference towards her. Devaki was never ready on time, so Manjula would always wait for her in front of Devaki's house on the way to school. Manjula had gotten used to waiting for ten or fifteen minutes outside their house. If Devaki's mother ever came out and saw her,

she would say,

'Come in, sit down. She's still eating breakfast...' Manjula would get annoyed, thinking, 'What's wrong with her that she can't wake up half an hour earlier?' As she stood there, shifting her weight and biting her nails impatiently, Devaki would eventually emerge from inside. Manjula would rush her, saying, 'It's late, it's late...'

'Hey, nothing will happen. Your clock must be fast,' Devaki would justify, and it wasn't entirely untrue. Manjula's father had a habit of setting the clock forward by at least ten minutes. Even though everyone knew this, they had adjusted their daily routine to that time. At Manjula's house, every morning was gruel for breakfast. As soon as the coffee pot came off the stove before dawn, the gruel pot went on. Before the gruel was fully cooked, a little thin liquid would be drained, and to that, some curd, salt, and a slice of raw mango would be added for Manjula and her brother. This was their lunch for the afternoon. In winter and rainy seasons, the food would be cold and biting, and in summer, it would be stale. They would often give more of it to the dogs wagging their tails and drooling nearby than they would eat themselves. Devaki would bring a different kind of snack every day. Whether it was roti, dosa, idli, or upma, even if it was cold and dry, Manjula had developed a taste for it. Manjula would watch her friend open her lunchbox with the corner of her eye, and then reluctantly, with embarrassment, open her own. Devaki never ate what she brought all by herself. Half of what her mother packed for her would be offered to Manjula. 'No, you eat it...' Manjula would say, but she would gratefully accept what her friend offered in the lid. 'That's why she follows her around, showing her teeth...' Manjula's brother, Janardhan, would taunt his sister. He was always annoyed by his sister's behavior.

'Why do you wait at her doorstep? We can just go on our own...' Janardhan would say. Was this friendship formed just for the sake of snacks? 'Absolutely not,' Manjula would shake her head vigorously. Some people become close for no reason. Some become unpleasant. As an example of this, a bond had formed between the two girls. Manjula would accept the share Devaki generously gave, but if Manjula offered, 'You eat a little curd rice,' Devaki would unhesitatingly refuse. 'I don't want your curd rice. Only the pickle for me...' she would say, and at the end, she would take a piece of pickle, keep it in her mouth for a long time, and savor it, giving Manjula the satisfaction of having repaid a debt. Thus, the girls, who were as close as neck and throat, grew up. Both their studies ended with SSLC. Their families did not want to send them to the city for further education. Neither of them had the urge to study further. Not just them, Manjula's brother Janardhan had also stopped studying. While he was getting skilled by helping his father in agricultural activities, the girls were getting trained by helping their mother with household chores.

It might be wrong to talk about both of them together. Devaki had no compulsion to learn anything. If she wanted to learn something out of her own pleasure, there was no obstacle. Reading storybooks one a day and discarding them, and trying new recipes were Devaki's favorite pastimes, while Manjula's mother insisted that Manjula learn everything. Expectations, hopes. Manjula became skilled in everything from sweeping the front of the house with cow dung and drawing a different rangoli every day, to going to the cowshed and milking, and cooking. It was the beginning of an era when it was difficult for marriageable young men to find suitable brides. Especially for those in villages who depended on agriculture, this heat was starting to be felt. In this transitional period, the friends had grown up and were ready for marriage. They had passed adolescence. Devaki was the first to get married. 'When giving a girl, one should give her to someone of a higher status than us; when bringing a girl, one should bring her from a family of a lower status than us' is a common saying everywhere. Devaki's father believed in this saying. Salaried young men who counted their monthly wages did not appeal to this father. Devaki's father proudly married his daughter, his only beloved child, into another landlord family that was considered a notch higher in prestige, as if horns had grown on his head. 'Will it work? Will a village house work?' Neither he nor Devaki's brothers failed to inquire about their beloved daughter, who was the only one in the house. Devaki had no objection to this. Like her own house, that house also had cars for transportation, servants for household chores, and all kinds of luxuries. Manjula participated in this grand wedding celebration, which lasted for three days with a canopy erected at the doorstep, with wonder, joy, and enthusiasm. 'You must stay with me. You must not go anywhere without me,' Devaki had commanded. Although their school days of being together had long ended, their companionship had not been affected. They would meet at least once a day. Especially on days when Devaki made a new dish, Manjula would compulsorily have to go to her house to review her friend's culinary skills. Manjula was sad that her soulmate friend was getting married and moving away. But there was no choice. She too would have to detach herself from this village one day. Manjula had touched and rejoiced in the bride's glittering expensive silk sari and gold ornaments.

'How beautiful, how beautiful. If you wear all these and sit on the wedding altar, you will look exactly like a queen...' her friend praised her to the skies. In the wedding clothes shopping, Manjula also received a silk sari with a thin border as a gift from her friend. 'You shouldn't accept such expensive gifts. Do we have the means to return them?' Despite Manjula's mother's displeasure, Manjula had a blouse stitched to match the sari and shone at the wedding house. As her friend had instructed earlier, she also boarded the bus with the wedding procession to send her off to her husband's house.

***

This was the mindset of that era. If a daughter's peer got married, the anxiety of mothers about their own marriageable daughters would reach its peak. 'Where is he waiting for her...' a lament would begin. Even though the time for such excessive worrying had passed, and even though there were instances where boys' families would come forward on their own, setting aside dry tamarind, Manjula's mother had started worrying about her daughter's marriage. 'If she just sits idly like this, days will just pass...' she would say, nagging her husband. Even if a proposal came now and then, nothing would materialize. Nothing, that is, mainly the horoscopes. Even those who would verbally say, 'We don't believe in such things,' would change their tune when it came to their own matters. Horoscopes played a major role in marriage then, and still do in arranged marriages. Exceptions are very rare. Matching horoscopes, seeking divine blessings, and ultimately proceeding with an important matter like marriage with the hope of reassurance from some source about an unseen future is the belief of most people. The 'Kuja Dosha' (Mars affliction) in Manjula's horoscope was a major obstacle to her marriage. If a girl's horoscope has Kuja Dosha, the boy's horoscope must also have a similar affliction. Even if the boy's horoscope has a slightly greater affliction, it's not an obstacle. The rule that multiplying two negatives results in a positive applies here too. This is a belief.

'It's all our karma, our bad luck. Many people apparently get their Kuja Dosha horoscopes altered to show no affliction...' Manjula's mother indirectly tried to say something like this and got scolded by her husband.

'If you alter the horoscope, will the affliction be resolved? If some calamity strikes later, who will suffer? There's a way to talk,' he had said, performing the Mangalarati (a ritual with lamps).

Devaki had gone through her confinement, given birth to her first son, and they had celebrated the naming ceremony grandly, like a wedding. 'This confinement is just a hassle, dear. Mother tells me to lie down for twenty-four hours. You must come every day, okay?' Devaki had instructed. But Manjula's mother found it inconvenient for her daughter to go and sit there every day. 'What treasure have you buried there? What stories are there to talk about every day?' her mother would say. Manjula knew she was jealous. Is there anyone in the world who isn't jealous? Even if you search with a lamp, you won't find one. No matter how close people are, if something good happens to them, even if they pretend to be very happy on the surface, if they don't feel even a little envy deep down, they are considered deities, not human beings. Wasn't Manjula jealous too? It would be wrong to say no. But beyond that, when you are with very, very close people, there is a certain feeling of comfort, isn't there? Manjula had that too. If two people can share what's on their minds without needing to filter their words, it's the result of decades of friendship. A kind of inseparable bond.

***

Devaki finished her confinement and left. For a few days, Manjula felt empty, as if a heavy burden had settled on her head. As the areca nut harvest began, intense work started, leaving no time even to scratch an itch, and everything else faded away. But her mother would not fail to mention her daughter's marriage and worry about it at least once a day. Manjula was fed up with hearing it again and again.

Finally, God said, 'So be it.' Perhaps the word 'finally' is not even necessary. Time determines everything, and if we calmly await that time, make efforts within our reach, and leave everything to fate, problems will eventually find answers, even if slowly. What happened in this house was a relationship formed by giving and taking. Manjula went to another house as a bride. And another bride came to this house from that house.

Manjula's new home was like her parental home, a family that was neither too rich nor too poor. There was no difference in the work between this house and that house. Like their house, it had red cement floors with plaster peeling off here and there, a tiled roof that leaked everywhere when it rained, cattle in the cowshed. A small flower garden at the edge of the courtyard. Well water. Areca nut harvest, paddy harvest. A kitchen with groceries stored in blackened containers. Endless village housework. What Manjula found new and comforting here was her husband's company. Since she was not one to dream of sitting on a high pedestal, she accepted the mangalsutra without any objections, kicked the rice pot, and filled her husband's house. The wedding had gone smoothly for both families without any financial strain. Devaki had attended her friend's wedding with her two-year-old son. She put a gold ring on her friend's finger and instructed, 'This must always be on your finger in my memory, dear. You must not take it off...' The ring, set with a red stone, sat elegantly on Manjula's ring finger, adding to its beauty.

'I'm telling you the truth, dear. You being here was a big reason for me to rush back to my parental home. Now, it might become rare to see you...' Manjula felt there was no reason for Devaki to be so emotional. But at her friend's trusting words, her own eyes welled up.

***

Over time, Manjula had two children. The first was a boy. The second was the girl, Manorama, mentioned at the beginning of the story. When she was young, she spent more time at her grandfather's house than at her father's. So Devaki had seen this girl since she was tiny, whenever Devaki came to her parental home. Later, as the children grew older, Manorama's visits to her grandfather's house became rare. Devaki could understand the subtle reasons. Her maternal uncle's sons were growing up. Perhaps Manorama too had grown up. No mother would let a grown-up girl roam freely without keeping a close eye on her. Just when it seemed that meeting each other was a distant dream for Manjula, who would only visit her parental home in unavoidable circumstances, and Devaki, Manjula attended her brother's second son's Upanayana ceremony at her parental home, and it was no coincidence that Devaki also came to her parental home at the same time. She too had a cordial invitation to the Upanayana house. The great eagerness to see her friend at this event had drawn Devaki to her parental home. After four or five years, she bit her finger in surprise when she saw her friend's daughter. Could an ordinary girl change so much? Like nature blossoming and flourishing with the arrival of spring, Manorama, who had stepped into youth, had changed to match her name, attracting everyone's eyes and attention. As Devaki gazed at the girl, who moved like the goddess of spring, did such a dream sprout in her mind? A dream of strengthening friendship with kinship? It was not untrue that she felt it would be nice if such a girl filled her house as a daughter-in-law. Her son had just joined engineering then. Even though there was no necessity for him to study, join a job, and earn, Giridhar had a great interest in studies. Devaki's wish was that her only son should always be before her eyes. Her husband also did not take his wife's side in this matter.

'You say you'll tie down a boy who wants to study in the village, what era are you living in? In every house, at least one person is abroad. They call their parents, take them around, and send them back. What is our son lacking? Let him come if he wants to later. Let's not be selfish...'

It was like throwing a frog at someone who was already scared.

'Oh, don't fill his head with all that. If he goes far, who will be our support here?'

'Be quiet, woman. Don't talk as if I've already left for abroad. Let me finish engineering first...'

'And then? Are you thinking of going far away then?'

'No, Mom. I won't go far from you, okay? Whatever it is, it will be in this country...'

Devaki was somewhat relieved. With a half-hearted consent, she had sent her son to Bangalore.

***

If humans have one plan, fate has another. It didn't take long for Devaki to experience this. She wasn't so foolish as to believe that after all that education, her son would return to the village and live with them. He would definitely get a job. Let it be, if he was so eager, let him join a job. After working for a few years, he could satisfy his desire and then be persuaded to return to the village. Or, if not, they could uproot themselves from here and live with him. All this land? A palace-like house? Even if they couldn't go and live where he was, there was no harm in visiting, was there? The rest was his wish, his will. Devaki kept on calculating and calculating. She let her imagination run wild. The mind is a wild horse. It constantly wanders, chasing after some subject. How could Devaki be an exception to this? As she had thought, her son got a good job in a famous company at the final stage of his studies. A salary in lakhs right from the start.

'Do you really have to go to work? Is there any less income here?' Devaki didn't even believe that her objections would be useful. The fact that the job was in Bangalore was a relief. But he would have to go to other places now and then. If Giridhar was waiting for an opportunity to go abroad, Devaki would fret, 'Such an opportunity is not needed.'

When her son was twenty-five, twenty-six, Devaki started thinking about his marriage. Her mind was filled with all sorts of fears, anxieties, and worries.

'You watch too much TV. First, reduce that,' her husband would say. It wasn't a lie. Watching TV was Devaki's only way to pass the time. Especially the dramatic love affairs, deceptions, murders, and crimes that were repeatedly and sensationally broadcast, like serial stories, kept her glued to the TV.

'Oh my, what injustice and immorality happen in the world,' she would marvel. She always tried to convince her husband that she was well-informed about current affairs. She had decided that they should be careful. She had also started acting on this. Whenever her son came to the village, the instances where he had to listen to his mother's advice increased.

'Listen, I've told you. All these years, we've listened to what you said. Now you have to listen to what we say. Don't fall into the pit of love and all that, dear. It's a time when you have to drink even cold water after cooling it down.'

'Do you still think I'm a baby sucking my thumb, Mom? Do I have to learn everything from you?'

'It's not like that, dear. It's that age. You earn a lot. There won't be anyone to tell you what to do or ask you questions...'

'And then?'

'Am I telling a story to say 'and then'? You have to marry the girl we find...'

'What if I don't like her?'

'Isn't it enough if we find someone you like? I'm telling you to leave all that to us.'

'Don't rush, Mom. Let that time come...'

'As for time, if you make up your mind, it will come tomorrow itself.'

Devaki's treasury had so many things to share with her son and husband – some heard from others, some seen, and most learned from TV. Her husband was always within reach. But she had to wait for her son to visit the village. She had to control her itching tongue. It wasn't that she couldn't share things over the phone. But as soon as her mother started to introduce such topics, Giridhar would understand the hint. 'I don't have time now, Mom. I'm hanging up...' he would say, and unhesitatingly disconnect the call. Devaki would feel humiliated. However, Devaki wouldn't dwell on it for long. She would wait until he came to the village, whisper what she wanted to say into his ear, and console herself that she had done her duty. She would be surprised, biting her finger, at how time had changed, like a dosa being flipped.

It wasn't like this before. Definitely not like this. Whether the husband was a drunkard, a scoundrel, a madman, or a fool, after tying the mangalsutra, they would cling to a detached belief that 'it's all fate' and follow along. There were also several compelling reasons to follow along. If, rarely, cases of women leaving their husbands were heard, it would become a big topic of discussion wherever they went, as if it were a major event. Even if the wife had left the husband, she would still be referred to as 'the one who left her husband.' But now? Devaki sees and hears at least a few examples of divorces happening in places she knows. The time when this was considered a big deal by people is over. The universal opinion is becoming that it's better to separate than to struggle together if 'compatibility doesn't exist.' Girls, without any shame, break off relationships with those they feel 'don't fit.' Those who want to remarry do so. Even 'live-in relationships' without marriage have become common. In the past, girls were married off at a young age, and parents would wash their hands of the responsibility. It wasn't that it was less difficult for those of that age to adjust, but rather that the teaching that adjustment was inevitable would be ingrained in their minds. Those without financial independence would not dare to think of other options. Or, such thoughts would not be encouraged in the home where they were born and raised. 'Question of honor' meant fear of death for the middle class. It was the women of such families who would get caught in the pincers. If there were sisters of marriageable age, this grip would be even tighter. Now, the age of marriage for girls has moved forward. Most, like boys, have studied, found employment, and gained self-confidence through their own earnings. With only one or two children in the family, they don't have to worry unnecessarily about ruining the future of their siblings. They always have the support of their parental home for their decisions. Devaki, as a mother, thinks on behalf of a young man of marriageable age, but her mind doesn't go beyond that. She doesn't need it to. She has also gathered information about 'matrimonial centers' that help marriageable young men and women find suitable matches. She has been shocked to see how rigid and bewildering the expectations of parents of daughters are. The boy should not only be self-earning but also own a house if it's a city like Bangalore. A car for commuting. Girls are not ready to live with Rahu and Ketu (inauspicious planets). Her suspicious heart says there is no guarantee that the relationship will remain strong even after marriage. Why does her husband tell her to 'watch less TV'? But isn't it by watching that she has realized so many things? Her husband doesn't understand that. This man is the type who believes 'all that is white is milk.' The proverb says, 'Like mother, like daughter.' It's enough if the son doesn't turn out 'like father.' It's enough if he doesn't listen to her reports of 'this happened there, that happened here' with one ear and let it out the other. Whenever Devaki sees instances of love marriages gradually souring and ending in various tragedies, her heart beats erratically. Above all, there is another worm that keeps her awake. Instances where young men are married for financial temptation, then trivial reasons are given to go to court, and large sums of compensation are extorted from them. False accusations. Complaints that include the husband's in-laws and relatives. How much truth and falsehood there is in these is known only to those involved. If the matter is true, Devaki has sympathy for such people. If it's false? Devaki's daily occupation has become calculating, 'My son must be married to someone I know. He shouldn't get caught in such a whirlpool,' and for this reason, she has started leaning more and more towards her friend's daughter, Manorama. As soon as she learned that Manorama had completed her degree, Devaki, who never used to go, went to Manjula's village and, being clever, gave a reason for her unexpected visit.

'You were coming in my dreams every night, dear. I started feeling a strong urge to see you. I came without telling you...'

Manjula melted with this love. Her eyes welled up.

'You haven't changed at all, dear, from then till now. If it were anyone else, they would have grown horns on their head. What have you lacked?'

Exactly, exactly, it's precisely because she hasn't lacked anything that this precaution is taken. It's not that Manorama-like girls haven't caught anyone else's eye. But age, education, and above all, beauty, character, and nature, all must match, right? If this girl is made to catch her son's eye, it will be easy to make him fall into the pit. After all, what first appears beautiful is what is seen, isn't it?

Manorama looked more attractive than before. She still had her vivacity. If this girl filled the house, she wouldn't have to worry about her son's future. The girl also seemed humble enough to adjust.

As the time approached for Devaki to leave after eating the feast her friend had joyfully prepared and served, she asked, as if just for conversation, 'What is your daughter doing next? Her studies are over, aren't they?'

'Where are they over? She wants to do M.Sc. She wants to get a job...'

Was her castle in the air collapsing? Devaki asked with a dry throat. 'So, that means no thoughts of marriage for another three or four years...'

'Oh, where did I say that? If you listen to her, the cat will get a fever. If a good match comes, marriage first. After that, she can dance as much as she wants...'

'Girls of today. You have to agree with what she says, don't you?'

'My daughter isn't stubborn like that, dear. Her studies will continue. If a good match comes, we'll marry her off. We also need to shed our responsibilities, don't we?'

'...'

'She says she has no objection to getting married if the person agrees to let her study further. Who knows the future? Do we have to pay money to dream?'

'When you're getting the horoscope made, tell me too, okay? If I see anyone, I'll tell you...'

Manjula's eyes welled up again. If one understands, this hint is more than enough. Doesn't Manjula know that Devaki has a son of marriageable age?

'Okay, dear. If it's a known connection, it's reassuring for us too, isn't it?' Manjula had said.

Devaki got into the car, satisfied that her purpose had been served. The matter of horoscopes was just lip service. Devaki's current thought was simply that it would be enough if a familiar girl filled the house. Was she overthinking, as her husband said? Or are mothers just like this? Excessive fear about the future might be some people's nature, like hers, Devaki thought. What if her son married someone unknown, and it ended in divorce, and half their property was transferred, and it didn't end there? What if her son suffered mental anguish from this worry, and his future was completely ruined... Such nightmares had kept Devaki awake many times, and though it might sound ridiculous, it was not untrue. If such things were to happen, Devaki would sometimes feel that it was better if her son didn't get married at all, rather than this happening, and she would be distressed by her own wandering thoughts. She would repent, saying, 'Wrong, wrong...' After visiting her friend's house, she felt a new energy, a new dream, a new hope.

***

What we intensely worry about not happening, sometimes happens anyway, whether it's fate's irony, predetermined, or coincidental, Devaki's experience became an example of this reality. Giridhar had come to the village as usual, with a three-day holiday including the weekend. Devaki chose her moment and whispered in his ear,

'I've found a girl for you, dear. You should touch her after washing your hands, that's how she is...'

Her son's face tightened.

'Didn't I tell you not to rush, Mom? You're saying you've found a girl without even telling me...'

Could her son speak so rudely, hitting her in the face? This was a new experience for Devaki too.

'Don't I even have that much freedom? It was worth it to give birth to you and raise you...'

The son, using his mother's shoulder, began to speak affectionately, 'Don't get angry, dear. Wouldn't you be scared if someone told you to marry someone you don't know?'

'What are you scared of? She's someone you know...'

'What kind of person is she, will she adjust to me, will she adjust to my job, don't we need to look at all this, Mom?'

'I told you, I've found someone who will adjust...'

'Mom...'

'What are you dragging out?'

'I've been wanting to tell you for a long time. I've fallen in love with someone, Mom. She works with me.'

Devaki felt her vision darken and almost collapsed. She knew that such a thing was not uncommon in this day and age. Even though honor killings were reported here and there, most people didn't bother about caste rules. If something like this happened to someone else, Devaki would be the type to say, 'Let it be, if they are happy, it's fine.' But like everyone else, when it came to her own matters, she was against such things. Aren't we here to arrange marriages? Has marriage become a childish game? No matter how careful one is about a choice that has to last a lifetime, it's not enough. Seeing Devaki distraught as if the sky had fallen on her head, Giridhar consoled her,

'What are you thinking, Mom? She's a good girl. You see her once. You'll know. Would I take a step forward without asking you?'

'You already have, haven't you?'

'No, Mom. I'm asking if I should...'

'What will you do if I say no?'

'I know you two won't say no for no reason.'

'Isn't it, how much I've pleaded. I've told you stories of those who got married today and divorced tomorrow, haven't I told you stories of those who swallowed half the boy's property?'

'Are you saying that if you arrange the marriage, that won't happen?'

'Absolutely not. I guarantee it. Just see the girl I told you about...'

The son, imitating and mocking his mother, replied in the same tone, 'If I marry the one I love, that definitely won't happen, Mom, I guarantee it...'

'Are you turning my words back on me?'

The son laughed.

'You're just a scaredy-cat, Mom. Watch less TV.'

'You're one to talk, giving advice...'

'Don't you want your son to be happy, Mom? Would I make a decision without thinking all that through? The girl is from Delhi. But I guarantee she'll adjust to us...'

Another bomb. A reiteration of the guarantee. Would someone who knew nothing of the customs and traditions here adjust to this house? The son was smitten. Devaki didn't ask, 'Caste?' As a mother, her optimism told her she could bend him to her will.

'She's not from here. She doesn't even know our Kannada. Is this the reward you give for spoiling your only son and carrying him on your head? All this is a four-day fever, dear. You'll know the secret after you build a family. Do you need all this?'

'Isn't it the same no matter who you marry?'

'Look, don't argue with me. I've chosen someone who will adjust to us. Let's go see the girl tomorrow. You'll know my words are true...'

'Tomorrow? Tomorrow? Are you crazy, Mom?'

Devaki cried. Could her own son speak like this?

'If I leave you on the path you're on, you'll drive anyone crazy...' Devaki said.

'Understand, Mom. Not everyone who marries for love is ruined. Not all arranged marriages are successful. As you say, it's all fate. Don't try to write my fate. If something goes wrong tomorrow, you won't have the regret of having made a mistake...'

Giridhar was speaking at length. Words he had never uttered before were falling from his lips. Had he memorized all this to speak? Or was he saying it naturally because the situation demanded it?

'No, dear, this won't just remain a matter of regret. Half the property will be washed away...'

The son laughed again.

'If you keep thinking so far ahead, you'll definitely go crazy, Mom. Who knows whose future, tell me. You shouldn't overthink anything...'

'You have hot blood. Everything seems like a joke. What elders say becomes a hassle. Shouldn't you at least think as much as you can? What's a head for, otherwise?'

'Do only you have a head? Don't I have one at all?' the son said humorously.

'Your eyes have gone to your head. This is all the glory of the salary you earn...'

'Let it be, Mom. Let's drop this topic for now...'

'Don't drop the topic, drop the girl you've seen...'

'Poor thing, why are you scolding her?'

'Look, look. You're already taking her side. Should I praise someone who has fooled someone like you?'

'She didn't fool me, Mom. I fell in love myself,' the son didn't argue with his mother. He knew that another Ramayana would begin as soon as his father, who was out somewhere, came home. Giridhar, a gentleman who believed that true love had a commitment, could not even dream of betraying his love. His only hope was that his father was not as stubborn as his mother.

It was surprising but true that Manorama was also a reason why Devaki, who thought she could bend her son to her will, gave up her efforts and left everything to fate. Manorama was going to marry her maternal uncle's son, who, unable to give up his connection to the soil and his desire for education, had built a life in agriculture, combining both. He, who had completed a degree just for the sake of it, would apparently let her study further even after marriage. If she got a job, he would let her join that too. They had apparently decided their future themselves. This news reached Devaki's ears just a few months after she had mentioned her son's marriage to him, causing her castle in the air to collapse. Her brother, who had conveyed the news, hadn't stopped there.

'They were in love from the beginning, dear. That's why...' he had added a seasoning. Didn't Manjula know? Or did she know and not tell? A feeling of resentment towards her friend for no reason. Amazement at the tremendous power of love that is ready for any adjustment. Was there really love between them from the beginning? If not, what else could be the reason for this decision, which might seem a little unnatural and difficult to those with worldly experience? Love, love, and love. Devaki's inner self was already preparing to bow to destiny if the necessity arose for her to also be ready for adjustment.

Her husband was also saying the same thing, 'Their life is their own.'l
