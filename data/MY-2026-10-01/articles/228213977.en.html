Gommata Majjana: H.R. Sujatha's Essays

Nityananda B. Shetty

After the collection 'Neeli Moogina Nattu', H.R. Sujatha's second collection of essays, 'Gommata Majjana', published now, is a unique collection for many reasons. This small book of 114 pages contains seventeen essays. Some of the essays here, which deal with mythological, historical, contemporary, and everyday topics, contain a deep and subtle understanding of worldly affairs.

Some of the incidents here bear witness to the fact that Sujatha's eyes are filled with a mother's affection and love, like the dense fog in the Shiradi Ghat of Sakleshpur. A brave, serious, and noble character like Bahubali appears to the author as a child. The following lines written by Sujatha, who gazed at the monolithic statue of Bahubali, who bowed his head for the Mahamastakabhisheka, are very dear to me.

"What if Gommata was immersed in water, tender coconut water, milk, kumkum, turmeric, rice flour, and all the colors of the rainbow of human desires? What if he was adorned? What if he was soaked? Without a mother to scrub and wash under his nose, under his armpit, under his penis, and in the crevices, his bath seemed incomplete, with dirt visible here and there, striking the eye. I thought that perhaps only wind and rain, especially heavy rain, could completely soak that child." (Gommata Majjana, page 6)

This small paragraph in the middle of the essay highlights Sujatha's tenderness in looking at the world and the intensity of her motherhood.

Who will gently lift the penis, which is casually hanging between the pair of testicles on the dark line between them, on the thigh of the brave young Bahubali, who stands firmly on the Shravanabelagola hill, and gently pull back the foreskin and wash it? Similarly, who will push their hands a little further down, a little deeper, and clean the equator between the fleshy twin testicles, carved to suit that tall body? These questions themselves become poetry here. Perhaps these are lines that only a mother could write. Along with this poetic line, the interpretation that the torrential rain poured by nature is the real bath, rather than the anointing performed by institutions like tradition and culture as part of religious rituals, which is somewhat spiritual and philosophical, has illuminated the power of this essay's voice.

The essays 'Mallige Thota Allalla... Coffee Thota' (Jasmine Garden No... Coffee Garden) and 'Kannadiyalakkilidu... Ernakulam Kannadi...' (Entering the Mirror... Ernakulam Mirror...) are like the cream that rises when Sujatha gently simmers her soft and sweet language. One must read and enjoy the eloquence here. In the essay 'Hennu in Oral Tradition' (Woman in Oral Tradition), Sujatha has woven three very short stories from the strength of her memory. They are not just stories that remained within her; they are stories that haunt the readers as well. Similarly, the writings 'Ooru Mane' (Village House) and 'Macche Miyya' (Spot Miyya) are such that they raise suspicion as to whether they are essays or stories.

Along with describing the scenes she has witnessed, the author also translates them into her emotional lexicon. This process of translation can be observed in the rhythm of re-narrating the objects and details she has seen, and the minor shocks she has experienced. Sujatha, who selects many threads from her rich life experience, has developed the skill of weaving them with affection. Therefore, even though many pieces of information, details, examples, and incidents here may seem scattered, when they are all brought together, a meaningful whole is unveiled.

The essay 'Kappu Gummagala Aalapavoo Sufi Santara Dargavoo' (The Lament of Black Ghouls and the Dargah of Sufi Saints), in just four pages, is like creating a quilt from many threads of fear and devotion, worship and ritual, pain and sorrow, and covering the dargah with a sheet. Sujatha, who exposes the dark news of the mysterious world to the gentle breeze, searches for the meanings of life, interprets them within her limits, and seeks to understand what it means to make sense of life.

Every human life has a certain meaning because of their awareness of history. Sujatha's writing is concerned with where humans, who should be sensitive and humble in the presence of the profound and mysterious collection of meanings of complex history, have gone astray, without being burdened by the bundles of dark history, and without being arrogant due to the awareness of rich history. Most human expressions can blossom, suffer, or get angry due to the awareness of history. Observing these, Sujatha proudly writes about women who try to cross this existence by placing historical points of the human world like a bindi on their forehead and easily shaking off their hands.

Sujatha does not write like someone who settles the transactions of human life amidst a hundred and eight worries and writes the final comment; instead, she tries to understand them with her poetic mind. Even if she sometimes makes overly simplistic sweeping statements, her sole stance seems to be that we should critically examine our perceptions of life. Sujatha, who has the skill to identify and explain the existence of all animate and inanimate objects historically and humorously, without just looking at her own reflection in the water, is capable of continuing her writing work with even deeper dedication.l
