CHANDRAHAS KOTEKAR

S. Divakar

S. Divakar, Art: Vishwanath A.S.

Whenever you met, didn't you always ask what was special in your town? Listen, we'll tell you what all happened in our town in the last year. Now we are all very proud that we have a huge statue. Ever since that statue came to us in eighteen pieces, we haven't talked about anything else.

It was nine o'clock that night. As soon as we heard someone shouting outside the village, all of us who were eating ran towards that side with our hands still dirty from food. Although there wasn't much light in the eight and a half acres of government land, we saw thirteen large trucks lined up. About thirty to forty people were shouting 'Hoy Hoy, slowly slowly, be careful be careful' and unloading large bundles. As we all went towards those trucks out of curiosity about who they were and what might be in those bundles, some policemen, probably those who had come as escorts, showed us guns and chased us away.

After unloading all the bundles, those people pulled some tarpaulins over them, sat down in one corner, and opened the food they had brought. To be honest, none of us dared to talk to any of them. We all stood at our respective doors and just watched.

Before they all finished eating and got into the empty trucks, they called some of us closer, told us they would come again next week, and left, but they didn't come for six months. Then everything they had piled up got soaked in the monsoon rains.

Meanwhile, our village children must have been more curious about those bundles than we were. Children, after all. One afternoon, while playing, they suddenly decided something and pulled off the tarpaulin that was on the bundles. Then they danced and jumped on those bundles. Some children even played hide-and-seek among them. Once, a boy's legs got stuck in a crack between the bundles, and he cried out loudly, and some of us ran and pulled him up so forcefully that one of his legs became disabled.

Let alone the children, we too used to go to where those bundles were every day, walk around them once or twice, and stand there for a while, wondering what might be inside. Our village women brought clothes washed in the river and dried them on top of them; some ruffians, at night, tore off bits and pieces of the clothes wrapped around those bundles, spread them on the ground, and played cards. Those who had money even hosted parties. One morning, news spread that a boy from our village had brought a girl from the neighboring village and slept behind it. And as for the dogs, no need to ask, they would lift their legs on those bundles whenever they passed by.
Who sent those bundles to our village, from where, and for what purpose? Nobody knew. The Rathasaptami festival came the next week. On that day, the elders of our village gathered and decided that since those people hadn't come for so long, there was no point in waiting for them, and today was an auspicious day for us to see what was in those bundles ourselves. Accordingly, the entire village began to unpack the bundles one by one.

First, a foot was found. Ten people stood at the toes and ten at the heel, and chanting "Harohara Harohara," they had to lift it and put it down in one place with a sigh. Someone among us called Rukkujirao, the tailor of our village. As soon as he came and measured it with a tape, saying it was fourteen feet long and four feet wide, we realized how big that foot was. We started opening other bundles to see what else we might find.

By evening that day, a pair of shoulders were found. The next day, an arm, two thighs, and another foot. The third day, a flat chest was found, so we concluded it must be a male statue. As days passed, a knee, a back, another arm, and a belly with a navel were found. Finally, one day, a right hand with a clenched fist was found. But there was nothing in its fist. Then another knee and a buttock wearing a Veeragase were discovered. Thus, when we gathered everything that was in the bundles in one place, it looked like a pile of heads, torsos, hands, and legs of those who had died in battle.

Meanwhile, the Archaeological Department also got wind of it and came in droves, believing it to be an ancient, damaged idol belonging to some temple. The first thing they did after seeing what we had piled up was to dig an acre of land there, build a five to six feet high platform, and lay flagstones on it. Then, as we watched, they placed two feet side by side and cemented them so they wouldn't fall. We had all seen feet, though not on such a large scale, but to a considerable extent, like Sri Rama's feet, Hanuman's feet, Jagadguru's sacred feet, worshipped by our people in temples, on hills, in caves, or in monasteries. Some of us leaned a ladder against that pair of feet, while others, after purifying themselves, climbed up and began to worship it.

As soon as it was known that a huge statue was being erected, people from surrounding villages rushed to gather. Gradually, the news spread to distant towns. People from there also came and congratulated us by shaking our hands, as if some good fortune had come upon us.

As days passed, when the crowd became so large that none of us could manage it, the police came. They put up a barbed wire fence three-quarters of a mile from the platform.

By the time the Archaeological Department found and arranged the various parts like knees, thighs, and belly to match the feet they had already assembled, large cranes arrived. Engineers, with the help of these cranes, began to assemble the appropriate body parts one by one. All in all, it took almost a month for their work to be completed. The statue stood tall enough to be seen from a considerable distance. But it had no head.

Before the head, there must have been something in the clenched fist. Our village elders said it must have held a sword, and it must have fallen somewhere during transport. Some officials discussed among themselves how it would be if they made a sword and put it in its hand and placed the head of a valiant person like Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, and news somehow spread that they were even going to carve a sword. Some prominent people flatly opposed it, insisting that the statue needed the head of a social reformer. As soon as that was known, the party that fought for our country's independence demanded Mahatma Gandhi's head. Socialists said any head like Narayana Guru, Ambedkar, Jyotiba Phule, Periyar was acceptable. Our village devotees, thinking all this was impossible, insisted that it must be the head of Maryada Purushottama Sri Rama. At the same time, some mischief-makers decided that a Kodanda (bow) was suitable for the clenched fist and began collecting donations for its carving. Then, the householders of our village, realizing this, asked, "How can Sri Rama be alone? Shouldn't Sita be there? Is it a complete family only if Lakshmana and Hanuman are with him?" The members of the Gram Panchayat, on the other hand, provoked, "All well and good, but how can we carve Sita and Lakshmana on such a large scale to match Sri Rama? Who will carve them? How many crores of rupees will it cost? Has anyone thought about this?" Then, the arguments, debates, counter-arguments, and sophistries that started among ourselves have not been resolved to this day.

Almost a year ago, whoever bestowed this statue upon us must have been some virtuous soul. But we may not have been as virtuous as them. Whatever sins we committed in which birth, now the statue standing sky-high in our village has no head. But can any head be brought and attached? Therefore, we have been discussing for two or three months what kind of head is needed. When it comes to discussion, we truly advocate democracy. Not even a single child has been kept away from the discussion. You, having heard all this, can also participate in our discussion and freely suggest what kind of head you would like.l
