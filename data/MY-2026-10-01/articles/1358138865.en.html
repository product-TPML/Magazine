Conversations that confront the crises and debates of the times

Raghunath C H

Dialogue in Soliloquy

Manjushree Kadakola

Arun Joladkoodlagi

Arun Joladkoodlagi

In Kannada, diverse works by new generation and senior writers are being published. Some of them show their face and disappear, while others provoke us. Among the important works I have read recently, Vikram Visaji's 'Dialogue in Soliloquy' (Conversations with Writers) is important for several reasons.

The work 'Dialogue in Soliloquy' contains long conversations with 22 thinkers, including Mahasweta Devi of Bengali literature and V.M. Manjunath. Five of them have passed away. These are conversations that transcend the boundaries of Kannada and pure literature, including Sheldon Pollock, a Sanskrit scholar, Mahasweta Devi of Bengal, Nabarun Bhattacharya, Rajkumar of Hindi, Arun Kamal, and Ashok Bhaumik of visual arts. Similarly, there are conversations with Kannada's expanded wisdom, such as Sha. Shettar, Chandrashekhara Kambara, Baraguru Ramachandrappa, K.V. Narayana, H.S. Raghavendra Rao, S.N. Sridhar, Veeranna Dande, D.S. Nagabhushana, Vijaya Guttal, H.S. Shivaprakash, Rajendra Chenni, Basavaraja Kalgudi, Rahamat Tarikere, Nataraj Huliyar, and Mogalli Ganesh.

There are books that compile brief conversations of those who write in Kannada. However, books of long dialogues that become thought processes are rare. To the best of my reading, Rahamat Tarikere's 'With the Anti-Socials' and 'With the Just' are both important works. They contain conversations with 30 people from different disciplines of knowledge. Similarly, the books of political historical conversations of the socialist movement, which are not much discussed in Kannada, are B. Peerabasha's two volumes of 'Socialist' interviews. Apart from these, some books can be listed, including Devanur's conversations, Ananthamurthy's 'Conversations with Ten Contemporaries'. Thus, 'Dialogue in Soliloquy' becomes an important work as a collection of dialogues that re-examine thought designs. If we connect the threads that run equally through all the conversations here, there is a discourse on how writers and thinkers confront the crises of their time. Similarly, a re-examination of several cultural debates has been possible. These seem to intensely grapple with their own time, just as they confront the tradition of knowledge. There are traces of Kannada's universal usage.

There are many insights into the search for Kannada's own uniqueness. Basavaraja Kalgudi says, 'In Kannada, there was a principle that allowed any text to be viewed from multiple angles. In Jain philosophy, there is the concept of Anekantavada. This is not just a philosophical dimension of Jainism, but a perspective for viewing the world.' Perhaps Western criticism has kept alive the desire for Kannada's own uniqueness, which is imposed. Rahamat Tarikere, in support of his criticism, says, 'Not reading much English did not seem like a big problem to me. Kannada texts provided inspiration for my criticism.' H.S.R. finds both 'literary vision' and 'world vision' as paths to originality in relation to literary criticism. Similarly, Kuvempu's discussion on 'universal' and 'local' also seems like a longing for the search for originality. Chandrashekhara Kambara and Veeranna Dande's understanding of folk poetics seems like a search for the identity of Kannada in folklore studies.

There is a discourse here that breaks popular myths about the 'Sanskrit' language. Sheldon Pollock, in his talks about classical texts including Sanskrit, speaks about transcending the limited linguistic frameworks of literature. Sha. Shettar has sparked a new debate about Sanskrit. In a thousand years of scriptural records, Brahmins wrote very little; Vishwakarmas and Shudras wrote more. Shettar calls it a pure lie that Brahmins did not allow Sanskrit to be read, heard, or written. This is a rumor spread by those who could not read Sanskrit. All those who created great Sanskrit poetry were Shudras. The Sanskrit taught to Brahmins in agraharas was only priestly mantras, not a deep understanding of literary creation. Therefore, Brahmins were mostly unable to create great works in Sanskrit, he says. This discussion is truly astonishing. It inspires further deep exploration. Prof. Rajkumar of Hindi literature says that Hindi influenced by 'Sanskrit' was attached to Hindus, and Hindi influenced by Persian was called Urdu and attached to Muslims. He says that dividing language on the basis of religion is a great tragedy.

Many people have criticized the Bandaya literary movement and organization in their speeches. Rahamat Tarikere says, 'In my youth, the Bandaya movement helped me understand the society in front of me and the intellectual frameworks to understand it.' KVN says, 'The works of Bandaya were reactionary, and I found few works that could transcend time. In the Dalit movement, there was a need for a struggle for a dignified life, was there a Bandaya movement apart from literary works?' H.S. Shivaprakash, mocking the Bandaya slogan 'Let poetry be a sword', recalls that guns, pistols, and bombs have come instead of swords. He remembers saying, 'Let poetry be a blade' that everyone can use. D.S. Nagabhushana says, 'Poetry should never be a sword, if poetry becomes a sword, it cannot empathize with people's pain.' Baraguru says, 'Lankesh had called Bandaya 'Dambaya'. I don't know what the philosophical difference was more than this kind of ridicule and sarcasm.'

Many writers have openly shared their limitations. Mogalli Ganesh says, 'I have hurt many in criticism. I had no other way, now when I remember it, I feel sad.' Baraguru, regarding the lack of proper criticism of his works, says he is hurt, 'I am the child of the sin of the prejudice of the established.' He further writes about continuing to write, living as he writes, and maintaining the organic relationship between life and writing. This is something that most writers in this era do not firmly state. Prof. Rajkumar of Hindi literature says, 'I want to keep myself low profile,' and that the disease of gaining fame quickly has increased. He says that the biggest problem of Hindi literature is the loss of serious philosophical debates. The same situation seems to exist in other languages, including Kannada.

Many have argued that the very complex literary and social life should not be viewed in black and white. S.N. Sridhar, a little sharply, says, 'We should come out of the illusion that we can create the whole world in Kannada, more and more bilingual, multilingual scholars should be formed.'

The writers here have summarized their in-depth studies. As a result, those who have read their works gain new insights, and those who have not read them feel a greater desire to read their original works. The calculations and obstacles that arise during writing seem to be overturned during conversation. This can be observed in the words of K.V. Narayana, Kalgudi, Mogalli, S.N. Sridhar, Prof. Rajkumar, and others.
There is also a debate about what it means to be a true writer. Nabarun Bhattacharya of Bengal says, 'It is difficult for those who have no sense of sin and who do not capture the voice of the people to become true writers. If there is no real spark within the writer, the writings of cannons and bombs they create are useless. They should learn with the confidence of a plant in a cracked earth. They should have the courage to drink tea made by people who keep a kettle on a volcano.' He further says, 'We need to prepare for a new revolution now, democracy in India is not that healthy. We are somehow enduring and living. The question of the existence of capitalists has become more important than the question of the existence of people. They are constantly sharpening the sword that stabs us every day.' This is not just the experience of those who write in Bengal, but the crisis of everyone who writes in India.

Some senior writers in Kannada have nurtured younger writers as promoters of their writing. Such people need to be touched by Nabarun Bhattacharya's words. He says, 'Let the weak part of my writing be destroyed, let new people point out these weaknesses and cause their destruction, I am waiting for such new people.' The conversations here contain insights needed by the new generation. KVN says, 'Standing and looking, looking inside, looking back, looking around is not seen, those who are moving are just moving.' These words are very suggestive. KVN's words, with their deep insights, make us think new thoughts. The question raised by writers about claiming ownership of their writing is something that can spark a new discussion.
There are many insights into criticism and poetics. From KVN's 'Enough of Kannadigas now', it moves to 'The tools to analyze Kannada literary tradition are hidden within it.' Rajendra Chenni says, 'In the changed situation, critics should also give up the old-fashioned feudalism and become collaborators instead of judges.' H.S.R. says, 'While I look at a work as a critic, I also look at it as a human being,' which breaks the established discourses of criticism. Regarding the challenge posed by criticism, Tarikere says, 'Our time has posed a great challenge to engage with our literary works while confronting communalism and globalization.' Many, while criticizing Marxism, do not deny the perspective that Marxism gave to view the world.

Even though these conversations took place in different contexts, when read together, a jugalbandi has also taken place. KVN, writing about H.S. Shivaprakash's poems, says, 'In forty years, there are no memorable poems except for four or five.' In response, Shivaprakash says, 'Sometimes I feel embarrassed that I wrote too much. I wonder why I wrote so much, if there is no qualitative difference from collection to collection, why write?' This seems to be a match between the critic's view and the poet's self-reflection.
Mahasweta Devi says that others should say that her son has been influenced by her, and her son Nabarun Bhattacharya says, 'I have not been inspired by my mother's writing. I have walked my own path.' Both of them remind us of Kuvempu and Tejaswi in Kannada.

In these conversations, apart from V.M. Manjunath, Mogalli Ganesh, and Sheldon Pollock, the absence of Ambedkar's reading is significant. Many, including KVN, received Gandhiji's writings in their childhood. Kannadigas had to wait until the eighties to get Ambedkar's writings. Therefore, Gandhi's influence is greater in overall Kannada thought. Kuvempu's influence is recorded in Kannada criticism and poetics. H.S.R. says, 'It is difficult for me to say that I will only read and criticize the world's best literature. The world vision and art vision that appear in great works also appear when writing about new boys and girls.' He warns that it is the responsibility of seniors to read and write about newcomers.

If one listens to the words of linguist S.N. Sridhar, one realizes how far behind Kannada linguistics is. His insights into Kannada linguistics emphasize the need to work in this field. He has expressed with anger and regret where Kannada language and literature stand in the eyes of the world. The statement that most of Kannada's overall linguistics revolves around trivial matters, though harsh, is true. Vijaya Guttal's experiences of comparatively viewing the aspirations of the Vachanakaras with Greek literature show a different way of looking at Kannada literature from the perspective of Greek literature. On the facade of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi in Greece, 'Know Thyself' is inscribed. It is astonishing to see how this saying developed as a principle among the Vachanakaras, Dasas, and later the Tatvapada writers.

This work needs to be read carefully and ruminated upon to digest what has been read. Since Visaji has touched upon people from different fields, the depth of some fields has not been fully understood. In such places, he has only touched the surface. Sometimes, it seems like he has circumambulated the writings of those he is conversing with. Except for V.M. Manjunath, all the others are seniors. Therefore, as a junior, he has asked subtly provocative questions while showing humility. He has subtly mentioned the criticisms and reviews that existed about them in the literary world, increasing their struggle. This is like connecting the missing links of writers and filling the gaps that remained. In some places, this conversation seems to have given them a 'breathing space'. It seems that Vikram started talking to them when they were struggling to say something. On this pretext, they seem to have said what they wanted to say and felt relieved. l
