'Gandhiji is a litterateur, and literature itself'

Prof. Shakira Khanam

Gandhiji was not just a political leader; he was a litterateur, and literature itself. His life was a text, his existence a lesson. He was a profound leader who created literature in silence. Although he did not create a vast wealth of novels, stories, and poems as a writer, it is difficult to fully understand a large part of modern Indian literature by setting aside Gandhiji's thoughts, struggles, moral conflicts, and life experiences. This is because Gandhiji did not merely provide subjects for literature; he offered a new human being, a new moral perspective, and new questions.

Gandhiji's literary inclination was not just an interest in writing. It was a life's yearning to seek truth. For him, who believed that truth is the source of dharma, writing was also an experiment with truth. His 'My Experiments with Truth' is not just an autobiography; it is a record of a person constantly questioning the human within himself. Usually, in autobiographies, individuals highlight their achievements and justify their personalities. But Gandhiji honestly documented his mistakes, weaknesses, fears, desires, doubts, and moral dilemmas. From childhood mistakes to experiments with meat-eating, experiences with smoking, the humiliations in South Africa, and the formation of Satyagraha from there, his life appears as a continuous self-examination. This honesty is the great strength of his literature.

When reading Gandhiji's autobiography, the reader not only gets to know Gandhiji but also begins to question themselves. His autobiography is a living example of how literature should evoke introspection in the reader. By placing himself in the position of a culprit and documenting his life's mistakes without concealing them, he gave a new moral dimension to autobiographical literature. The search for truth continued in his writing as it did in his life.

'Hind Swaraj' is another important work reflecting Gandhiji's literary and contemplative personality. He put forward the idea that Swaraj is not merely political liberation from British rule; it also encompasses self-governance, morality, self-reliance, and responsible living as integral parts of Swaraj. His writing this work in Gujarati demonstrates his faith in Indian languages. His stance was that when speaking to people, one should use their language. Through newspapers like 'Young India', 'Navajivan', and 'Harijan', he continuously wrote on many topics including politics, society, religion, education, Gram Swaraj, women, caste, untouchability, economy, and morality.

Gandhiji's linguistic stance is one of his significant contributions to Indian literature. He believed that literature should not be distant from people; it should speak to people's lives in their language. His language was simple. But behind that simplicity was deep thought. His style of conveying profound ideas in few words became a new model for both journalism and literature. His vision that language should not be a wall separating people but a bridge connecting them gave confidence to the literature of Indian languages.

Gandhiji's literary inclination is also evident in his extensive reading. John Ruskin's 'Unto This Last' had a significant impact on his life. He translated it into Gujarati under the name 'Sarvodaya'. Tolstoy, Thoreau, the Bhagavad Gita, the Bible, the Quran, Jain and Buddhist philosophies shaped his mind. Gandhiji did not just read books; he experimented with the ideas he read in his life. Thus, for him, literature was not merely an accumulation of knowledge, but a power to change life.

Gandhiji's religious perspective was also inextricably intertwined with his literary personality. Although raised in Hinduism, he did not consider his religion to be the ultimate truth. He believed that by understanding other religions, one could understand the truth of one's own religion more deeply. The message of forgiveness and non-violence in the Bible's 'Sermon on the Mount', the aspirations of kindness, justice, and brotherhood in the Quran, the non-violence and Anekantavada of Jainism, the compassion and self-examination of Buddhism, and the values of labor and service in the Sikh tradition all engaged in dialogue with his thoughts.

Gandhiji's interest in other religions was not merely intellectual curiosity; it was a deep humanitarian concern. He showed through his life that loving one's own religion does not mean hating another's. His interfaith prayer meetings were a living example of this. Prayers, bhajans, and recitations from various religions had a place there. The sentiment 'Ishwar Allah Tere Naam' became a symbol of his religious harmony. In his view, the scriptures of different religions were not weapons of mutual opposition; they were different paths to seeking truth.

During the partition of the country, when Hindu-Muslim relations deteriorated, Gandhiji's efforts to establish peace were a true test of his religious faith. In Noakhali, Kolkata, and Delhi, he stood among the people. He undertook fasts, risking his own life. He put forward the humanitarian view that the safety of people of one religion is not the defeat of people of another religion. Thus, his religious thought also took the form of a moral question in literature.

Gandhiji's influence was not only on the themes of literature but also on the concept of literary characters. While kings, heroes, the wealthy, and officials were once the main characters of literature, in the Gandhian era, the lives of farmers, laborers, women, Dalits, the poor, and marginalized people came to the center of literature. Gandhiji's moral vision played a significant role in making the dignity and self-respect of the common person a central theme of literature.

Shivaram Karanth's 'Chomana Dudi's Choma is a symbol of a man fighting for self-respect amidst social injustice. Through his labor, pain, dreams, and helplessness, the injustice of the caste system is questioned. In Raja Rao's 'Kanthapura', the way Gandhiji's movement enters rural life, the influence of Satyagraha and non-violence, and its moral conflicts are depicted. This work shows how the freedom struggle changed the lives of common people.

In Kuvempu's 'Malegalalli Madumagalu', one can see the 'Vishwa Manava' (universal human) perspective that views humanity beyond caste, class, and social boundaries. Although Kuvempu's and Gandhiji's thoughts cannot be simplified as identical, the movement towards human dignity, equality, and broad humanity dialogues with the social thought of the Gandhian era. In D.V. Gundappa's 'Mankutimmana Kagga', thoughts on truth, morality, self-examination, and human values are also found. Although this cannot be directly called an extension of Gandhiji's literature, its dialogue with the moral thought of that period can be recognized.

Not only literature that accepted Gandhiji's influence but also literature that questioned him is significant. U.R. Ananthamurthy's 'Samskara' questions the conflicts between religion, caste, rituals, and morality. Devanuru Mahadeva's 'Kusumabale' and Siddalingaiah's Dalit poems question the deep roots of the caste system. Although Gandhiji's Harijan upliftment and struggle against untouchability were historically significant, Dalit literature questioned that mere compassion is not enough; equality, self-respect, and social justice are necessary. Thus, Gandhiji not only led to acceptance but also to literary dialogue of dissent.

This is the uniqueness of Gandhiji's literary influence. Literature arises even when he is accepted; literature arises even when he is opposed. He is a question for the writer who praises him; he is a question for the writer who criticizes him. The inability to move beyond him shows the extent of his influence. Gandhiji is not just an individual; he is a continuous question about truth, non-violence, caste, religion, nation, poverty, development, language, women, village, modernity, and human moral responsibility.

The scope of Gandhiji's thought was so vast that 'there is no plant that a goat has not touched'. He spoke about religion; but did not accept fanaticism. He spoke about politics; but said that politics needs morality. He spoke about economics; but did not see man merely as a means of production. He spoke about education; but said that education should help in personality development. He spoke about villages; but linked Gram Swaraj with self-reliance and human dignity. Women, farmers, laborers, cleanliness, health, alcoholism, untouchability, and religious harmony all became part of his thought.

His relationship with literature was also broad for this reason. Literature is not just a mirror of society; it is also a power that questions society. Gandhiji's life played both these roles. He reflected society; at the same time, he tried to change society. In his writings, moral urgency is more prominent than rhetoric. Experience is more than embellishment. Experiment is more than imagination.

Gandhiji did not write novels, nor did he compose epics; but he made his life a poem. His ashram was a laboratory, his fasts a self-examination, his foot marches a poem of life mingled with people, his vow of silence a self-dialogue, his Satyagraha a moral drama. Khadi was not just cloth; it was a symbol of self-reliance. His body became a text; his words and deeds became a message.

Thus, Gandhiji's literary significance is not in how much he wrote; it is in how many questions he wrote into the Indian mind. He did not merely provide new subjects for literature; he made the common man the hero of literature. He gave respect to the language of the people. He made self-examination the moral strength of literature. He did not use the literature of other religions as a wall of hatred, but saw them as different paths to truth. He minimized the gap between literature and life as much as possible.

Therefore, Gandhiji is not dead; he fell victim to a bullet. But as a historical figure, as a symbol, as a moral question, and as a continuous dialogue, he lives on even today. Neither the writer who accepts him nor the writer who rejects him can move beyond him. He is a question for the one who accepts his truth; he is a question for the one who points out his limitations.

To place Gandhiji merely in the pages of political history is to see only one part of his personality. He remains within Indian literature as a character, a thought, a moral conflict, a linguistic vision, a social question, a dream of religious harmony, and a continuous dialogue. His life tells us that literature is not born merely on paper; it is born from human pain, struggle, truth, mistakes, dreams, love, religion, and self-examination.

Finally, the true significance of Gandhiji's literature is not in the number of pages he wrote; it is in the depth of the questions he wrote within us. What is truth? What is dharma? How to live my belief while respecting another's belief? Is it possible to stand non-violently against injustice? Can the pain of the last person become my pain? What is the distance between my words and my life? Gandhiji places such questions before every generation. That is why he is not a closed chapter; he is a text that needs to be read anew in every era. He is not just a political leader; he is a litterateur who sought truth, a thinker who tried to build bridges between religions, a moral force who brought the common man to the center of literature, and a unique individual who shaped his life into a great literary work. To read Gandhiji is not just to read an individual; it is to read our time, our society, and ultimately ourselves. In the context of literature, Gandhiji is not an event of an era, not a finished history; he is an unending conversation, a continuously written text, and an experiment with truth that must take place within every human being.l

Prof. Shakira Khanam is proficient in Kannada and Hindi languages. She is a translator and thinker. Currently, she is the head of the Hindi department at Al-Ameen College of Arts, Science, and Commerce, Bengaluru. She has published several works including 'Contribution of Muslim Women to India's Freedom Struggle' (translation) and 'Kendalugalu' (collection of stories).
