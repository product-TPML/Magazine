D. Govinda Das, the poet who touched the Mahatma

Manjushree Kadakola

D. Govinda Das

Krishnaprasad

D. Govinda Das (1910-1987), a poet of the Navodaya era in Kannada literary tradition, is my great-uncle. His first collection of poems, 'Harijanabhyudaya', was published by the Harijan Seva Sangha of Hassan district in 1937. My friend Dr. M.S. Shekhar, along with this published collection of poems, visited my village with me and with immense effort collected his unpublished poetry, plays, and autobiography, which was then published by Kannada University, Hampi in 2007. On February 8, 2010, the birth centenary of poet D. Govinda Das was organized by the Karnataka Sahitya Academy at the Hassan Sahitya Parishat Bhavan.

The program was inaugurated by the senior Dalit rebel writer K.B. Siddaiah, who spoke as follows: 'Poet D. Govinda Das was a royal poet. He composed poetry even before independence. The Kannada literary world did not recognize him simply because he was born a Dalit,' he lamented. (February 9, 2010 'Janata Madhyama' newspaper). Surprisingly, Dalit-rebel writers have not yet discussed the literature of poet D. Govinda Das! I mention this for the readers' attention.

Another incident my great-uncle told me. The 'Prabuddha Karnataka' magazine, like the mouthpiece of Kannada literature, was being published since 1923. The editor of this magazine was A.R. Krishnashastri. Poet D. Govinda Das wrote some poems advocating for the entry of untouchables into temples. Dalits were prohibited from entering the Gommateshwara (Bahubali) temple in Shravanabelagola, famous as the 'Kashi' of Jains. (When I was young, I struggled to get a darshan). Poet D. Govinda Das expressed his rejection of entry to Gommateshwara in 1934 in his poem as follows:

'How unfortunate am I, O Gommatesha
How partial are you, O treasure of devotees
.......

My birthplace is your birthplace
My village Shravanabelagola is your land
They don't let me see you because I am low
I don't know the foundation of my birth, look.

... The poem continues like this.

D. Govinda Das sends this poem to 'Prabuddha Karnataka' magazine for publication. From the other side, the editor of the magazine, A.R. Krishnashastri, sends it back with a letter saying, 'It is not possible to publish the poem you sent in the magazine.' This incident is an example of how poet D. Govinda Das's literature was subjected to disfavor.

D. Govinda Das was a staunch follower of Gandhiji. He was a disciple of the famous social reformer R. Gopalaswamy Ayyar. Many eminent writers, including Jnanpith awardee D.R. Bendre, have composed poems about Gandhiji. Govinda Das composed more than 15 poems about Gandhiji. My great-uncle had told me about an incident where he saw and touched Gandhiji when he was a child.

Once, Gandhiji had come to Nandi Hills for rest. R. Gopalaswamy Ayyar had taken Govinda Das, who was in the Seva Dal, and his disciples there. Due to the rain the previous night, small potholes on the road were filled with water. Gandhiji was walking with long strides. Just as he was about to step into a pothole, someone shouted, 'Hey Govinda Das, hold the Sahib!' Young D. Govinda Das ran and held Gandhiji's hand, stopping him. Gandhiji then replied, 'Main theek hoon, Baba.' He came back and told the people of our village about this incident. The people of our village rushed to touch Govinda Das's hand, who had touched Gandhiji.

When I was studying in Hassan, I used to come to the village during Dasara and summer holidays. When I came to the village, my father would cut my hair, surpassing any barber. This was because even though there was a hair cutting shop in our village, they wouldn't cut the hair of people from our street. I used to experiment with the cutting skills I inherited from my father on my friends' hair. My great-uncle, who noticed my barbering expertise, called me and said, 'Cut my hair too.' (He usually got his hair cut when he came to Hassan). Otherwise, he would get his hair cut by me. This continued until I finished my B.A. studies.

Poet D. Govinda Das had a very disciplined personality. Every day after bathing, he would immerse himself in morning and evening prayers. He would spread a deer skin on the floor, sit on it, and meditate in front of a picture of Lord Rama. During his prayer time, a kind of self-imposed curfew would be in effect around our house. My great-uncle was a representative of the people for the then Mysore state. He had built a tank near his land using government grants and his own money. Cattle would quench their thirst in that tank. The mothers of the village would clean their utensils and clothes. We used this same tank for swimming. This tank was named 'Govinda Dasara Katte' and was known by that name in the surrounding villages.

An incident my great-uncle, poet D. Govinda Das, told me during a discussion. In Chikkamagaluru, my great-uncle joined a government school as an assistant teacher with the help of the doyen of Kannada literature, Masti Venkatesha Iyengar.

At this time, an unpublished poem by poet D. Govinda Das titled 'Visha Mullu' (Poisonous Thorn) goes like this:

'Everywhere are poisonous thorns, O Ayyappa, enough
Where else can Harijans move, O place
In the village, outside, on the roads
In water wells, towns, cities
Thorns in the temples of neighboring gods
...........

In fairs, in pilgrimage sites
In chhatras, chawdis, muhurtas
Friendly society, sacred assembly
Motherland is full of poisonous thorns'

When this poem was shown to Masti, an angry Masti said, 'Are you trying to ruin the country? First, remove the last line of the last stanza.' He agreed, bowed, and left. However, he did not print this poem in his published collection of poems. But he retained the last line of the original poem as it was. Thus, poet D. Govinda Das faced insult and rejection at every step.

My great-uncle had built a Sri Rama temple for our street in 1932 with government funds (now the youth of our village have converted it into a library). My great-uncle would sit on the platform of this Rama temple and capture the events happening around him in his eyes. When I had time, I would sit with him on the platform and ask him various questions. D.R. Manjegowda, a freedom fighter from our village, would join us there and discuss national affairs. Once, when I asked Appaji, sitting on the platform of the Rama temple, 'Why do you worship sitting on a deer skin?', he smiled faintly and remained silent.

At the same time, he asked Bhoje Gowda from our neighboring village, who was passing by, 'Hey, are you going to the market? What's the price of coconuts?' The person replied, 'Holegga,' and left. 'Hey, son, see what he says. Coconuts are Holeggava!' he said. I didn't understand anything and asked, 'What does Holegga mean?' Hole agga means 'free price,' no one buys the coconuts! That is, in our society, our people are used for free. If someone dies in the village, they dig their grave, if there is a wedding in an Okkaliga house, they put up a canopy in front of their house, if cattle die, they carry them out of the village and throw them away, they have to beat the drum for free at fairs, festivals, and death ceremonies. That's why he compared the price of coconuts to our caste, he explained.

I was in the ninth grade then. Our neighboring village was Dinka. A person belonging to the Okkaliga caste in our village had made a vow to Dinka Devi for some reason. The time came to fulfill the vow. The person's wish had been granted by Dinka Devi's vow. He had promised the mother a buffalo for sacrifice. After discussing with the villagers, they brought a strong buffalo one day, gave it an oil bath, performed 'madlakki' and 'arati' in front of every house in the village, and after the puja, brought the buffalo to our street. A team from our street, already prepared with knives and daggers, stood ready to slaughter the buffalo. When my great-uncle learned about this, he sent word that he would file a police complaint if they proceeded to slaughter the buffalo. The buffalo-slaughtering team, shocked by the news, grumbled, 'What's wrong with these people? We are the ones who slaughter, we are the ones who eat,' and went home. The Okkaliga leaders, who learned that Govinda Das was obstructing the slaughter of the buffalo, gathered in rows near the Rama temple on our street. Honnenahalli Puttegowda, who presided over justice panchayats for seven villages, rushed there. He said, 'Dasare, let them be, let them slaughter and eat as they please.' Then my great-uncle said, 'I will not let the buffalo be slaughtered, nor will I let it be eaten. If you do, then slaughter it over my chest.' Enraged by these words, a group of Okkaliga youths started abusing my great-uncle in singular terms. A team of youths from our street, who were studying PUC and degree, turned against the opposing youths, saying they would not let the buffalo be slaughtered. My great-uncle intervened and pacified the youths from our street, saying, 'Is it goat and sheep meat for your street people, and buffalo meat for our street people? If we are to eat, let everyone eat buffalo meat. Otherwise, give us goat and sheep meat.' Then everyone gathered there whispered among themselves and left as if there was no toll on the way they came. At this time, the words spoken by Padma Shri awardee, Nadoja Dr. Siddalingaiah at the centenary celebration of D. Govinda Das in Hassan come to mind. Those words were: 'Where there is inequality, there is impurity. Where there is equality, there is purity. D. Govinda Das's poetry, plays, and articles have been an inspiration to the youth and writers,' his analysis is noteworthy.

I used to engage in conversations with my Appaji from time to time. He would speak with regret, disgusted by the social system. He would lament, 'When will our people be free from this exploitation?' He would discuss Buddha's ideas with me. He used to say that our people should convert to Buddhism. Only then would liberation be possible for our caste.

One of his unpublished poems goes like this:

'Conversion is the only great mantra
A strategy to eradicate untouchability
The sacred land of Bharata, which savored Ramayana and Mahabharata
A burning pit of Jamadagni for untouchables
........................

Ramanuja, Basava, Gandhi
Failed
Monks toiled hard, suffered, and lost
Even those bound by rituals and traditions lost
For this, conversion is the great mantra
A strategy to eradicate untouchability'

Thus, through his literature, he showed the path of Buddha to the suffering people.l

Krishnadas, the grandson of poet D. Govinda Das, is from Hassan. His life and personality are intertwined with the 'Dalit Sangharsh Samiti'. He is a postgraduate in Economics. He rejected all job offers that came his way and found fulfillment in life through the organization. Krishnadas, who was a companion of Chandraprasad Tyagi, is still striving to strengthen the philosophy and organization of 'Dasamsa'.
