Yankappa ate, lay on his back and watched the clouds. The moon in the clouds was rounder and shone like silver more than ever. Various pictures appeared on the moon's face. He stared at them intently. People were lying around after eating. A cool breeze was blowing. The croaking of frogs could be heard from the stream. The lullaby of many creatures filled his ears. The more he heard, the more joyful his mind became. The water in the stream flowed peacefully. The open field was clearly visible in the moonlight. Yankappa, who was staring at the moonlight, glanced at the field. He saw someone coming towards their street. The shadow of the approaching people seemed to be longer than them, moving forward. 'Who are these people?' he thought to himself. As the shadow came closer, he realized it was Shambappa Gowda. 'Gowda at this hour...' He was worried for a moment. As he was thinking, Gowda came and stood near him. 'Oh... Gowda...' he suddenly sat up.

'Ha... Ha... Yankappa, have you eaten?' he asked, standing and looking around.

'Yes... Sir... If you had sent word, we would have come ourselves.'

'I had to come towards the street,' he said, looking towards the street. People were sleeping in front of the Janata houses everywhere. They were sleeping soundly without a care.

'Sir...' he stood with folded hands.

'Nothing, how many families are there here?'

'Sir, there are forty families...'

'How many years has it been since the Janata house was built?'

'Sir, it's been five years. You told the government and had it built, didn't you?'

'Yes... I had forgotten how many years it's been.'

'If the river overflows, won't the water come into the street?'

'It will come... That's why we've asked you many times to build a ten-foot wall. You said you would build it.'

'Yes, yes, I said that,' he said, standing deep in thought.

Not knowing what to say, Yankappa stood with wide eyes.

'Yankappa, come here,' he said and walked towards his field. Yankappa followed him. After walking a short distance, he said, 'Yankappa... my son has come from America to Bangalore. He wants to set up a mobile factory in the village. He needs land for that. This street area near the river has been chosen by him. He will set up the factory here. So you will have to leave the street,' he said, looking at the moonlight.

Hearing Gowda's words, Yankappa felt as if the clouds had burst and fallen on him. The brightly shining moon seemed to have turned black. It felt as if the ground had collapsed and gone to the netherworld. He felt as if he had fallen backwards, his tongue stuck in his throat, unable to move.

'What is this, Gowda... Where should we go...'

'We have a field near Goladinni, I will give you land there. I will also build houses for you in the same way.'

'Sir, we have been here for hundreds of years. Whether in hardship or happiness, our relationship has grown with the stream. Sir... our life has grown with this land. How can we leave it now...'

'I know that too. But it's my son's wish. What to do. No matter how much I explained, he didn't listen...'

'Sir... you gave us food... we ate and lived. We toiled in your field...'

'I know that. But my son needs to know... It was a mistake to send him to America for studies.'

'Sir... when will he come to the village, we will talk to him.'

'That's impossible. He talks about the law. This place where you live belongs to our great-grandfather. He gave it to your people to live. You have been living here. But this place is not yours according to the law. It belongs to our family. If we give permission, you
should stay, otherwise you should leave. This is law, justice.'

'Sir, what do we know about all that... We have lived here for so many years. We have sheep, chickens, cows, buffaloes, dogs, cats, all with us... Where will we take all of them. This doesn't seem right to me.'

'Yes, I don't like it either. I want you to stay here. But my son won't listen. He says he will go to court. If he goes to court, he will win. You won't even get the field in Goladinni,' he said, turning his face towards the moon.

'Sir... what time has come...' He shed tears.

'Alright, we'll talk in the morning. I had to tell you this, and I have. The rest is up to you,' said Shambappa Gowda, walking towards his house on the moonlit path.

Gowda's shadow seemed to grow long in the moonlight and run with long strides. His shadow, darker than Gowda's, filled the earth like a cloud, and as Yankappa watched, his eyes merged with the shadow for a moment, and Gowda disappeared. The shadow seemed to swallow Gowda suddenly. 'Oh Gowda...' he screamed sharply.

Gowda did not hear Yankappa's scream. He continued to walk forward. Yankappa looked around in a panic. The moonlight was spread all over the earth, looking like a sea of milk everywhere. 'Why is this happening, God...' he lamented his own condition. The neem trees and mango trees scattered in the field were clearly visible in the moonlight. Those green trees stood adorned with flowers all over their bodies. Looking at the trees, his mind felt a little peaceful.

The people of the street had been living here for the past one or two hundred years. Most of them used to go to Gowda's fields to work. Now, many changes had taken place in the street. The people of the street had gone to school, obtained degrees, got jobs, and gone to cities. Most of the young people refused to work in Gowda's fields and went to Bangalore to work. Only a few elders lived in the street. They did not want to leave the street. Even when their children called them to Bangalore, they would say, 'We won't come... We have grown up eating Gowda's food, we will stay here.'

Yankappa's son had also joined Bangalore four years ago. When Yankappa was called to Bangalore, he would say, 'I won't come. How many days will you live and eat there? You have to come to the village someday. You can earn and
eat there. You can't live there permanently,' and he stayed in the street, raising a few goats and sheep. He had asked Gowda many times to build a wall for the stream so that the street would be safe. Gowda had said, 'Yankappa, it's not possible to build such a wall. It requires a lot of money. The government has to come forward and build it. It will happen if our MLA tries.'

Yankappa, along with the people of the street, had met the MLAs three or four times and requested them to build a wall. They had said, 'Vote for us. We will build it.' But they had not come back this way. However, Yankappa's efforts did not stop.

Since he had been working in Gowda's fields for one or two hundred years, he believed that they would help him. But now, when Gowda came and told him to leave the street, he didn't know what to do. Worried, he lay down in front of his Janata house, looking at the sky. He couldn't sleep. It was still a long time until morning. He was worried about how to inform the people of the street about this. He could hear cows, buffaloes, sheep, and goats sneezing, breathing, and grunting here and there. His mind was not at peace. He decided to go to the stream and come back.

The stream flowed peacefully. The gurgling sound of water filled his ears. 'Mother, you must protect us,' he prayed with folded hands. The life of the street was intertwined with the water of the stream. Sometimes, when it rained, the stream would rise. Water would enter the houses sometimes. But there was no danger to life. Yankappa was worried that if there was a heavy rain in the future, water would enter the street. Therefore, he was trying to build a wall as high as a man, thinking there would be no problem. He thought he would go to Gowda's house in the morning and plead, 'You must protect us.' Gowda would not let him down. He comforted himself with this thought and lay down. Only his eyes were closed. His mind was restless.

While Yankappa was immersed in worry in the street, on the other side, Purushottam, Shambappa Gowda's son, was negotiating with a smart mobile company in a five-star hotel.

'Look, Purushottam, we will invest a hundred crores. We will give you a share of the profit. We will arrange everything. Send the people living there to another place. We really like that place. Most importantly, the water is natural. Our team has reported that water flows there constantly,' said Surendra, the manager of the smart company.

'Don't worry about that, my father has plenty of land. He will give them land elsewhere. They will settle there,' he said.

'That's not how it is, Purushottam. It's not easy. How can they just leave suddenly? Don't they need houses to live there? Where will they stay after they leave? They have cattle, sheep, chickens with them. You don't know all this. You are comfortable. You work in an American company and earn a lot of money. We experienced all this when we established the company. Here, we have to give so many kickbacks to these officials and politicians. No matter how much we give them, it's not enough. Along with that, we have to manage these people. You will sign and go to America,' he complained about his problem. 'Oh, I don't know all that. I have told my father. He will take care of all that. I will not stay in this country. I will stay in America. I need fifty crores to buy a good big house there. If I get that much money, it's enough. Let anything happen here. Anyway, the percentage will come later. I will live comfortably in America,' said Purushottam with a smile.

'Oh... we know... you are all like this. You are born and raised here. You can't live here. You fly abroad for higher education. You stay there. That's why people like us look for people like you to establish companies. Our work is easy. If we had asked your father about this, he would not have agreed.'

'Don't worry. I have told everything to my father. He will send them from there. You start the work,' he said indifferently. 'Alright. Sign the agreement. We will take care of the rest. We will not evict anyone. We will make them leave on their own,' he said with a smile.

'I will tell my father that I have signed,' Purushottam called his father.

'What, son, why are you calling at this hour?'

'No, father, when it's day for us, it's night for you. When I come to Bangalore, it's night here. So it feels like this time for you to call.'

'Oh, alright... I understood after you went to America for studies. That's not the point. I asked because you are in Bangalore and called so late at night.'

'That's it, father... I signed an agreement with the mobile company. I will go to America tomorrow. You can sell everything in the coming days and come there. Or if any other company contacts us, let's sell all our fields to them. You come to America and live comfortably. Why do you live alone in that big house? Mother is not there either. I will not stay in India. I don't like this weather. There are no facilities here. They don't value humans at all.'

'Alright, son, how many times have you said this. We will see what happens next. One hundred and forty crore people live here. I am one of them,' he said and cut the phone.

'Father never understands life like this,' he thought. Surendra noticed the change in Purushottam's face. 'What did your father say...?' he asked.

'Nothing, I have told him everything. You can do your work now. I will go to America tomorrow. I have seen a house with five thousand square feet and a ten-acre garden there. I will buy it. Send them the money,' he said.

'No problem, Purushottam. Our company owners are in America.
We work as they say. So the company will give you the money in America,' he said, handing him a whiskey bottle, 'Take this, it's a good brand, two hundred years old. It's been that many years since your street people started living there. So this two-hundred-year-old drink is a gift for you,' he said, shaking hands and bidding farewell. 'Alright, see you again,' he said, carefully putting the whiskey bottle in his bag. 'That's right. How did you know I liked all this?' asked Purushottam.

Sudhindra coolly said, 'Sir, when we establish a company, we study it from all angles. We examine everything thoroughly and undertake the work after seeing what everyone needs. That's how the British trapped us all, isn't it? Now we are trapping ourselves,' he said meaningfully.

'Oh... alright. I don't want this country anyway. Do whatever you want,' he said and left. 'Purushottam, we won't do anything. We will just set up a mobile factory in your place,' said Sudhindra, saying goodbye.

Purushottam went to America. On this side, as soon as the sun rose, Yankappa ran straight to Shambappa Gowda's house. Gowda was still sleeping. A ghostly house. Built at some
time, a hundred people could live there. Gowda was sleeping in some corner.

'Oh... Gowda,' he shouted. 'Who is it...?' Gowda got up and came. 'Sir, how do you live alone in such a big house...?'

'Why are you asking as if you heard something new? I'm used to all that. Siddamma comes and cooks, and I eat,' he said and sat on a chair. 'But Gowda, where do we go if we leave the street?'

'Look, Yankappa, there's no point in me saying anything. All agreements are made in Bangalore. We don't know anything. Whatever they say, they talk about the law. It's not possible to go against the law. You just have to leave the street.' 'That's not how it is, how can we just leave suddenly?'

'Hey Yankappa, now the company people will come with a JCB to build a wall for your street. Stay until the wall is built. My son has given me notice. Who should I tell? That's why I said I would give you land near Goladinni. If anything is found in the ground there, I don't know what my son will do with it. That is in my name. You stay there as long as I am alive. After that, it will be as God wills,' he said, shedding tears.

'Sir... why are you crying?' Yankappa asked, wiping his tears and sitting down.

'Yankappa... you go back to the street now. Sudhindra will come. He will build a wall for the stream. We will not obstruct him. My son has sold the street land to them. Now that stream and street land belong to them. Nothing can be done. It's not property I made. It was made by ancestors. I have no authority over it. It should go to the next generation. They can do whatever they want with it. We have no right to question it. They have come with such an agreement,' he said. Yankappa felt as if lightning had struck him. To whom else should he tell, he thought, and with a dejected face, he came to the street. Six or seven JCBs had already arrived in the street. All the people of the street were standing and watching them. Sudhindra had already come and was telling the JCB operators how to dig the foundation for the wall. Yankappa went straight to him. 'Sir... where should we go...?' he asked.

'You don't have to go anywhere. If you want, stay here until we build the wall. Then go to Gowda's field,' he said calmly.

Yankappa didn't know what to say next and collapsed. The sound of the JCBs echoed to the sky.l
