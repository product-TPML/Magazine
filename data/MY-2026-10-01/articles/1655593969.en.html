Manjushree Kadakola

Nigoni

Manjushree Kadakola

Govindaraju M. Kallluru

Govindaraju M. Kalluru

Ravikumar Neeha, who has dedicated most of his intellectual efforts to the field of criticism and research, has been pursuing story and novel writing for the past three to four years. Even when Neeha shifted from research to narrative, what haunted him relentlessly were the objects, individuals, and subjects that had been largely neglected within the history of the 'world called village,' which had served as the fundamental element for his research so far. These very elements have now descended into his narrative. As a result, we have before us today the collection of stories titled 'Avu Ange' and the novel titled 'Nigoni'.

'Nigoni' is Neeha's first novel. This work of art, woven linguistically, culturally, as a part of history, and as an expansion of Kannada wisdom, possesses some distinct characteristics. The thrilling language that pervades the novel; at times dark, cloudy, and like a dazzling light, 'Nigoni' has the power to captivate its readers into a thrilling trap with its cinematic technique that is felt even while reading.
However, 'Nigoni' does not attempt to dull the wisdom of its discerning readers along with its thrilling reading experience. This too should be called a unique quality of this novel. This is because it tries to keep the readers' consciousness always awake by providing them with a full opportunity to communicate with 'Nigoni' not only emotionally but also intellectually. This is also the immediate and permanent responsibility of any 'good work of art'. Even while being caught in the charm of 'Nigoni', which is like fragrant jasmine written on the Nellakki Hase, this novel leaves us with some important questions.

Those questions are as follows.

1. How does 'Nigoni' continue the creativity of the Kannada language? What is the future path of its language and linguistic study?

2. What aspects does this novel wish to add to the contemporary understanding of Kannada?

3. Since the author of 'Nigoni' is also a critic, what is the consciousness of art when criticism and creativity occur together?

4. Through this novel, the work of re-establishing memories of the past from the colonial period to Nalvadi is happening. When the historian and the author are one, what kind of re-narration of Kannada/Karnataka history does he attempt, and what is its nature?

Can the discussion of these questions be called the lifeblood of 'Nigoni'? At the end of the 256-page novel 'Nigoni', meanings are provided for more than a hundred words from the regional Kannada of Tumakuru-Koratagere. Here, the Kannada reading world encounters 'new words' throughout the novel, which are not so much new words as words we have not recognized.

Thus, while the practice of providing word-meanings at the end of a literary work or in the form of footnotes is not new to Kannada, such works open up opportunities for readers to adopt new words that are not in their linguistic world. Through this, they continuously strive for the expansion of language. Therefore, an author always tries to develop and illuminate his language through his work.
'Nigoni' is working to expand Kannada words and thereby the Kannada language by finding new words for Kannada. Whenever we observe this process, we need to consider whether we only focused on commonly used words when creating dictionaries, and to what extent we recognized and documented words used regionally and by caste.

There has always been a strong desire for a work to expand its language and the vision that can be obtained through language. Works like 'Nigoni' are doing this work as a social responsibility. Just as learning other languages is a matter of pride, so too should practicing our native languages be a priority. This is because every regional language survives among us with its own unique characteristics. The question of 'mother tongue education' that repeatedly comes to the forefront, and the relationship between language and caste, also compels a re-examination of linguistic diversity.
The Kannada of the Koratagere region in 'Nigoni' makes us ask many questions such as what is 'mother tongue education', what is the nature of mother tongue education, whose mother tongue, and what is the mother tongue. Through this, it mirrors the linguistic hierarchy system that is naturally intertwined within the Kannada language. It makes us ponder the effects of social and economic inequalities on regional languages. 'Nigoni' raises questions about the difficulties we faced in creating our local languages and linguistic materials.
The flow of time in 'Nigoni', from the advent of colonialism to the coronation of Nalvadi, makes it intriguing to see what aspects it wishes to add to the contemporary understanding of Kannada and how it confronts this era. Among the characters that haunt throughout 'Nigoni', a very important one is 'General Richard Steward Dobbs'. As soon as he was appointed as a British officer to the Chitradurga-Tumakuru region, Dobbs eliminated many problems that were afflicting the people in the name of rituals and traditions in this area.

He prohibits the practice of making women and girls Basavi in the name of 'Atithi Devo Bhava' (Guest is God). To all the words like 'Our guest is God. You are our gods, aren't you, master? That's why we keep Basavi to please the guests. They have no other life. Their job is to please everyone who comes,' Dobbs calls the Amaldars and says, 'It doesn't seem right to me that those women dance in skimpy clothes. I don't want the income from those women anymore. What kind of tradition is this? Shame! You should be ashamed. To make those women dance like this and enjoy it. Stop it here,' and issues a decree.

Before increasing revenue, he constructs new tanks and dams, and takes measures to improve soil fertility in agricultural lands. He suppresses thieves and robbers who looted people and money. He prevents the menace of animals attacking crops and people. He visits temples in the Chitradurga region, including Devarayanadurga, and opens them to people of all castes.

When epidemics like cholera, plague, and influenza struck, he joined with Siddhas and Sadhus to protect the people of the Chitradurga region. He grieves, wondering how he can remain clean and peaceful without touching or embracing others when the whole country is a land of death, mourning, and disease. Dobbs, who possessed both understanding and compassion, began to build the Chitradurga-Tumakuru region like a 'realm of kings akin to Janaka'. Dobbs' foresight, where he would go to the people with his servant Talwar Nagra even for a small problem of the subjects, makes one ponder about the administrative policies of current politics.

'Nigoni' is not just a work of art, but also a cultural text of Kannada. Because it carries this responsibility, this novel contains some cultural questions within itself. In the current era where the food and medicine market is filled with plant-based products, 'Nigoni' provides a long list of which animal and bird meats worked as medicine for which diseases and ailments in 'its time'.

'Nigoni' teaches a health lesson about what can happen if one consumes the meat and specific parts of many animals such as wild boar, pig, deer, cow, sparrow, white rice bird, peacock, jungle fowl, squirrel, monitor lizard, cat, rabbit, porcupine, wild pig, mongoose, etc. Furthermore, 'Nigoni' attempts to expand the concept of 'pure cow ghee' which today dominates the market. Along with cow ghee, pig ghee, monitor lizard ghee, and peacock ghee mentioned in the novel, 'Nigoni' explains the traditional medicinal system where fatty substances produced from animal and bird meat and fat found inside animal bones were also used as ghee for various purposes. It critically examines the market opportunities available for plant-based food and medicines versus meat-based food and medicines. It also raises questions about the system where popular market spaces like newspapers, visual media, etc., are widely covered by plant-based products.

Moreover, in countless daily products, local plant-based ingredients like salt, ginger, lemon, tulasi, hibiscus, soapnut, giloy, neem leaves, etc., fill the TV screen, but the modern advertising market, which does not include any meat with health and medicine, provides most of its advertising space to plant-based products, intensely promoting the plant-based market and giving very little space in popular media to the market of meat and meat-based products, which is the food of the majority. This is a market strategy to separate people in the name of food or to demote non-vegetarians and meat products to a second class in the name of pure veg and plant-based. 'Nigoni' shatters the 'veg' medicine market created by the modern market by providing details of 'meat medicine'.

This work questions the green capital created by the modern market, along with the nationalism linked to plant products and the prohibitions linked to non-vegetarian food. Is capital accumulation, created in this country in the name of nationalism, gods, and beliefs, also happening in the name of plant-based food and medicines? What else should this be called if not a division of capital and market?

In front of our various traditions that have used meat and meat-based products as food and medicine, this work attempts to provide a different definition of health through local food and simple walking, for studies that show only 'plant-based' products to the modern market, or that Ayurveda means only herbs, or that yoga and exercise mean unnaturally twisting and displaying human limbs and head. The natural walk seen throughout the novel appears as yoga and exercise.

'Nigoni' attempts to re-establish the concept of pluralistic Karnataka throughout its epic journey. It tries to subtly depict the cruelties of anxieties between castes and religions. The Christian officers in the novel go from village to village and engage in public service. The wisdom of 'treating others as oneself' is attached to the Christian characters throughout this work. The concern for other religions, which was not ours, shakes the faith of the castes and religions that we embraced and accepted as our own. Along with that, it leaves the question of what is my religion and my identity. The internal pressures for conversion and migration are no longer just internal simmering. They have now transformed into the pressures of moral policing. Many characters in the novel confront this pressure. They put to the test the history that is built based on landmarks. In this novel, the history of 'Nigoni' begins with the characters at the bottom of the social hierarchy or with people who have no identity. Thus, 'Nigoni' attempts to construct the cultural and social history of Kannada through these end points. Therefore, it appears as a people's history of Karnataka.

The dense details expressed here seem to be the very lifeblood of 'Nigoni'. 'Nigoni' shines even brighter with the rich details of 'the bear hunting incident', 'the dogs' heat', 'the incident of Sinuga going to learn mantras', 'the incident of searching for herbs', 'the love of Ayya and Narsi', 'Padumakka's story', 'the wedding ceremony at Kodi', 'how Christianity spread in Gubbi', 'Kodi's valor', 'the narrative of the Madiga community settling in the Tumakuru region, and as part of it, the formation of Gallebani in the Tumakuru region; the leather industry, the rare plant wealth on Siddhara Betta that could convert iron into gold, details of Sanjeevini plants, the story of Hyder Ali receiving treatment for a back injury, etc.
The construction skill that shines throughout the novel, the way language is used like raw earth, the therapeutic vision that uses the novel and its artistic tools against the politics of division without the reader's awareness, and the details that appear like cinematic techniques here and there, 'Nigoni' strives to further expand the world of Kannada novels. Through this novel, Ravikumar Neeha has found a new town for Kannada. 'Nigoni', the area around the Jayamangali river, is a world that represents pluralistic Karnataka. The need for such towns and worlds is greater today than ever before.
