Varadamma, who was working near the stove, got annoyed when her daughter called for water for the second time. She grumbled, "How much water do you empty? My arms will break from carrying water!" As she took a mug of water and approached the door, Kusuma, from inside, snapped, "Amma, I have my period! Don't you know? You're making such a fuss over one mug of water?" Varadamma placed the mug near the door and returned, speaking in a lowered voice. Lately, she couldn't understand why she was getting so angry. The news she received recently, the competition near the well, someone's bad jokes – all these things were suppressed in her mind and would erupt as anger at some unrelated time, making her regret it later.

"Amma, is breakfast ready? Do you need any help?"

"Isn't it Sunday today?"

"They have special classes for tenth graders."

After Kusuma left, Varadamma gave her son a plate of chitranna, filled a plate for herself, and sat on the outer veranda, leaning against the wall. Twenty meters down a small slope in front of the house, the village lake was visible. To the right, adjacent to the bank, was their one-acre field. She had heard that her husband and mother-in-law had built a house here the year she got married, after dividing their property. She had enthusiastically joined her mother-in-law in plastering the mud walls with cow dung. For twenty years, sitting or standing in this spot, enjoying breakfast or tea while looking out at the world, had become dear to her. In the monsoon, the lake, filled with red water, would overflow. The lake, bustling with people washing clothes, swimming, and bathing cattle, was never short of activity. In the afternoons, there were fishermen, and in the evenings, people came for a stroll. Only this corner, near the tree, was reserved for drawing drinking water. For the past few years, as winter ended, the water would dry up! Along with it, the water in the wells would also hit rock bottom. It was only February now. Except for a few muddy puddles here and there, the lake looked like a barren field. She noticed that the bottom of the silted lake was almost at the level of the bank. How much water could it hold? In the monsoon, the playful rain had started causing havoc at unexpected times, submerging the entire village. What happened to all that water? As usual, there would be a water crisis later. They had built buildings where the lake overflowed. People said it was the share of those in politics. The torment of anticipating a disaster rather than facing it felt terrible to her.

***

Channaveera, who was sitting on the outer veranda leaning against the wall, finished his eighth-grade math homework and stretched his legs, intending to relax. Flies buzzed around the empty chitranna plate and tea glass next to him. There wasn't a single leaf on the silk cotton tree on the path leading down to the lake from the courtyard. He had recently learned why the villagers called them 'Boorlamanevaru' (people of the silk cotton tree house). The giant tree, which he and his sister couldn't embrace even if they joined hands, soared high into the sky. In its prime, the tree, covered with large, crimson flowers, gave the impression of being on fire. Hundreds of unknown birds would flock to the tree, teaching the red color to sing, and shedding flowers. Then, when the pods dried and burst, countless white cotton balls would float away like swans, with a black seed as their charioteer, drifting far and wide for many days. The fascination of watching this never faded since he was old enough to understand. For the past couple of years, the tree hadn't been as expansive as before. Two of its branches on this side had broken and fallen. "Channaveera... you just sit there, go get two pieces of firewood from the backyard. How much can I do alone here?" Hearing the voice, he got up, went to the backyard from the side of the house, carried four pieces of firewood, and came to the kitchen.

"Are you studying and writing, or just wasting time?" Turning to his mother, who was blowing on the stove, he retorted, "Wasn't I doing that just now?" "If you study a little, you might become something, my son... that's the only path I see..." She didn't have the patience to listen to her good intentions.

As summer approached, she couldn't sleep at night. Women would gather near the well before dawn. This was the only well for the six houses on this side. The well would fill with water during the night's rest. At first, the pots of those who drew water would fill up. Later, there wasn't enough water to submerge the pot, so they would only draw half a pot, which was a struggle. By the time they filled the large pots and vessels in the house, her arms would be exhausted. During other times, even though they were close neighbors, they became impatient rivals during the well's commotion! Harsh words and taunts would slip out. Pushing someone else's pot to establish one's turn, stealthily lifting it and placing it back in its original spot, grumbling that the urgency wasn't just for them and that they didn't have servants at home either, was common.
But today, the same well didn't let them quarrel. Shashikala's husband worked in the Panchayat office. She had shared the definite news that orders had already come from the state government and the district collector's level, which pushed the daily water problem to the side. "Do you think Parashuramappa will do nothing and remain silent? He also has land and four houses, doesn't he?" When Kashibai expressed this ray of hope, Shashikala said that this had come from above, even surpassing the chairman and MLA. Thus, for a while, silence prevailed there, except for the clatter of pots and the sound of water overflowing. Varadamma also realized that she was unnecessarily snapping at her children. But this was not in her control. She couldn't discuss it with the children. Some anxieties couldn't be shared. Sharing an anxiety that she herself wasn't clear about with helpless people would only double her anxiety by making them fearful, with no other benefit. But suppressing it would tear her apart from within!

The next morning, near the well, everyone put their pots aside and shared the information they had. It became clear that the men were repeatedly discussing and forming a plan for a protest.

When Varadamma, who had no husband, asked if she would be included in the protest if she came, there was a stir. A decision was made that if the affected men and women went together, the pressure on the government would be effective. They divided among themselves the task of contacting the remaining households in the village. In two or three days, the men and women of the village collectively discussed and, under the leadership of Shashikala's husband, met Parashuramappa, declaring that he should lead them and that they were all ready to accompany him to the taluk and district levels, thus forming a rebellion.

***

When the villagers recount history, they say that the lake was built by some chieftain. The lake, built according to a plan, had a large catchment area. The lakes in the upper villages were connected like a chain, and the excess water flowing from their overflows would join one another and then flow into this lake, from where a large canal led further. There were sluices to irrigate hundreds of acres of land downstream, and a waterman to manage them. The water in the lake, which was four feet deep in the middle, was used not only for agriculture but also by the villagers for bathing and washing clothes. A specific spot was arranged for drawing drinking water. Over time, as agricultural land expanded in the upper villages and canals narrowed, the catchment area shrank. Fertilized water from fields and sewage water flowed unchecked into the lake, causing lush growth of reeds.

By the time Parashuramappa dropped out of college and entered politics, encroachments on the lake had already begun. He made a fuss about the encroachments, and those behind the encroachments became his followers, gaining him both fame and power. When Somesh, an ambitious young man, and his friends resisted the person who bought land downstream of the overflow and started releasing water whenever he wanted, group politics grew, and Parashuramappa was elected as a Panchayat member in the next election. Parashuramappa's group filled the canal coming from the neighboring village with silt and built and sold houses. When water flooded the streets during the monsoon, Somesh's group, who had fiercely protested, encroached on another part of the lake and planted a garden. The struggle between the groups only increased their strength and animosity, but it brought no benefit to the village. During heavy monsoons, water overflowing from fields and through the streets would carry silt and piles of debris into the lake. In four or five years, the lake water became so polluted that it was unfit even for bathing, let alone drinking. Sheds and cattle sheds built over the canals with sluices obstructed irrigation of the fields. Moreover, the heavily silted lake began to dry up at the beginning of winter. People started relying on old wells for drinking water. Due to connections with city businessmen, a craze for drilling borewells began. The harsh sound of huge engines drilling into the earth could be heard for kilometers, and the villagers, unable to close their eyes during their afternoon rest, would curse. Children, forgetting their school homework, would watch with wide eyes as one borewell after another was sunk into the ground and disappeared. News would come of people scratching their heads and stopping work, cursing, because no water came out no matter how much they drilled. Somewhere in between, shouts of "one inch of water came, two inches of water came" could also be heard. Many borewells that remained dry and abandoned, or dried up within a year of yielding water, still stand around like open-mouthed tunnels of death. One night, Parashuramappa's followers, enraged that Somesh's followers had confronted them in his coconut grove, pushed his pet dog into the mouth of a half-drilled borewell, sending it to the underworld. When Parashuramappa's side got a hint, they vowed to show the opponents by putting their children in borewells and asked them to wait. It seemed guaranteed to the villagers that bloodshed would occur sooner or later. The year Parashuramappa became Panchayat chairman for the second time, it rained continuously for three days during Ganesh Chaturthi. In just two hours, water entered everyone's houses in the village, making it impossible to distinguish between the lake and the village. Who had time to listen to the cries of cattle, sheep, and chickens when people were scrambling to save their lives by climbing onto terraces, roofs, trees, and any high ground they could find? The people had spent the night without sleep, food, or water, in fear of the flood, before government aid arrived. Water had entered Varadamma's veranda. They had spent the night sitting in the slightly elevated kitchen, listening to the roar of the water splashing against the veranda beyond their knees, fearing that it could enter at any moment. The next day, they found shelter in a gruel center. But a month later, when representatives came and wrote that their house had not suffered such damage, they did not receive compensation. "We will see, we will talk to them. Two sheep, four chickens, isn't that right, Varadamma?" Parashuramappa promised. He kept repeating the same reassurance occasionally. It was just a flood like the sea. In three months, the lake bottom was visible! The struggle for water began! The next year's festival came. And then the flood came again!

One day, a jeep with the Ashoka emblem arrived, and three people got out near the lake. They wore helmets and held metal-like instruments in their hands. All day long, they moved around the lake and many parts of the village, digging up soil, putting it in bags, and taking notes. "They are from the Geological Survey Department. They are doing some tests," Somesh had informed. "Since it's a government department, they must be doing related work," Parashuramappa said, implying that it wasn't important. In the next meeting, he had put forward a proposal. "All the water from the fields and the village rushes into the lake at once, and during heavy rains, floods enter the houses. We should build a long embankment where the water enters the lake so that the water flows away from outside. That way, the water won't overflow all at once." Somesh's group resisted this. In the summer, Parashuramappa had the embankment built at his own expense. Somesh's group, suspecting that Parashuramappa had some hidden benefit in this, broke the drain on the other side, allowing the rainwater to flow into the lake. That year, there was no flood damage due to less rain. But in the untimely rains of the next year, the embankment broke, and the village was submerged by water rushing in from all sides. A rumor spread that Somesh's people had broken the embankment. In this vengeful quarrel, Somesh got a whiff of Parashuramappa's grand plan. "Oh, you cunning fox! I didn't even realize it!" he gritted his teeth, and a different plan formed in his mind. He conveyed to Parashuramappa his decision that he wanted an equal share in this. This turned into a fierce rivalry, reaching a point where an open fight could break out at any moment.

The Geological Survey people had come and gone twice more. News had been circulating for a year that this place had some importance and that the government would acquire it. But no one had a clear idea of its scope or method. "Oh, forget it! How many such projects come and go? How many actually materialize?" With this attitude, two years had passed. Parashuramappa had gained more clarity on his own project. Varadamma's daughter had entered tenth grade, and her son had moved to eighth grade.

***

Parashuramappa was pleased that the men and women of the village had come seeking his leadership. He too was restless, hoping to find a way. The lake was filled with silt, and its bottom was almost at the level of the bank. No one relied on it much anymore. He had subtly hinted at this to those in power. But now, all this was unexpected! Surprisingly, his bitter rival Somesh had also come forward to take co-leadership! He too was being harmed by all these developments.
Parashuramappa and Somesh revealed the sudden calamity that had befallen the village, the uncertain and helpless state of mind the villagers were experiencing because of it, and the shock and pain they themselves had suffered. "It's terribly painful. This is the land where we have lived through generations of joy and sorrow. Are we to become orphans in our own land now? If someone comes and tells us to give it up, will those above just nod their heads in agreement? Don't they have any human empathy or conscience?" they lashed out passionately.

"How much land are they asking for... will it include fields and houses?" someone raised a doubt.

"How much land? The entire village, not just fields and houses, but even the lake, my friend! For now, they've asked up to the border of the neighboring village!" At that, everyone understood the gravity of the situation. "If they suddenly tell us to leave, where do we go? Grandfathers, great-grandfathers... don't the people who have lived here for so many generations have any rights?" Amidst the gathered crowd, criticism and counter-criticism led to an uproar. Parashuramappa raised his hand to silence them.

"A rare mineral deposit has been discovered in this area of our village. It's not found everywhere in the world. It's very important in electronic technology and military weaponry. That's why the government is working fast. We should consider it our misfortune; it's difficult to escape," Parashuramappa said, looking at the people's faces for the effect of seriousness, and then fell silent.

"But how can we just sit idly by? Haven't people in other states protested and made laws reverse? We are ready to come with you to the taluk, district, wherever you need. You must do something," one person said, and everyone echoed in unison. The two leaders needed something like this.

With the permission of the concerned authorities, a delegation of a hundred people staged a sit-in protest in the ground next to the taluk office. After shouting slogans for two hours, the two leaders received a call from the MLA. They informed the people and both headed towards the MLA's office. The MLA, who called them in, saw a situation where a snake and a mongoose were together, and though he felt like laughing, he didn't show it. They explained the danger that had befallen the village and earnestly requested him to stop it somehow, adding, "The people are angry about what the MLAs and MPs they elected are doing." The slightly irritated MLA chuckled, "You were turning the lake into plots... it wouldn't have remained like that anyway..." "How can you say that, sir? I talked to you, didn't I... was I betraying you?" he pleaded in a trustworthy voice. The MLA became serious.

"Look, this is a village in my constituency with my supporters. Am I happy? But this is a very rare mineral. No country will compromise on it. Here, it has been given great importance. Preparations for the Gazette notification have already been made. No one can do anything," he expressed his helplessness and came with them to the protest site.

"I have told Parashuramappa and Somesh about it. You are the people of my constituency. Don't worry. I will try my best for your welfare in every way. Stop the protest," he said, joining hands.

***

Channaveera was standing on the outer veranda. His annual exams had finished the day before. "Are you standing there looking at the tree again? Get ready, who told you to go and grind flour...?" Varadamma, who was alerting him, also inadvertently looked towards the silk cotton tree. The branches of the leafless tree were full of red flowers! Disturbed by a slight pain in her gut, her gaze swept over the dried-up lake and the distant fields, then returned to her feet. News came daily. People had almost accepted the situation of leaving the village. The topic of compensation would come up for discussion wherever four people gathered – near the well, near the temple, near the aralikat. Parashuramappa and Somesh brought news almost daily. "Our MLA is pressuring the higher authorities to provide jobs to locals," they would say one time, and then another time, news would spread that a large amount of compensation would be given. "If it's yours, let it go, it's just one acre. Some people will lose ten or twenty acres..." Parashuramappa said. Wasn't that the only support for a one-legged cripple? Wasn't breaking that a big deal? She fell into anxiety. It also spread that government jobs would be given to those who had completed tenth grade and degrees. People moved around, listening to all this in an uncertain state of mind.

The May sun was scorching fiercely. Chennaveera had passed. Kusuma had scored ninety percent in tenth grade. Parashuramappa brought a written circular, as if delivering the final verdict. There was no doubt left. Twenty lakhs per acre for the fields. A committee would soon come to calculate compensation for the houses. There was also an option to provide plots in a taluk town thirty kilometers away for those who wished. An NGO, collaborating with the government, offered to build affordable houses on those plots and deduct fifteen lakhs from the compensation as another option. Many people took the full amount. Varadamma, along with her own opinion, chose the government-provided plot, even if small, and the house they would build, which would at least have water and toilet facilities, rather than the risk of going to another town with underage children to find a house and deal with rent deposits. She wrote it down. As the committee members went down with the papers, everyone noticed that the heat was still intense. A cloud was rising from one corner of the sky. In half an hour, suddenly, a strong wind blew. Dust and garbage rose and flew, and the atmosphere became gloomy. Countless cotton balls rose from the silk cotton tree, filled with dried black pods, covering the sky like white clouds, and gradually began to travel in all directions.

They had been informed that they would have to vacate in January if not December. Some had left their fields fallow, while others had cultivated them, saying they would see. Varadamma had also sown her field, thinking it would come if it came. The rain was moderate. Fortunately, there was no flood later. Compensation was paid in January. In February, a call came to hand over the newly built houses.

***

Houses built in rows on a twenty-by-thirty plot. A small hall, two narrow rooms. Water was supplied twice a day for an hour. It was a layout built on government land outside the village. Arranging their belongings and getting to a certain stage felt like winning a war. The rice and provisions they had brought from the village ran out in a week. She had deposited the remaining five lakhs of compensation in a nearby cooperative bank. She took some out and bought enough groceries and rice for the month. In their old house, the vegetables grown in the backyard were enough. Here, even coriander leaves had to be bought. In the uncertainty of when they would have to vacate, both children had not been admitted to the next class. Chennaveera would go out to wander in the afternoon and return in the evening. Kusuma had not yet made any acquaintances there. When everyone was at home, it felt suffocating, as if some pressure was constant. Varadamma watched with anxiety as the money in the bank dwindled month by month. As another month passed, the truth stood before her: if this continued, everything would be depleted, not grow, and she couldn't find a source of income. As she stood at the door today, restless as usual, something struck her. There was a large ground a little distance from the layout. There were a couple of permanent shops selling vegetables. Otherwise, there were children playing and nomads passing through. Once a week, a market would gather. On that day, the entire ground would be filled with vendors bringing goods from all over. Beyond the ground was a city council office. There was constant movement of people going to and from the office. The next day, after moving around there from morning till afternoon, she gained some courage. Deciding that nothing would be known without trying, she talked to her children and withdrew some money from the bank. After moving around and pleading at the city council office, she got permission. It cost more to put up a sheet and cover it with another sheet in the line of vegetable shops, and then place six cheap plastic chairs inside. The inauguration of the eatery took place in front of the two or three people there, besides the vegetable vendors, with rice, sambar, and buttermilk prepared at home. On the first day, rice was left over. In another two days, the neighbors found out, and the number of customers increased. On market day, everything ran short, beyond estimation! At the end of the month, when she counted, the expenses were more than the income, and she collapsed in despair. On Kusuma's advice, she moved around the city council office, promoting her eatery and pleading with the employees and customers who came and went there. She received a lot of information and advice. With her daughter's help, she started making chapatis and jowar rotis along with rice early in the morning and made them available at the eatery. The number of people coming for meals grew. On market day, it was a big business. At the end of that month, for the first time, she deposited some money into her bank account. Another month was ending. Chennaveera was sitting near the outer door, talking to his sister. Something floated down towards them and landed in the three-foot courtyard. It wasn't silk cotton; it looked like a parachute, with a black seed hanging at the bottom. He watched in wonder as it landed at the base of the spinach plant.
After ten o'clock at night, Varadamma closed the eatery, carried the bundle of rotis and rice home, and after everyone had eaten, she said, "Kusuma, go to college tomorrow and inquire about admission. Let's admit Chennaveera to the government high school. Keep all the mark sheets ready." She grumbled with a sigh, "Both of them lost a year!"l
