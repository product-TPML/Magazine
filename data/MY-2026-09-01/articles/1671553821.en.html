We had gone to a wedding reception for a friend's daughter. Just like all hurried receptions these days, we stood in a line, wished the bride and groom, and when the videographer, who was the mastermind of the entire wedding, told us to move, we laughed. Even though he didn't explicitly say "move aside," he gestured with his hand as if to say "move forward." When we reached the corner of the stage, the bride and groom's parents immediately told us to eat, so we headed for dinner. Even though we ate the same puri and sagu, my husband not only declared, "The food was first class!" but also inquired about the cook's whereabouts and their entire history. As soon as he heard the cook's name, he exclaimed, "Oh! They are from our town, so how can the food not be super?" and even gave a certificate. Just as sambar and rasam powders have become famous brands, the thought that cooking and cooks have also become a brand made my mind fall into the world of cooking.

The whole world seems to have fallen into the food bowl, with hotels, darshinis, and cafes everywhere you look! YouTube is full of food vlogs. If a new hotel opens in a neighborhood and you haven't visited it yet, it feels like life is wasted.
Just recently, when the news of the biggest hotel in Bangalore opening spread across everyone's mobiles, someone in our WhatsApp group complained that they went there and struggled to find a place. Immediately, the other half of the people started sharing their hotel experiences. I wondered if we had stopped cooking at home altogether! Is cooking a skill or a struggle? Now, it seems no dish has retained its original form and taste. The idli, which used to appear soft and cotton-white on the plate, is now covered with all sorts of powders, falling victim to the "new" creative idea. Dosas have their own Ashtottara. If a good lecture on YouTube gets a hundred or two hundred views, a vlog on making ordinary rasam gets thousands of viewers in hours. How this eating game started at this level is a PhD topic in itself!

It was the beginning of my marriage. Days when I was like a fly caught in the sugary syrup of the illusion of making new dishes and earning my husband's praise. Born and raised in a garden house by a heavenly riverside, I came to Bangalore and settled in a nest called a 1BHK. For someone who spent her days anxious that a loud cough would be heard by the neighbors, cooking was a full-time job. It was a time when I wasn't involved in any other job or activity, and the only thought I had was to make new dishes and earn praise. Among the clothes I carried in my suitcase when I left my parents' house, Kadambila Saraswati's cookbook, carefully given by my mother, must have been sitting with anxiety and curiosity, just like me! Kadambila Saraswati, from rasam and chutney to dishes that only experienced cooks can make, and soft, delicate, authentic South Canara bele holige, was the cooking mother who taught us to cook. About three to three and a half decades ago, when married women left their parents' house, their bags probably never left without Kadambila Saraswati's cookbook! To that extent, it was a cookbook that was a cooking anthem. Thus, following Kadambila Saraswati's instructions, using a pavu seer and my mother's eye-measurement, I was turning to a new life by cooking. My husband, who would finish everything served in an instant and eat heartily, would eat in such a way that I would definitely fall into the illusion that I was Nala's daughter. The balloon of my daily pride was pricked by an accidental guest. As he ate, he said, "The rasam is good," emptying the bowl. Seeing me smile proudly, he said, "If there's asafoetida and coconut, even a monkey can cook wonderfully, isn't that right, dear?" and took another serving of rasam. While I was shrinking with anger and embarrassment, my husband was in the final stage of his meal, pouring rasam into a glass and drinking it. From then on, in this kitchen war of mine, I am always Arjuna, ready to lay down my weapons and run!

Isn't the history of cooking the history of man himself! The one who started living by eating raw food when hungry, then discovered fire and learned to cook food over it, continued to learn in the days that followed. He kept experimenting without critically asking, "Is this needed or not?" He categorized foods essential for the body. Along with that, he developed a special emotional bond called "mother's home cooking." Man fell into the whirlpool of food to the extent that this game called cooking determined the way to win hearts. But man, in the intoxication of pleasure, failed to notice the biggest change: that food, which was only for hunger, later transformed into an object of pleasure, comfort, and show.

Thus, cooking is now on our plates, a concoction of the nine rasas and eight directions. Truly, the whole world is contained in it now. For our mothers' generation, cooking was a bond of love. It was the thread that held the family together. Among my mother's relatives, my grandmother was a clever cook. When we went to grandmother's house for holidays, if we had two items like rasam and palya for lunch, sometimes by evening, it would suddenly become sambar. When we, as small children, ate it without knowing, our uncle, who was eating with us, would reveal grandmother's secret. He would add a little leftover palya to a little leftover rasam, give it a tempering, and serve it, making it fragrant. Uncle would jokingly say, "Come, come everyone, mother's special sambar is ready," embarrassing grandmother. But that night's sambar was so delicious that we felt as satisfied as if we were eating nectar. That was grandmother's touch. Even today, perhaps no one has achieved the perfect consistency of the pickle grandmother used to make. My favorite snack was the tempering dosa my mother made. After grinding coconut and rice finely, the next day she would add a thick tempering of mustard and red chilies and pour it into a small pan. That dosa didn't even need chutney to go with it. The way my mother would slowly and delicately lift the edges of the dosa, without disturbing its round shape, is beyond my current hurried thought of taking out dosas and putting them in a box.

My sister-in-law, who is of my generation, is an amazing cook. Even with very few ingredients, she prepares something as if by magic, and by mealtime, she has cooked at least two or three varieties. Once, when she visited us, I generously said, "Sister-in-law, just rest for two days," and gave her a collection of stories by my favorite authors to read. After a while, when I came out, I saw her asleep with the book in her hand. When I woke her up, she laughed and said, "If I sit like this, I'll go crazy, I'll make something special." It's a truth that cooking is her expression of love, and it's an equally big truth that she doesn't approve of anyone else's cooking!

When I got married, I joined a joint family. For me, from a small family, the number of dosas made daily at my husband's house was equivalent to a week's worth of dosas at my parents' house. It was a time when cooking was done on a wood-fired stove. In farming families, gobar gas stoves, which were easy to cook on, had also started. For rice and other dishes, the wood-fired stove was used, and the gobar gas stove, which was a single stove and convenient to move around, was used for making dosas. By 7:15 in the morning, my mother-in-law would sit down for this ritual of making dosas. For my mother-in-law, who made dosas slowly and systematically, cooking was like her right.

At my husband's house, for breakfast, there were a variety of dosas. You name a vegetable or a grain, and I would have eaten its dosa there. At the beginning of the dosa-eating session, those who had to catch the 7:30 bus would go first, followed by the elder brother. In this way, one by one, they would eat hot dosas and leave. If anyone ate even one more dosa than the usual daily amount, my mother-in-law's round face would widen with joy. She would be sad to see me, who was embarrassed to eat, like an unwanted animal. My mother-in-law, who used to say, "It doesn't even have a mouth to ask," might have realized after a few years that it has a very sharp tongue! In this game of eating dosas, the children and men of the house would eat up to ten or fifteen dosas, which were made as thin as paper.

We have had the practice of soaking whole wheat grains, grinding them, and making dosas for a long time. Without adding too much water, they would grind the batter thickly until it was a fine, gritty rava-like consistency, meaning the batter should be firm enough to hold in the fist for making dosas. Then, they would spread it on a hot griddle with their bare hands. When my mother-in-law, elder sister-in-law, and other sisters-in-law made dosas this way, my heart would be in my mouth. I was amazed at their skill in making dosas without touching the hot edge of the griddle, and also angry at the difficulty they endured. I used to think, "Would anyone intentionally expose their hand to a hot griddle like this?" But for them, who were used to it, making dosas this way never seemed difficult. No cooking process was ever difficult for them. Especially my brother-in-law's wife, even if you woke her up in the middle of the night and asked her to cook fresh food, she would do it with the same enthusiasm, without any annoyance, and if necessary, even grind it in a stone mortar and serve it. She is such a kind-hearted person.

Thus, in our large family, cooking is a very important ritual. Moreover, I have grown up seeing that the joy of serving what has been cooked is even greater. About twenty years ago, in our village, there was a daily custom of setting plates according to the time of the last bus arriving in our village. It was a tradition to wait for anyone, whether close or distant relatives, who would arrive by the last bus, and then, after considering the time it would take for them to reach home, ask them to eat. For me, who grew up seeing the practice of cooking extra food, my mind is now stunned when I put rice on the plate as if weighing it. What pleasure has this small family given? What joy have I truly lost! So, when I go to the village, when I cook a lot of food for the helpers who come for farm work, I often experience a divine feeling. I had told this to my friend and was scolded. "Yes, you only have to cook like this six or seven times a year, but if you had to cook a lot every day like me, no divine feeling would arise." So, are we, our generation, truly not experiencing the sense of fulfillment that our grandmothers and mothers felt in this entire process of cooking and serving food? Or have we ever thought of cooking and serving as a feeling that brings fulfillment? Is it just a thought from our mothers' generation!

Once, when I was traveling in North Karnataka, we went to a friend's house for dinner. As we were leaving after dinner, his mother placed a small bundle in my hand. When I asked, "What is this, mother?" she said, "It's a packed lunch, I've kept ten jowar rotis and palya." "Oh, why did you take so much trouble, mother? We don't need it, we'll eat something on the way," I said formally, and she was upset. For her, who believed that cooking and packing food was her duty, refusing it was like refusing her intimacy. Cooking was a reason that connected a life to the world.

'Ratatouille' is an animated film. In the film, a mouse named 'Eli' is a skilled chef. Separated from his family, Eli comes to another city and joins a restaurant there, helping a young man named Linguini who can't cook. There's a scene where Linguini has to cook without Eli's help, and he throws the ingredients needed for the dish into the pan, making it feel like I'm throwing them myself. Linguini's struggle is often mine too. I belong to the category of ordinary cooks. I don't have much interest, I just cook what's necessary to eat. But according to my daughter, I'm an excellent chef. The rasam, masala dosa, pulao I make, the coffee I mix, everything is super
super! My family and friends have also said this! I have felt thrilled inside hearing this! But truly, only God knows the secret inside! It becomes delicious without my knowing. It's a dish that happens beyond my control. Like Linguini, I haphazardly put a little of this ingredient, a little of that ingredient into the pan without much thought, and when a dish is ready, I say "Aham Brahmasmi" and taste it from my palm with a spoon as if offering it to God.

I sing, 'You are in me, and I am in you, Hari,' and offer it to Krishna. Therefore, I now firmly believe that cooking is a magic that happens in that moment. All chefs have made this true over these many years.

Even chefs who cook for thousands of people sometimes make mistakes. We have often eaten food that was either too salty to eat or so spicy that it made us drink water. Sometimes, when the food is so delicious that I don't even want to wash my hands, my husband goes looking for the cook and says, "The food was very good, wasn't it?" Once, remembering the extremely crispy vada I ate at a hotel on Mysore Road, I went there again when I passed by and ate vada, but the vada was full of green chilies. After eating so many chilies with each bite, I went straight to the manager and angrily told him, "Did your cook put chilies in the batter or batter in the chilies?" As I wiped the Kaveri flowing profusely from my eyes and nose and got into the car, my husband was terribly happy and laughed under his mustache as usual. If it's the same hotel, the same cook, and yet the calculations go wrong, then isn't my theory that it "happens" correct? Furthermore, perhaps both the loss of balance and the attainment of balance in cooking are games of destiny, which is also an interpretation of my overthinking influenced by the Bhakti movement.

I have found hundreds of instances to prove that cooking is a magic that happens. Even renowned chefs sometimes lose their touch. For that touch to happen, along with experience, salt, sourness, and spice, the vessel in which it is cooked also plays a crucial role. The fire placed at the bottom of a clean, untarnished, and unburnt vessel must also cooperate. It should not burn fiercely or too slowly, but spread evenly across the bottom of the vessel. The intensity of the fire's heat must be adjusted intermittently. Purandaradasa has woven a beautiful kirtana using the metaphor of cooking. It constantly reminds us, "Cook with experience / And let the experienced ones come and gather." In this kirtana, the Dasaru first says that the dirty vessels must be washed. Then, pure water must be placed in them, and the fire of knowledge must be lit. For it to be cooked perfectly, there must be complete attention, he says. That is, for the dish to be perfect, with complete devotion, without the mind and eyes wandering for a moment, it will be perfect only when one is stirring it. To make it perfect is, in a sense, meditation. To look at it. To look at it as it is. A moment's inattention can ruin the cooking. Thus, when we become one with it, Nalapaka happens! Isn't that right! To become Nalapaka, a person must hide their existence like Nala and become merely a cook. They must cook with the food. They must be cooked and become perfect. Thus, if men, from Nala to Bhimasena, who have the ability to cook for thousands of people, only sit in front of their plates like guests and eat...? Ha! This is the biggest problem for today's working women. If the men of the house would at least help with the extra work that working women, both inside and outside the home, do... they would set fire to heaven, my dears, just watch! Although many homes have improved, a large number of men still only come and sit in front of their plates. When hunger, regardless of gender, is a universal law, learning and teaching the work of preparing it without any discrimination is also a part of evolution.

Thus, all these stages of cooking, washing, cutting, boiling, sautéing, and simmering to perfection, in a sense, appear to be our very lives. In recent years, they also appear to be a reflection of life's journey. Like sautéing failures and frustrations in a pan, like chopping onions quickly, protecting fingers from sharp knife blades, sometimes with eyes closed, filled with tears, and sometimes shedding tears, through stages that cannot be avoided, and moments of happiness that feel like fleeting flavors of life, like the aroma of ghee wafting through the nose when a sweet dish is perfectly cooked. Thus, the act of cooking, which has been going on for three decades, now feels like an Agnihotra at this moment. Sometimes as an inseparable part of a tradition, sometimes as a habit, most often
as a necessity, again as a need, and once again, very rarely, like meditation...l
