This year, the rain god is angry. He is peeking to see whether to come or not, troubling and confusing people. On the day he gives a holiday to schools, he himself takes a full holiday and sends the sun to earth saying, 'Go and heat up the (head) a bit.' He has pushed farmers into anxiety... While people grumble like this, I get lost in the memories of my childhood monsoon, my village's farmer friends who made that monsoon a beautiful poem, our village river overflowing, the crops, and the greenery.

Even a month before the monsoon begins, the 'rain-catching preparation' of my village's farming community, that is, the monsoon preparation, starts.

The drains and main channels of the areca nut garden are cleaned to allow water to flow smoothly without stagnation during the monsoon. Workers in everyone's garden are busy with these preparations. The dried leaves of areca nut trees, fallen banana tree waste, etc., are all neatly removed and covered at the base of the areca nut trees to protect the soil from erosion. Nowadays, old torn sarees are being used for this work. The base of the areca nut trees covered with sarees looks colorful. If one or two rains fall, then the spraying of medicine (a mixture of copper sulfate and lime) on the areca nuts begins. Arranging the necessary materials for this, bringing brave people who can climb trees by pleading with them, and begging them, 'Hey, don't let the rot come, spray the medicine quickly,' starts along with the rain.

The abundant monsoon of the 70s-80s in Malnad was a wonderful 'annual festival'. Dark, overcast rainy days, and continuous heavy rain throughout the night. School was a mere formality. Whether one went or not didn't matter.

Primary school children didn't have umbrellas. Instead, they were given a warm blanket cover called 'kambali koppe', artistically folded and secured with an iron rod to prevent it from unfolding, allowing them to cover their entire body and go. Hiding inside it and splashing water as they walked towards school was an inexpressible moment of joy for the schoolchildren. When children joined high school, they were promoted from this blanket to an umbrella and a raincoat.

When they arrived at school, bamboo racks were set up here and there to hang these 'kambali koppes'. In the evening, when classes ended and they left for home, those blankets would have dried on their own and wouldn't be heavy like wet, damp blankets. Our 'kambali koppes' would happily head home. Even after going home, these 'kambali koppes' were kept on racks above the fireplace to dry. Sitting around the fire in the fireplace, chatting with the children, roasting and eating jackfruit seeds, papads, and cashew nuts was a fun pastime during the monsoon. No one wanted to miss this. Even adults would pretend to scold the children and then join the fire gathering, drinking herbal concoctions.

It was during these heavy rains in the evening that our house servant, Dyava, a young man, would narrate ghost stories with acting, making us all tremble. 'It was raining just like today, my dear sirs, that day. It was pouring like this. I was coming alone with fresh grass on my head. Right in front of me, about a meter away, a water goddess stood from the road to the sky. White saree, disheveled hair, body and face soaked and drenched in the rain. My hands and feet wouldn't move. Yet, I gathered courage and prayed, 'Mother water goddess, this time on the day of the Aridra rain festival, we will offer you special milk and huggi (a sweet dish) puja. I will set aside fruits and nuts today itself. Please allow me to pass.' After that, the tall water goddess allowed me to move forward...' Such stories were only scary to hear during the monsoon. Because we had to come from school in that rainy darkness, right? If the water goddess stood like that on the way to school, she might let us go back home. But if this water goddess blocked our way while coming from school, we couldn't go back to school. So, we would cover ourselves with all the 'kambali koppes' and walk home quickly with extreme caution.

Meanwhile, when heavy rain came, my father would ask teasing questions like, 'Have you seen, children, 'mud bears' and 'plant pigs' come?' A 'mud bear' is not any bear. When mud mixes (turbid) in the river, it becomes a red river, that is 'mud bear'. When plants sway in the wind, that is 'plant pig'. Such were the storytelling sessions. It was a grand monsoon without any other entertainment facilities. No one had any concern for time. After it got very dark, we would light a lamp in the veranda, light castor oil lamps everywhere, serve food to the servants, and then we would eat. If we slept at night, there would be a heavy downpour on the tiles. For children sleeping on the attic, there was a change during the monsoon to sleep downstairs in the hall next to the prayer room. To avoid the splashing of water from the tiles above, and children getting scared by thunder and lightning, they were made to sleep in the dark room inside. The special sound of rain cockroaches during the monsoon, even now, wherever I hear it, reminds me of the days when I would sleep warmly covered in that dark room, listening to the sound of rain cockroaches.

The lifeblood of the successful farmers of Malnad back then were the garden workers who considered their work on the land as service to Mother Earth. The mistresses of the house loved them like their own children. It was a collective farming family. In our evening fire-warming sessions, Dyava, the house servant, had the freedom to discuss and advise on all the happenings of the family.

Dyava, who had no world of his own like a wife, children, or home, considered our house his own. When we sat by the fire during the monsoon, he would entertain us with stories, riddles, and folk songs. Then, after a while, he would command, "Now, everyone, take out your books and read." After coming home from school, if the elder brothers sat idly, he would scold them, "Father is doing so much gardening, can't you go and look around? You sit like grandsons of Bajirao." He would be humble only with my father and mother, not daring to order them.

The women of our house would follow Dyavanna, Dyavappayya, seeking his guidance for all their work. Every Wednesday was the Siddapur market. On that day, Dyava would go to the market and return with watermelons, candy, and vegetables, saying that the oil god had entered his stomach. That night, he wouldn't eat dinner, just drink buttermilk and sleep. But as soon as he got off the bus, he would start philosophizing. He would say, "These Hegde children have become spoiled without working."

He had a stomach ache. When he groaned, 'Gowramma, I'm going to die today,' my mother would make him a herbal concoction and scold him like a child, 'Didn't I tell you not to drink liquor? They say they make it with battery cells. Won't you get a stomach ache if you drink that burnt liquor?' She would then make two or three types of medicine for him.

In Malnad, there were small thefts like 'someone picked areca nuts from the neighbor's garden' or 'someone took fallen coconuts from this house', but there were no professional robbers. Even now, there aren't. However, news slowly spread that two boys who failed SSLC and ran away somewhere, then returned, had formed a gang of thieves with some local boys. News spread that the village shop was robbed and the temple hundi (collection box) was stolen. There was a slight fear among those who walked to Yakshagana at night, and the boys who went to guard the tall bamboo houses in the fields, beating gongs to keep pigs away. My father had told our Dyava, who would go to his relatives' village once a month to eat and drink his favorite food, 'You can sleep there tonight and come back tomorrow morning, my dear, they say a gang of thieves is roaming around.' On the days Dyava wasn't there, our fireplace session would be very boring. We would only tremble with the imagined fear of thieves, without the experience of his ghost stories, thief stories, and other trembling stories by the fire.

One day, our master Dyava suddenly disappeared from our house. His office, clothes, everything on the attic of the washroom was empty. My father joked that Dyava, who was bored of eating lentil curry, might have gone to eat chicken curry and fallen ill with a stomach ache, but my mother cried. The other two boys who used to sleep on the attic started sleeping in the veranda inside the house, scared without Dyavanna. At night, all 12 doors of the house had to be locked. My mother, scolding Dyava, saying, 'That blessed soul used to nag me to check if all the doors were bolted before sleeping, Gowramma,' would lock all the doors before sleeping.

Four or five months passed. There was no news whether Dyava was alive or dead. The monsoon ended, winter came, and the areca nut harvest was completed without Dyava. Meanwhile, an incident occurred where a gang of thieves, who had set up camp inside the adjacent forest and were sharing stolen goods, was caught, their legs broken, and handed over to the police. When the thieves were jailed, everyone in the village breathed a sigh of relief. Women and children started moving around bravely. The day before Diwali, Dyava appeared in our courtyard. He was thin and crooked. My mother, scolding him, 'We thought you were dead, you scoundrel,' gave him dosa and tea.

Even though my father scolded him for running away to some desolate place without telling anyone, everyone was so happy that Dyava had returned that they kept running in and out of the house. Davangere tobacco was also prepared. He had ordered the other two children to take out his bedding.

When they sat in front of the fireplace at night, the truth came out from Dyava's mouth. The gang of thieves would stop Dyava every day and tell him to unbolt the door of Hegde's house at night and sleep, and they wouldn't harm him. They also threatened to kill him if he revealed this. Dyava, who couldn't betray the house that fed him, had run away. Now he had returned and brought back the familiar Dyava to all of us. He had brought life to the stories by the fireplace.

For me, who came from such a Malnad house to the coast as a job seeker, Mangaluru, a commercial city pouring sweat, welcomed me. Unable to bear the heat here,

Like ice in Kashmir
Like dust in Ballari
Like fog in Madikeri
Sweat in Mangaluru

I would write poems and console myself. Only for a month or two. Here too, the glorious monsoon of the rainy season arrived.

I was astonished to see the difference between the warm monsoon of Malnad and the torrential, dark monsoon of the coast. The song of Kadengodlu Shankara Bhatta, which I had read in textbooks, began to come to mind.

What is the glory of the rainy season
in the Kannada land of the western coast?
What is the dull body of the dark cloud
whose water, however much poured, never ends?
The incessant rain that pours day and night,
The flashes of lightning like sticks.
The trickling drops of water
inside and outside homes and hearts.

As if this song was vividly brought before my eyes, the monsoon poured heavily. The monsoon that brought sadness to Muddaṇa's beloved Manorame, the monsoon that inspired Panje Mangesharaya with the enthusiasm of the southern wind's play... What kind of monsoon do you want? The sight of the coastal monsoon had deeply enchanted me.

The government college where I joined as a lecturer, a red stone building built by the British, more than 150 years old. It has a unique design that is not afraid of sun or rain. High windows. Rare large trees like Nagasampige in the college campus. Proper channels for rainwater to flow away.

Once inside the college campus in the morning, the roar of the monsoon, the chatter of students, the dim library, would create a transcendental rainy world. One day, I had a class in a dark room next to the garden. I went there. In the rainy darkness, I couldn't see the students. I called out attendance. I marked attendance based on the sound of 'Yes, Madam'. Before starting the lesson, I asked, 'Can you see me, children?' They said, 'No, Madam.' 'Can you hear me, at least?' I asked, and they chorused, 'Yes, Madam.' There was a silence as if the rain was about to fall heavily. As soon as I started the lesson... it started raining so heavily that it felt like the sky had fallen. When the rain poured in through the windows and doors, the girls climbed onto the benches, and the boys started dancing. Because 'Rain Holiday' had been declared. What else would college students think of doing when they got a whole day off but rain dance? Mentally, I joined their rain dance.

Just as there are minds that rejoice when it rains, aren't there also people who frown? 'Clothes never dry, you know,' 'Can't wear good sarees,' 'It's hard to walk around with this umbrella dangling, you know,' 'If it rains this much, we'll close the shop, people aren't coming at all...' Such various professional grumbles fall on our ears even if we don't want them to.

When the rain comes, why are you sitting inside, my dear?
Let's bathe with the earth.
Let's also watch the play of the clouds!!
