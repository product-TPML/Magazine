A Rewriting for a New Era

manjushree kadkola

A little from within the Mahabharata
–Haadupaadu Ramu

R. Tarini Shubhadayini

The Mahabharata and Ramayana are texts that live on in both the written and oral traditions of Kannada culture. Just as these two texts are endlessly broken down and rebuilt by other Indian minds, a process of breaking down and rebuilding has also occurred in Kannada. The Mahabharata written by Vyasa, which was originally of a certain size, has expanded three or four times, shrunk, and been published in various designs. Perhaps for this reason, scholars sometimes say that the Mahabharata has no original. It is said that it is difficult to determine what is original and what is interpolated in the Mahabharata. Similarly, scholars say that it is not wrong to call the Mahabharata a series of translations. Surprisingly, the narrative of the Mahabharata has come alive in re-narrations, simultaneously in both classical and folk traditions. This creative process should be seen as equal to that of Vyasa. The great Kannada poet Pampa, in his initial verses, refers to Vyasa's Bharata and proudly but humbly states his intention to rewrite it, saying, 'I shall cross the ocean of nectar-like words of the great sage Vyasa...' The tribal poet Saraladas of the Oriya language has rewritten the Mahabharata from his perspective. A poet like Kumaravyasa has rewritten the Mahabharata in writing, intertwining the oral tradition. All these types of rewritings demonstrate creativity through their adaptability. Moreover, they have established rewriting as a strength of indigenous languages. The prose of modern Kannada also followed this model of rewriting from the Kannada poetic tradition. Muddana's narration of the Ramayana not only conceived the Ramayana in prose but also encouraged a new kind of reading in indigenous societies. In the context of modern Kannada, such rewritings, adapting the Mahabharata into Kannada, continued; sometimes partially, sometimes entirely.

Among such great endeavors, Alasingacharya's 'Mahabharata' and A.R. Krishnashastry's 'Vachana Bharata' are excellent examples of rewriting the Mahabharata. H.S. Venkateshamurthy re-narrated Pampa's 'Vikramarjuna Vijaya' in simple Kannada, giving another meaning to Pampa's 'Tirul-Kannada', while Lakshmisha Tolpadi rewrote the story of the Mahabharata by intertwining old and new sensibilities. It is against this background that Ramu's re-narrated Mahabharata narrative should be viewed.

Ramu, who was always immersed in reading, was drawn to the Mahabharata by his immense interest in reading, which led to this new text. In a time when many transformations have occurred and are occurring in reading, writing, and sensibilities, Ramu's reading of the Mahabharata is surprising. Ramu reads the Mahabharata with the confidence that he can narrate the text, which is deeply embedded in tradition, in a way that suits him and this land, without making it a work dedicated to scholars. Rather than imagining readers and rewriting the text, Ramu's rewriting appears like a monologue, where he himself, as an ideal and sensitive reader, reads more and more, testing it on a touchstone. In Ramu's rewriting of the Mahabharata, the main interest is not so much to view it as a modern text, but to rewrite the Mahabharatas translated by his predecessors A.R. Krishnashastry and Alasingacharya, and Van Buitenen in English, in the language of our new era. By doing so, there is a small self-confidence that he is once again affirming the indigeneity of Kannada. Therefore, Ramu's narration of the Mahabharata comes in the form of an indigenous monologue.

If the Mahabharata is read according to different perspectives, a Mahabharata is created for each perspective. As Kumaravyasa says, 'For kings, it is valor; for Brahmins, it is the essence of the supreme Veda...' The sponge-like humanistic quality of the Mahabharata must be the reason why it appears differently to different perspectives. What is curious is that Ramu felt the need to read the Mahabharata for its humanistic quality. That is why he has read the incidents and characters of the Mahabharata in his own way. In doing so, he takes care to highlight the naturalness of those characters. From this perspective, the re-narration of the great Sanskrit epic in simple Kannada appears primarily as a Kannada sensibility. Why this statement is important is that the formalities and informalities in classical literature are constructs. Here, love, devotion, and natural human relationships all wear a veil of formality. Such classical formality sometimes becomes sticky, sometimes a chain of rituals. Ramu attempts to build an indigeneity that overcomes this in his work. Therefore, the Mahabharata he narrates highlights the sensitivity of human urges and moral dilemmas. In the incident of Bhishma Parva, the Pandavas go to Bhishma and plead, 'Grandfather, tell us the secret of your defeat and death, otherwise we will be destroyed.' Then Bhishma says, 'Oh, grandsons, I cannot fight with those who have laid down their weapons, who have fallen, whose armor and banner have been torn off, who are running away, women, those named after women, those close to death, and those who are the only son of their father' (p.125). Thus, Ramu makes it seem natural that Bhishma reveals his own weakness. Behind this is Bhishma's lament; it is the wisdom of this world. Similarly, 'Child, I am bound by the debt of Kaurava's salt. A man is a slave to money, money is no one's slave. Therefore, I spoke like a eunuch' (p.125).

The word 'Jolavaali' in Pampa once again appears in Kannada as 'uppina runa' (debt of salt) in the context of the native language. Such small hurts might acquire solemnity and become formal in a language like Sanskrit. As Rajendra Chenni says in his foreword, if we consider that the Mahabharata is a poem that honestly expresses the unresolved paradoxes, confusions, and dilemmas of the human world, then what Ramu sets out to do in his narration of the Mahabharata is to remove the veil from these human qualities and make their naturalness shine through.

Devanur Mahadeva has edited Ramu's Mahabharata notes. He explains the dilemmas that arise during the editing process using Ramu's work as a pretext. Here, Devanur primarily focuses on the words Ramu uses in his editing. Devanur does not approve of some words due to their social meanings. Such matters are explained in the back notes at the end of each chapter. This can be called another kind of re-reading (because scholars suggest that textual editing is another dimension of translation). Lakshmisha Tolpadi has called Devanur's work 'word service'. He says that if it is word service, then it goes without saying that it is also meaning service.

What Devanur wants is a Kannada that strongly supports the idea that the Mahabharata narrated by Ramu belongs to this land. Therefore, in his editorial scissors, he cuts off all the difficult Sanskrit branches. He calls Ramu's narrative Kannada 'Nelagannada' (native Kannada). Thus, following the trail of native Kannada, the work of adapting the Mahabharata to the Kannada sensibility has been done. However, in some places, when the question of culture arises, Devanur's editing also becomes dim. For example, in the episode of Shakuntala, when Shakuntala talks to Dushyanta about the superiority of women, she says, 'The husband enters the wife and is reborn through her... The child that comes out of her as a boy, saves all your ancestors who wander as ghosts' (p. 33). To this, Dushyanta says, '... If what you say is true, then Vishwamitra must be a cheat' (p. 34), 'Has he grown so tall / Today he must be of a low birth' (p. 34) – in such lines, one cannot help but feel that the language could have been different. Some words are specific to certain languages. Whether their use in another language is appropriate or not is determined by culture. If so, the question arises as to what culture is. It is difficult to answer this.

Regarding the Mahabharata, there have been many interpretations, from literal to ironic. Ramu's choice leans from literal interpretation towards the scope of suggestive meaning that transcends the literal. Therefore, for him, the inquiry into meaning takes precedence over verbal splendor. As the editor says, Ramu is not fully engaged in this work. Nevertheless, his craftsmanship cannot be overlooked. In short, it is evident that Ramu has tried to adapt the Mahabharata into Kannada. The source texts for Ramu are A.R. Krishnashastry's 'Vachanabharata', Alasingacharya's 'Mahabharata' (Srimaha Bharata is Karnataka Vachana), and Van Buitenen's translated Mahabharata ('The Mahabharata'). These works, which narrated the Mahabharata in prose, are important from the perspective of Ramu's narration.

Pampa's Bharata, Ranna's Bharata (partially), and Kumaravyasa's Bharata, which were based on the Mahabharata already existing in Kannada, were in poetry. Ramu leaves this tradition and bases his story on narrations suitable for the new era. Thus, Ramu has shown that his aspiration is to build the Mahabharata contemporaneously once again in the tradition of Vachana Bharatas.

The Mahabharata and Ramayana have survived among the common people in the form of recitation. Did Ramu try to grasp the Mahabharata orally? I don't know. But he must have felt it appropriate to construct the Mahabharata again and again by following the 'Vachana' style that is close to the heart. Therefore, his notes, as small articles, attempt to bring the Mahabharata into Kannada. By the way, Devanur, his friend, says that Ramu speaks in half-sentences. The greatness of this book is that it has been edited in such a way that the words of Ramu, which are stuck in his throat, are released and the form of Saraswati emerges.l
