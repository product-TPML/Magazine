A relaxed stroll in Jannu's world!

Manjushree Kadakola

Little Jannu, Big Eyes

Gowri

Childhood is a mine of writing for all writers. Not just for writing, but for life, childhood is the root and foundation that shapes our personality. That's why childhood stories never bore anyone; they never feel enough. 'Little Jannu, Big Eyes' by Soumya Prabhu Kalyankar is a work like a sweet water stream flowing calmly through such a forest of childhood.

Although it is written as a 'mini-novel' on the cover, this work does not neatly fit into the genre of a novel. In fact, it doesn't fully adapt to any genre. If considered a novel, it appears as a story, and if considered a collection of stories, it shows shades of an essay. If considered a children's work, it easily transcends that scope, and if considered an adult's world, it presents the free spirit of a child's mind. If one simply reads it without bothering with the molds of all those models, it gives the pleasure of drinking the cool water of a stream flowing naturally in the forest. The fluidity of the stream's water and the complexity of the eddies in its path are both present in this work. The inclusiveness of the water embracing both banks is also evident in the language of this work. The tenderness and innocence seen in the world of children are blended into the language here. However, it is noteworthy that the author does not show the cleverness of overlooking the cruelties of life and the pettiness of the adult world encountered there, in the rush to romanticize that innocent world.

Let's consider the first two chapters of the work. The pair of Jannu and Timmu, without the knowledge of the elders, bring okra saplings from Vasudevajja's backyard next door and plant them in their own backyard. What starts as a playful game of children, this gardening effort, however, has serious consequences. Jannu and Timmu are best friends. But there is also an abstract chasm between them. Although that chasm is not visible to children's minds, the adult world keeps showing it in different ways. Even though both have an equal share in the operation of planting okra saplings, Timmu is more affected by its bad consequences.

In the second chapter, 'The Lost Child', another facade of such a chasm in the adult world unfolds. Jannu, who wanders around and unknowingly ends up in front of Perbu's house, is picked up and caressed by Lavinakka. She calls him inside and makes him sit. That house, that world, is completely new to Jannu, and moreover, it is a world he should not enter. As soon as he enters that world, which was mysterious until then, Jannu begins to like it. He also likes the people there. But there are cracks there, beyond Jannu's comprehension. He doesn't understand why Lavinakka sheds tears looking at him. The author also doesn't explain it. This unspoken silence makes this chapter even more emotional; it moistens the mind. Many such characters encountered on Jannu's path are powerful enough to hold us. But the author tells such characters and incidents very naturally and simply, without underlining them. She is also careful that if she emphasizes even a little, Jannu's world would be disturbed. The picture Jannu sees in Kittu uncle's room, which looks like Lavinakka, is seen by the readers just as it is seen by Jannu. But the hint of another story being there is also subtly visible. Many such narrative hints are found throughout the book; they hold us for a moment, spin us around, and then send us forward.

As the author herself has stated, Jannu is her father's childhood name. The childhood stories she heard from him descended into the author's mind, grew, mingled with her own childhood, and also included her son's childhood, flowing here as Jannu's story. Because three generations of childhoods are intertwined in this way, the Jannu here appears as universal as he is private.

However, the typical cynicism that our childhood was the best and that children of today are deprived of it is nowhere to be seen here. It is by escaping the artificiality of this assertion that this writing has gained a naturalness. Without making any statements or comparisons, without any lamentations or cynicism, this book presents a rich, vibrant world before us.

The spring of innocence in the writing within the book seems to overflow on the cover as well. The way the Malnad house, courtyard, trees, and even the letters of the title are used as images is unique. l
