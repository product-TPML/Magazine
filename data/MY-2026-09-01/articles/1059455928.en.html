He sits at the end of the road
Shetty holding the scales!
His is the last word!
-Lines from G.S. Shivarudrappa's poem

Even though he is now a big contractor, Perumal was a foreman thirty-five years ago. He had made a name for himself in the construction of new lakes. The name "Ragi Mudde" (finger millet ball) before his name was given to Perumal by the laborers working under him.

There was a reason for this. Perumal was born into a family of many Tamil migrants who, unable to bear poverty, came from some village in Tamil Nadu in search of work and settled in Mandya. Like his father, he worked hard and diligently as a laborer, earned the trust of contractors, and became a foreman. As his experience grew, he continued in his own work, earned money as a contractor, and became rich. Seeing his son employing hundreds of laborers, his mother Andaalamma, who was once a laborer, said, 'Son Perumal, remember this well: one who lives by making the hungry work is not fit to live on this earth. Those who know the depth, breadth, and extent of hunger on this earth are the hungry! When hungry, a person's power of discrimination is disturbed, and he will do any work to satisfy his hunger. On this earth, besides the two main genders, male and female, what remains are

only the hungry and the well-fed,' she would always tell her son, saying, 'Money earned by making the hungry work is poison.' As per his mother's strict command, he paid fair wages for work, and for thirty years, he strictly followed the rule that anyone coming to work in the morning had to show him the ragi mudde they brought for lunch to be allowed to work.

Most of those who came to carry soil to the lake embankment were women! Since Perumal's stance was that no one should work on an empty stomach, all the laborers who came in the morning would take a break for half an hour at noon and eat a ball of ragi mudde to work for another three or four hours! That's why Perumal would only hire laborers if they brought at least one ball of flour for lunch.

One day, this happened. The behavior of a female laborer named Saroja, who had been coming to carry soil for three or four days, seemed strange to him. When she came to work, she would open the lid of her tiffin carrier from a distance, show it, and quickly go to work. At lunchtime, Saroja, who would go alone behind a distant bush with her tiffin carrier without mingling with anyone, eat, and return, made him want to investigate her meal. That day, Perumal, trying to avoid her gaze, pretended to open the lid of her tiffin carrier and was about to leave when Perumal stopped her and said, 'Mother, don't go forward. Open the lid of your tiffin carrier.'

Even though Saroja said, 'No, master, truly, I swear, there's no ragi mudde!', Perumal didn't listen:

'Give it here, mother. I want to see what your ragi mudde is like,' he said, snatching the tiffin carrier from her hand and opening the lid. Saroja stood with her head bowed.

Perumal asked: 'How many days has it been since you started coming to work, mother?'

'Today is the tenth day...'

'Do you eat this mudde every day?'

'Yes, master... this one...'

'Truly...?'

'Truly, master...'

'Then let this be with me. You must eat this in front of me at noon. Go, mother, go to work...' he said.

Such an incident had never happened to him in his life until then. Perumal kept observing Saroja. It was clear that worry had taken over her face.

It was twelve o'clock. It was lunchtime for everyone. Perumal said, 'Everyone, eat... Saroja, come here, eat your mudde,' he commanded. Everyone opened their lunchboxes. Everyone started eating the ragi mudde with chutney or salt and onions they had brought. Saroja also came to Perumal, extended her hand for her lunchbox with a little hesitation, and stood with her head bowed...

Perumal gave her the lunchbox. 'Eat in front of me...' he said.

Saroja opened the lid of the tiffin carrier, broke the mudde into mouthfuls, and without looking back or forth, started swallowing it with 'golak, golak' sounds, finishing the entire mudde in a minute or two, without leaving even a tiny bit for custom's sake. Perumal, who was watching with his mouth open in surprise, was covered in sweat! He had never seen or heard of anyone eating a mud ball of black soil as if it were more delicious than obbattu (a sweet flatbread).

'Isn't that black soil you ate, mother...?'

'Yes: what you say is true...'

'Have you been eating this for ten days, mother?'

'Yes! This... this black soil...'

'Why did you do this, mother...?'

'If I didn't bring and show a flour mudde... you wouldn't hire me for work, so I had to do this...'

Perumal now began to understand why Saroja would pretend to open the lid of her tiffin carrier, pretend to show a flour mudde, quickly leave, and go behind the fence of the rose bush ten or more yards away, and eat as if everyone else was eating!

'Don't be upset that I'm asking this... Do you eat this at home too, mother?'

'No. At home, I eat ragi mudde... only here... today behind the fence.'

'So, today is the day you ate the black soil mudde!'

'Yes! The house is full of people. Father has paralysis... Mother has tuberculosis: and then there are younger brothers and sisters... What can I do, tell me... If I work, there is food... otherwise, there isn't... The twenty rupees I earn is not enough for one meal.... So, if I eat one mudde in the morning, where will I get it for lunch?' She wiped her tears...

'I will provide enough groceries for your family for a week...'

'Oh, please, I beg you... don't you provide it. My father and mother won't tolerate such a thing. It's a surprise to everyone in our neighborhood that I'm sent to work. Ours is a respected family! Now Shani (Saturn) has turned unfavorable,' she replied with her head bowed.

Perumal's face fell, and he bowed his head!.

Perumal knew well the pain of hunger and its consequences in his life. Wiping his tear-filled eyes with the end of his dhoti:

'Alright, mother... From tomorrow, I will make sure a ball of ragi mudde comes for you from our house. Eat it... Don't refuse, mother! My soul won't be at peace... Aren't you like a daughter to me?'

'Yes! So be it... master,' Saroja said, stuffing the end of her sari into her mouth and swallowing the tears that came.

The next day, everyone came to work. Perumal had brought a flour mudde in his lunchbox for Saroja! He wished to see the effect on her face after she ate this mudde to satisfy her midday hunger...

But she was nowhere to be seen!

'Come on, everyone!' he called out to everyone...

The women and men came... and showed their lunchboxes.

The foreman, with anxiety: 'Where is Saroja? She's not visible at all?'

No one spoke...

A middle-aged woman spoke – 'Master, after finishing work here and going home, within an hour or two, she had severe vomiting and diarrhea and died in the middle of the night...'

Perumal's vision blurred, and he began to feel dizzy...

'Oh! What a murder I have committed,' he said, as if heard but not heard, and fainted! The flour mudde he had brought for Saroja in his lunchbox began to grow cold!l
