The narrative of the Dalit world written by eye and gut

Raghunath C H

C.M. Muniyappa Hasala's 'Eye at the Foot'

C.M. Muniyappa Hasala

K. Narasimhamurthy

C.M. Muniyappa Hasala of Kolar, a leading figure in the Dalit movement, has self-published his work 'Kaladiya Kannu' (Eye at the Foot) with the subtitle 'Dalit Loka Kathana' (Narrative of the Dalit World). Known as 'CM' by those who know him, he is also 'Sanchike' Muniyappa. 'Sanchike' is the name of the newspaper he publishes from Kolar.

Names carry history. His 'Kaladiya Kannu' carries such a history. It is the history of the Dalit struggle in Karnataka. The history of the birth and evolution of the Dalit Sangharsh Samiti. The history of Dalits revolting against inhumane exploitation. This is why it seems special that he has dedicated the work to 'the comrades in struggle who shed sweat and blood to lead the oppressed world from the darkness of discrimination and deceit towards light'.

What began as an autobiography at the outset, by the time it was written over thirteen years and released, it had taken the form of a 'Dalit Loka Kathana'. While encompassing the framework of a personal story, it has also expanded to the framework of the Dalit community's story. Thus, it is both a personal narrative and a world narrative. A world within an individual. An individual within the world. It also shows how solitude and the world are intertwined. His autobiography has emerged as the autobiography of the DSS. 'My autobiography is emerging as the autobiography of the DSS' is his assertion.

The greatness of this monumental work, spread across 140 chapters in seven lengthy parts and comprising 743 pages, is that it can be read from anywhere. This is because it is a collection of individual narratives of struggle. However, the way the book is structured suggests that one must read the front and back inner flap pages before proceeding.

The portraits of B. Krishnappa, the founder president of the Dalit Sangharsh Samiti, and Devanur Mahadeva, prominent leaders who built the Dalit movement, have been specially used. The notes below the pictures seem to represent the way they built the movement and the philosophy of the DSS. These notes are extensively documented in different chapters of the book.

Turning the cover page, the flap page shows a depiction of B. Krishnappa collapsing after witnessing the 'Chandragutti Naked Service'. The resolve to fight against it also begins there. On the last flap page, there is an excerpt from a speech delivered by Devanur Mahadeva at the DSS state committee meeting held in Mangaluru in 1984. It ends with the sentence, 'He gave the DSS the initiation of non-violence'. Muniyappa was a witness to both these incidents. Both clearly indicate that this work primarily aims to explain about Dalits and the DSS. The direction in which the work was written and the direction in which it should be read become clear. These seem to want to say something else about the intolerance that has now arisen between Holeya and Madiga due to internal reservation. It seems that Muniyappa's intention was also to draw the readers' attention to that. For the same reason, Muniyappa, who set out to write his story, has given first priority to documenting the memories of the Dalit movement.

This work is not just a collection of memories written while sitting in one place. To accurately touch upon those memories, he traveled across the state and conducted fieldwork. Arahalli, the place where student Munivenkatappa was murdered; Namada Chilume in Tumakuru, where the first state-level study camp of DSS was held; Konakuntlu, where the first camp in Kolar was held and where he got married; Hunasikote in Malur, where Anasuyaamma was raped and her father Sheshagiriyappa was murdered; Marasanapalli, Nagasandra, the sites of land struggles... thus, he has also done unconventional fieldwork. Senior and junior activists of the movement have also accompanied him. This has made it possible to weave significant details into the memories. The narrative has achieved completeness.

The titles given to the parts and chapters of the narrative also reflect Muniyappa's metaphorical power of language and the freshness of his narration. 'In the lap of Hasaladavva', 'In the Anubhava Mantapa called DSS', 'Hunasikote that expanded the horizon of the Dalit movement', 'DSS rises; struggles a hundred', 'In the lap of Uttar Pinakini', 'Sanghamitra of self-respect', 'They should be remembered in the cool shade' – these titles themselves reflect the vastness of the story.

During the writing process, he suffered three heart attacks when he revisited memories of his family's poverty and his struggle for money when his wife was admitted to the hospital for childbirth, which highlights the intensity with which he immersed himself in narrating the world story. Even when writing such painful memories, his patient use of language is evident throughout the work.

He does not elaborate much on the crushing poverty at home, the helplessness of his parents in facing it, and the violence and humiliation he faced in schools and hostels because he was a Dalit. Such experiences conclude within a page or two. However, when writing about the DSS and its struggles, his mind and hands do not tire. They unfold over as many pages as he writes. For example, the third part of the work, 'Hunasikote that expanded the horizon of the Dalit movement', dedicates seventy-eight pages to the rape of Anasuyaamma and the murder of her father Sheshagiriyappa. He documents all developments, from the protests demanding the arrest of the accused, the 'Bengaluru Chalo', to the sentencing of the culprits. There, he becomes 'Muniyappa the activist'. There is no room for fear or hesitation. There, home, wife, everything disappears as if invisible. But in the same part, he concludes in just two chapters that his wife Nagarathna gave birth and the postpartum period was spent in extreme hardship.

The documentation of the birth and growth of DSS, along with the struggles against atrocities on Dalits in the state from the 1970s to the 1990s, is based on experiences, which creates a sense of immersion in the readers. In every struggle, curiosity about directions and paths also increases. It also reminds one of the Telugu films that are coming out now. After all, Kolar is on the border of Andhra. For the same reason, Kolar's Kandelugu Kannada has also contributed immensely to this narration. In Kolar and Chikkaballapur regions, the influence of Kannada, Telugu, and Tamil languages is high. A unique dialect, a mixture of Kannada and Telugu, has developed among the people there. Kandelugu dialogues are spread throughout the work. Not only that, two chapters have been given Telugu titles! 'Yuvataram Kadilindi' (page 331), 'CM nu Mettudinchina CM' (page 686).

Along with Kandelugu, there is Kolar's own linguistic expression. For example, 'Along with him, Krishnappa's heavily pregnant wife Chinnamma, embracing her husband's intestines lying in a pool of blood, rolling all over the corpse, cried out, 'Oh... you who were going, have gone, now who is there for me and my three children? And this child in my womb. What shall I do with this helpless child?' and started punching her heavily pregnant belly with her hands.' This is the power of Muniyappa's language.

Instead of asking what is in the narrative about the Dalit struggle, one should ask what is not. All the major events of the Dalit struggles witnessed in this land, primarily in Kolar-Chikkaballapur districts, over a period of more than a decade and a half, are densely packed here. The efforts of leaders like B. Krishnappa, Basavalingayya, N. Rachayya, Siddalingayya, Kotiganahalli Ramaiah, N. Venkatesh, N. Shivanna, N. Muniswamy, Vijayakumar, Devanur Mahadeva, Gangaram Chandala, who inspired and influenced the Dalit movement, the birth of 'Panchama' magazine, Dalit Student Federation, Dalit Kala Mandali, the first study camp of DSS, Devanur's initiation of non-violence to DSS, the struggle against Chandragutti Naked Service, the influence of Jananatya Mandali, the cultural troupe of the Naxalite movement in neighboring Andhra – the companionship of activists, land struggles, thrilling protest songs... all these together build the history of the oppressed in Karnataka here. The commitment of activists who shook ministers and chief ministers of that era also shines through. The commitment of K.M. Kommanna, H.M. Ramachandra, Bangavadi Narayanappa, who worked alongside frontline leaders, also draws attention.

For this very reason, the work 'Kaladiya Kannu' seems to have unconsciously adopted Kuvempu's lines, 'Here no one is important, no one is unimportant', as its aspiration. Muniyappa, the narrator of the story, has made himself an instrument. Here, the struggle is important. Through it, achieving liberation, and making others achieve it, is important. This work appears as evidence that the land of struggle in Kolar still lives that aspiration. The work 'Kaladiya Kannu', released at a time when the joy of the struggle against the 'NEET' exam irregularities and the resignation of a central government minister by CJPU (Cockroach Janata Party) has spread across the country, silently states that such struggles have already taken place and won on this land from a non-violent foundation.

Many years ago, when the 'Aganag Geleyara Balaga' encountered an elderly person struggling down the Antaragange hill range in Kolar, carrying a bundle of firewood, who said, 'Swami, my eyes are not in my head, but at my feet,' that statement, which revealed the path of the Dalit movement and opened our inner eyes, became the title," says Muniyappa.

Just as he said, 'Kaladiya Kannu' has attempted to portray the path of the Dalit movement. As Muniyappa himself says, he has taken only a handful of water from the vast ocean of the Dalit movement. Therefore, if other frontline leaders also write their narratives, there is a possibility of many insights being gained. Kannada also needs this.l
