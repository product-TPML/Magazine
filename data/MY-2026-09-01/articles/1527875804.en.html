Chocolates, biscuits, etc., that caught the eye in the department store were crowded on the shelves. From behind me, as I was looking confused about what to take, I heard a child's voice asking, 'Give me that.'

A little girl, crying and asking in a single tone, was pointing to an attractive lollipop. As the mother, with divine indifference, was picking out the idli mix and sambar powder she wanted, the child's wailing grew louder. The mother, having finished her shopping, said, 'Look, if you eat a lollipop, you'll get a sore throat, wheezing, and all that. Then you'll have to go to the doctor. I'll get you something even better later, come on,' and forcibly picked up the child and left. The child's face was covered in snot and tears, all 'one'. The mother, after paying at the counter, said, 'I'm fed up with your stubbornness and tantrums. Come on,' and left the store with the child.

As soon as I heard the word 'stubbornness', a face began to appear in my memory. She was known for her stubbornness in her time.

The child's disappointment at not getting what its tender heart desired might have prompted the tantrum. But what desires, disappointments, or frustrations deep within her might have appeared as stubbornness?

* * *

In a street in that town, past midnight, a man stood in front of a modest house door. He knocked on his house door once. No one came from inside. A shout escaped. 'Bhagya... Sundra, open the door, son,' The people inside did not say 'Hara, Shiva'. 'Oh... Ho Daulat! I'm not hitting your mother's wealth. Are you going to open the door or not?' he said, kicking the door hard and then squatting. The sturdy old door had not moved. Inside the small room, mother Sundra and daughter Bhagya, who were sleeping, woke up. The daughter softly told her mother, 'Amma, father has come.' Mother Sundra did not stir. 'If he comes, he comes, be quiet. It's enough that he comes and makes a fuss every now and then. If he dies over there, we can put two handfuls of earth over him and a stone on his chest,' she grumbled and fell silent. His mother, who was sleeping in the central room and heard all this, wondered what else he would do, opened the door, and brought her son, who was slumped outside, inside.

Morning came. As if nothing had happened during the night, the daily activities of the house began. The husband, whose night's intoxication had worn off, ate the upma given by his mother and left for his work. Daughter-in-law Sundra had made radish sambar and rice for lunch. Daughter Bhagya had gone to school. Since the mother-in-law and daughter-in-law did not get along, they would eat their meals separately. At noon, the mother-in-law sat down to eat, mixed rice with radish sambar, and put a mouthful in her mouth, but immediately the morsel came back to her hand! The sambar was too salty! That was enough for an argument to start, and the mother-in-law roared. 'Who made that sambar?'

'Don't you know I cook? What happened to the sambar?'

'What happened? The sambar is too salty! Instead of putting salt in the sambar, you should have filled your... with salt,' the mother-in-law snapped.

The daughter-in-law's anger flared up. 'You say such things just because a little salt accidentally got in? Are you a mother-in-law? The other day, I made dal sambar, and you ate it all by yourself, even putting a lump of it in, and didn't give a single lump to this granddaughter. Not even to your son. You ate it all by yourself. Was the sambar I made good then?'

'I'll eat as I please. My husband has given me a share in this house. He has also put some money in my name in the bank. I'll eat as I please. Did you bring anything for me from your father's house?' the mother-in-law snapped.

At the mention of her father's house, Sundra's face became still. Her orphanhood suddenly came to mind. Nevertheless, she composed herself and retorted, 'If my father and mother were alive, why would they give me to your house? They would give me to a father-like house.' But as her anger and agitation increased, she did what she always did and left for the house of her only friend on the back street. Her friend's house often became Sundra's 'anger-house'. Her daughter, who came from school, went looking for her mother and also headed to the back street. 'Let her go wherever she wants, she'll have to come back here like a broken pot,' the mother-in-law said, remaining silent.

* * *

Behind Sundra's still face lay a pathetic story. She was the youngest. So her parents' affection was greater. Whatever she asked for appeared before her eyes. When all the children were between ten and sixteen years old, her parents succumbed to a strange fever whose name was unknown. The orphaned children were taken in by their very wealthy aunt. Everyone was obedient to the aunt. The elder brother struggled and passed SSLC, getting a job. The other two girls were 'literate'. Both were very beautiful. Sundra's elder sister was good at housework and was obedient to her aunt, becoming her favorite. Sundra, deprived of her parents' affection and suddenly fallen to earth like a bird's chick from the secure shelter of her parents, developed a feeling of orphanhood that created 'a kind of stubborn, sulky state of mind' instead of adapting to the situation. Sundra was as clever as her sister and could do all the household chores with ease. But the sulkiness that had made a home in her heart would occasionally appear as stubbornness.

The aunt had a very busy household. She had one son and a daughter-in-law. One day, the aunt finished cooking early and went to a special puja at the Shiva temple. The daughter-in-law finished her share of work and was resting in her room. When the aunt returned from the temple and removed the lid of the rice pot to prepare food, she found rice mixed with the cooked rice! She was shocked, and when she checked, rice was scattered everywhere from the kitchen pot to the storeroom where rice, dal, and other provisions were kept. The aunt, hurriedly putting rice on again, began to think. Whose work is this? This must be Sundra's work. Lately, she has been acting strangely. Still, I must ask, thought the aunt, and called Sundra, 'Why did you do such a thing, child? You made it so that the rice cannot be eaten,' she asked calmly. Sundra did not answer. She went straight and leaned against the stone pillar of the backyard shed. No matter who spoke to her, there was no 'hara' or 'shiva'. After a long time, the aunt, 'If you don't eat, I won't eat,' she said loudly, and then Sundra ate. Many such instances of stubbornness came to the aunt's mind. Once, the aunt gave the roses bloomed in the house garden to her daughter-in-law and Sundra to wear in their hair. What happened, Sundra went and leaned against the stone pillar of the shed and began to grumble, 'She is a beautiful whitewashed wall, and I? A black wall.' It took a lot of effort to calm her down.

Such incidents kept recurring. Sundra's elder sister married the boy chosen by her aunt and was running a neat household in her husband's house. But Sundra's behavior often felt very humiliating to her aunt in front of her husband. Her husband, amidst his vast business dealings, never had time to bother about household matters. All the happenings of the family were in the aunt's hands.

The aunt began to think. In many situations, Sundra's behavior was beyond imagination and logic. Although the sister's kinship and the children's orphanhood bound the aunt in a bond of love, her stubbornness day by day made the aunt thoughtful. I was born of shared blood. What need do others have to tolerate her? She needs a small family with fewer people, and the boy should be calm, she thought, and requested all her acquaintances to find a good husband. A boy with a job and a small house of his own was found in Mysore. The aunt advised Sundra a lot and convinced her to marry. The wealthy aunt gave all kinds of jewelry and clothes, as if to her own daughter, and got her married. The uncle never went against his wife's words. Thus, Sundra, as the aunt's adopted daughter, came to her husband's house in Mysore.

With the enthusiasm of having her own home and family, a new glow appeared on Sundra's face. To some extent, she began to get along with her mother-in-law. However, stubbornness occasionally peeked through. When Sundra had a daughter, the aunt, more than a mother, took care of her during childbirth and sent her off. But over time, one summer, the aunt suddenly passed away. The aunt, who had been a mental support even though she was in another town, was no more, and this once again pushed her into a feeling of orphanhood. Her brother and sister also became unwanted.

One day, after a usual 'clash' between the mother-in-law and daughter-in-law, Sundra lay down quietly and began to think. My mother-in-law and I don't get along. She always finds fault. What should I do now? That's when the aunt came to mind. All her words of advice came back. That's right, I'll do that, she decided and fell asleep.

One evening, when her husband returned from work, Sundra and her daughter were not at home. When he asked his mother, his mother said, 'What does she do for me? Where did she go, who knows, with her arrogance on her head and her stubbornness on her tongue.' When her husband inquired, she had not even gone to her friend's house on the back street. The husband became worried. Let it be, he thought, let's see in the morning. In the morning, he thought, perhaps Sundra has gone to her uncle's or great-uncle's house near Mysore? He decided to go there and inquire and went to Tarabanahalli. Sundra and her husband had often gone there for parties and village festivals. As he had guessed, she was there. No matter how affectionately he called her to come home, Sundra remained sulky. Finally, Sundra's uncle said, 'She came in some anger. Let four days pass, we will bring her, you go.' But that day never came.

Sundra's stay in Tarabanahalli seemed like a kind of liberation. But how long could she stay in someone else's dependent house? She mentioned this to her aunt Lakshmakka. Lakshmakka thought a lot and told Sundra, 'Look, you grew up in a rich house, labor is not for you. I have an idea, if you don't mind, I'll tell you.' Sundra agreed to do as Lakshmakka said. Lakshmakka said, 'Look, you are good at making snacks. Tell our master, I will get a small house built. There you can open a dosa, idli shop. You don't have to toil in the sun. The men of our village go to Mysore for tasty food. Let them eat here. You will earn in front of your house.' Sundra's small restaurant started on the veranda of a rented house. The villagers tasted Sundra's cooking. On Tuesdays and Sundays, idli and boti started, and business began to boom. Her daughter started going to school there. Money began to flow. Even with all this happening, Sundra remained strict when dealing with people. Those who came for snacks had to eat quickly and leave. If there was a slight delay, she would scold the young boys, 'Go,' 'Go look at your face in the mirror.'

Sundra was aware of her beauty and youth. Beyond that, she had self-respect and determination. She was focused on her daughter's future.

She would talk to customers only as much as necessary and save as much money as possible from her earnings for her daughter's future. She had earned the reputation of being a 'strict' woman among the villagers. Meanwhile, Sundra's husband came many times and asked her to return home. Sundra replied that she would not come at all. Finally, even when her mother-in-law died, she did not budge. Many times, those who saw her wondered what a stone-hearted woman she was.

In those days, villages only had facilities for education up to the seventh grade. For further high school education, boys had to go to towns with high schools and stay in relatives' houses. Thus, the education of many boys and girls was cut short. Noticing this situation, the village headmen worked hard and got a high school sanctioned for their village by the government. This made higher education possible for boys from surrounding villages. Sundra's daughter Bhagya completed her high school education. Seeing her mother's struggles, she gave up her desire for further studies and stood by her mother at home. Like her mother, she was beautiful and quick at work. But she was calm without her mother's anger or stubbornness. This was because the struggle for a stable life that her mother had waged and the education she had received made Bhagya calm.

Seeing her daughter blossoming with beauty and youth, Sundra began to think about her daughter's future. I left my home and came to this village, and I'm spending my days. Can my daughter do the same? She thought that she must provide a secure future for her, and she requested her acquaintances in the village about Bhagya's future.

The village headmen said, 'This woman is very stubborn in her words. But she is respectable. Our villagers like her seriousness. Now we must help,' they decided and began to look for a husband for Sundra's daughter. A respectable boy with a small house in a neighborhood of Mandya and a shop attached to it was found. The boy had a father and married sisters. Based on the good opinion of the village headmen, the boy and his father agreed to the match.

The marriage was fixed. Sundra did not even mention calling her husband. Then the village elders said, 'So far, your stubbornness has worked. Look at your daughter's future life. Don't make her feel like her father 'exists but doesn't'.' After this advice, she bowed to the words of the villagers and agreed to call her husband to the wedding.

The wedding was arranged in a beautiful garden around the Boredeva temple on the banks of the Kaveri. Sundra's husband came to the wedding. Then the older women whispered, 'Now at least give up your stubbornness and perform the 'dhare' ritual with your husband for your daughter.' Sundra silently performed the 'dhare' ritual with her husband for her son-in-law and daughter and tied the mangalsutra. The daughter went to her husband's house and lived a happy family life thanks to a good husband.

But Sundra's daily routine continued as usual. Noticing that Sundra and her husband were getting old, her daughter and son-in-law would often come and say, 'Enough of this struggle of toiling in front of the fire. Come and live with us.' But Sundra would say, 'Let's see when that time comes,' and send them away.

Some time passed. One day, a servant came from her daughter's house and said, 'Amma, your master passed away this morning. They sent me to bring you.' As he said this, Sundra's hand, which was sifting rice in a winnowing basket, stopped. 'Ah, he's gone,' she uttered.
