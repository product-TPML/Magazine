‘English Geethagalu’ on the threshold of a century

eglish geethegalu1.jpg

English Geethagalu

Dr. R. Lakshminarayana

The work ‘English Geethagalu’, which heralded the Kannada Navodaya poetry, is now celebrating its centenary. B.M. Srikantaiah’s ‘English Geethagalu’ is known as a pioneering work of Kannada Navodaya poetry. Therefore, Kuvempu rightly called Srikantaiah ‘the pioneer of a new era’. It appears that Srikantaiah had finished writing the poems included in ‘English Geethagalu’ by 1910. As Srikantaiah himself stated in the preface to ‘English Geethagalu’ (1926), a publication of the Karnataka Sangha of Central College, three poems were first published in 1919 (by Bapu Subbaraya in ‘Vidya Dayini’ magazine), 15 poems in 1921 (in the form of a small book titled ‘English Geethagalu’ in Karnataka Grantha Male by the same Bapu Subbaraya), and another 24 poems in 1924 (by D.V.G. in ‘Karnataka Janajeevana’ magazine), thus partially published in three places. Along with these songs, Srikantaiah collected all the remaining poems and published a full-fledged collection of ‘English Geethagalu’ with a total of 60 translated English poems, along with his three independent poems ‘Kanike’, ‘Bharatamateya Vakya’ and ‘Mysuru Makkalu’, making a total of 63 poems, in 1926 by the Karnataka Sangha of Central College. Therefore, although the year 1921 is considered the year of publication of ‘English Geethagalu’ (only 15 poems), the full-fledged ‘English Geethagalu’ as it exists now was published in August/September 1926. This has also been confirmed by T.N. Srikantaiah. It was only after its full publication that it reached all Kannada-speaking regions, created a stir in the state, captivated all poetry lovers, and subsequently influenced young Kannada poets and the world of poetry.

The significance of Srikantaiah’s work can be understood by knowing the state of Kannada poetry before the publication of ‘English Geethagalu’. Our eminent litterateurs, including T.N. Srikantaiah, have written extensively on this. Muddana, known as the harbinger of new literature, had, in a way, bid farewell to old poetry by saying ‘Padhyam Vadhyam Gadhyam Hrudyam’ (Poetry is to be killed, prose is to be cherished). With the beginning of the modern era and the influence of Western education, the educated Kannada minds of the new age, who were captivated by the poems of famous English poets, strongly felt that our style of poetry also needed to change. However, not knowing how, they continued to write in the old style, i.e., in the Shatpadi, Chaupadi, and Sangatya forms used in medieval Kannada, without abandoning alliteration, to suit new emotions and languages. Among them, S.G. Narasimhachar, Panje Mangesh Rao, and Hattiyangadi Narayana Rao were prominent. Panje and Narasimhachar had made some beautiful independent poems along with some heartfelt translations. Hattiyangadi, like Srikantaiah, had published a small collection of translated English poems called ‘Angla Kavitasaara’. However, these three elders wrote with minor changes in the style of old meters like Chaupadi, Shatpadi, and Ragale, and these were not in large numbers. And all these were poems limited to a specific number of stanzas, like four or five. Therefore, they were not in a prominent form to pave the way for new poetry. Hence, no matter what others say, Srikantaiah is definitely the pioneer of Navodaya.

The old poems that existed before the publication of Srikantaiah’s ‘English Geethagalu’ were long poems about the lives and glory of kings and emperors of the past, mythological figures, or devotional themes. Whether it was hardship, happiness, pain, joy, or a depiction of nature, everything was related to individuals of some other era, not to the common man of this time. As Srikantaiah stated in the preface, he beautifully translated and presented to the Kannada people the lyrical poems composed by English poets on subjects such as ‘war, love, death, patriotism, devotion, natural beauty, joys and sorrows of human life, passions, and purusharthas’, which are universal poetic themes. ‘Kari Heggedeya Magalu’, ‘Prarthane’ (Karunalu Ba Belake), ‘Duhkhasetu’, ‘Muddina Kurimari’, ‘Haleya Palakeya Mukhagalu’, ‘Vasanta’, ‘Mada-Madi’, ‘Binkada Singari’, ‘Nanna Premada Hudugi’, ‘Paduma’, ‘Hedaruvenu Na Ninna Binnanakele Henne’ – all these poems, as if they were originally written in Kannada, are so beautiful and natural that they have always remained in the hearts of Kannadigas. This collection includes poems by twenty-nine poets such as Wordsworth, Shelley, Byron, Tennyson, Browning, Newman, Shakespeare, Scott, Lamb, etc. Among them are poems by renowned poets of the English poetic world as well as by lesser-known poets. Their poems became famous after being translated into Kannada.

Its scope extends from Shakespeare and Nash of the Elizabethan era, Thomson of the Restoration era, Tennyson and Browning of the Victorian era, Burns of the early Romantic era, to Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley, and other 15 poets of the later Romantic era. From the perspective of content, 22 poems are related to love and affection. 7 poems deal with war and heroic deeds, 4 poems are about nature, 3 poems are about death, 5 poems depict sadness, 3 poems are about the British Empire... and 15 poems contain various themes that cannot be categorized. The English elegy came to Kannada through Srikantaiah.

To understand their beauty, we can look at a few lines from one or two famous poems:

Some lines from his most famous poem titled ‘Prarthane’ (Prayer):

Come, merciful light, in this enveloping darkness,
Hold my hand and lead me.
The night is a cave of darkness; home is far; have pity,
Hold my hand and lead me.

You have protected this fool until now, will you not
Hold my hand and lead me further?
Will you not cross the difficult forest, pass through hills and rivers,
And push away the night?

This is the Kannada translation of Cardinal Newman’s poem ‘Lead Kindly Light’. This prayer from a heart purified by repentance has become the inner voice of the afflicted of all times.

In the poem ‘Lord Ullin’s Daughter’ by Campbell, Srikantaiah has depicted the pathetic scene of a father seeing his daughter drowning before his eyes:

Return, return, my daughter,
He cried with a choked voice,
I have forgotten, I accept your lover,
Return, my child, he said.

Could she return? Could she go?
The waves crashed and
Rolled over her.
In sorrow for his daughter,
He remained (Kari Heggedeya Magalu).

Another equally famous and heart-wrenching poem is Hood’s ‘Bridge of Sighs’.

This orphan, another one, disgusted with life, unable to bear the pain, drowned herself in the river. Gently touch her, pity her, lift her up. What a tender body! What a fresh youth!

(‘Duhkha Setu’, the Kannada version of the poem ‘Bridge of Sighs’) Equally melancholic is the poem ‘Haleya Palakeya Mukhagalu’ (Old Familiar Faces by Lamb). Some lines from it:

Those who would caress me, saying, "My child, have you fallen?"
Those who would grieve for me were there.
On that happy day,
Those who would dance with me were there.
All, all have vanished,
The old familiar faces.

Burns’s ‘Duncan Gray’ here takes the form of ‘Mada-Madi’ and captivates us.

The hunter Mada came, hah, what a hunt!
He begged and died, turned to lime, hah!
What a hunt! What did Mada cut?
What scattered here and there? Hah! What a hunt!

Similarly, Burns’s love poem
‘My Love is like a red red rose’ was beautifully translated by Srikantaiah as ‘Nanna Premada Hudugi’, and Scott’s ‘Proud Maisie’ as ‘Binkada Singari’... And Nash’s poem ‘Spring’ has truly emerged as a fountain of beauty in Kannada and is considered a successful translation; or the atmosphere of England has taken the form of nature in Kannada land.

Spring has come, the king of seasons has come,
He has killed the cold...

The fragrance of mangoes, the scent of jasmine everywhere, the coolness of the breeze,
The crowd of people at the fair, a delight to the ears,
The beauty of birdsong,
Coo-woo, jug-jug, puvvi, too-witt-woo.

The depiction of nature in this poem mirrors the view of our village nature during spring. As Sri Dharwadkar said, ‘New meter, new structure, clear language, lack of rhyme, and fluidity were the main characteristics of English Geethagalu.’

There have been many criticisms and objections regarding the success and failure of Srikantaiah’s translation of ‘English Geethagalu’, the appropriateness of the words used, the freedom he took in translating, and the poems he chose for translation.
M.G. Krishnamurthy (Modern Indian Literature) even went so far as to make an illiberal and irrelevant criticism, stating that Srikantaiah, by translating not-so-great Romantic poems instead of the avant-garde poetry that had already begun in England when he translated ‘English Geethagalu’, misled Kannada poets. He argued that if Srikantaiah had translated those, avant-garde poetry would have already come to Kannada, and if he had translated the poems of 17th-century metaphysical poets, it would have helped modern poets to connect with Vachana literature.

If Srikantaiah had properly considered the state of Kannada poetry before ‘English Geethagalu’ came out, Krishnamurthy would not have made such an absurd accusation. As Srikantaiah himself said, he was aware that ‘not all the poems included here are considered masterpieces in English literature’. He chose poems that were suitable for translation and that he liked, and some of them he translated so beautifully and naturally that they seemed more cohesive than the original poems, as if they were originally written in Kannada.

When translating English poems, Srikantaiah’s aim was to open up a new highway of meter and subject matter that poetry should encompass, and to show the direction that future Kannada poetry should take. His greatest achievement was doing this most effectively and appropriately. Srikantaiah, who had a deep understanding of Sanskrit, English, Kannada, and Tamil meters, used the familiar rhythms (of 3, 4, 5 matras, 3/4, 3/5, 4/2) in Kannada poetry, adapting them to the stress-based meter of English, but with various modifications in stanza structure, number of stanzas, and rhyme scheme (using, not using, and using end rhyme), thereby building the edifice of poetry in our spoken Kannada. His name is immortalized for the guidance he provided to future Kannada poetry. The spoken Kannada here refers to the Kannada that was refined by the Vachanakaras of the past and the Tatvapada writers of the pre-modern era, the Kannada used by the people. That is, creating a middle-style poetic language that was not Sanskrit-heavy nor rustic, but necessary for the expression of the personal aspirations of the new-age poets, was an equally important achievement. As soon as ‘English Geethagalu’ was published, poetry was liberated from the old ways. Inspired by this, and taking the models of novelty, freedom, and diversity of subject matter and meter shown here as a benchmark, a flood of diverse lyrical poems flowed from all parts of Karnataka. In Dharwadkar’s words, ‘Karnataka became a nest of singing birds.’

Murtirao, defining the place of English Geethagalu, declared, ‘English Geethagalu stands firm as a milestone in our literature due to its inherent excellence, and perhaps even more so due to historical reasons.’ He correctly identified the miracle it performed by saying, ‘As soon as it was published and dawned, poetic songs began to be heard from all over Karnataka. It was as if a new world, hitherto unseen by poets, had opened before their eyes.’ Poets enriched the treasury of Kannada lyrical poetry by composing poems about the beauty of nature they saw, the pain, joy, love, friendship, sorrow, and feelings about their land and language they experienced, without being limited by any specific number of stanzas, adopting the various rhythmic patterns shown by Srikantaiah, which were familiar to the ears of Kannadigas.

Masti, Bendre, V. Seetharamaiah, Kuvempu, Puttina, Kadengodlu, Rajaratnam, Madhurachenna, along with the first generation, and later K.S.N., S.V.P., G.S.S., Kanavi, and others, stepped in and built a strong, rich tradition of lyrical poetry. Srikantaiah became the creator of this tradition. This came to be known as Navodaya poetry. Srikantaiah’s poetic experiment inspired the Navodaya poets to undertake further new experiments, making ‘English Geethagalu’ truly an epoch-making work. The uniqueness of ‘English Geethagalu’ lies in the fact that its brilliance has not diminished even after a century.

These lines, emphatically declared by Masti, confirm the irreplaceable position of ‘English Geethagalu’ in the birth of new poetry.

‘What sweet voice is heard from within Kannada, in its song is seen all that you opened, the new, unblemished, brave, vast, universal expanse of emotion.’l
