Why did the villagers tie Sumani to the tree? Who is he? Who are Veeregowda? What is the relationship between Chikkaveeragowda and Sumani, etc.: some might say, which is the head, which is the tail. This is natural, you see. Sumani's father's name was Nagari. His mother's name was Mastamma. He got the name Nagari because he was an expert in playing and making drums in dozens of villages around his native place. Therefore, his birth name Channa was forgotten and he became Nagari. Nagari and Mastamma had a total of eleven children. The first ten children stopped drinking milk five days after birth; their bodies stiffened and they died. However, if the children of the farmers, Lingayats, and Muslims in the village grew up drinking the overflowing milk from Mastamma's full breasts, then the even better Brahmins and Vaishnavas would send their children with money to Mastamma for her breast milk to use as medicine when their eyes hurt. It is not wrong to say that the entire village grew up indebted to Mastamma's breast milk. This great mother, who did not have the fortune of feeding her own ten children, would, when any mother in the village who was hanging without milk asked her to feed her child, quickly pick up the child, put her nipple in its mouth, and no matter how much the child sucked or bit, she would close her eyes in the intoxication of motherhood without getting tired. After all ten children closed their eyes, Mastamma became pregnant for the eleventh time. Nagari made a vow to the Ellamma Devi temple in the village: ‘Before, if my son doesn't survive this time, next time I will not beat the drum in your procession’ he threatened. Secretly, he tempted her by promising to sacrifice a young buffalo on a new moon night in her name. When the labor pains started, Veeregowda's wife, Doddathayakka, boiled a knife well in water with a yard of thread for hours, and as soon as the child came, she cut the umbilical cord with well-washed hands and tied it with the thread. She herself cleaned the waste well and served Mastamma. The son survived. Nagari named him Sumani Basava, after his grandfather. Even though she said no, on the eleventh day, she rolled the newborn baby on the ground in front of the Ellamma Devi temple and came back.

Veeregowda and Doddathayakka had two daughters first, and after they 'died', a son survived. This is our protagonist, Chikkaveeragowda, who was one year older than Sumani Maharaja, and who grew up drinking Mastamma's overflowing breast milk, a life-giving friend. Once in the morning, once in the afternoon, once in the evening - thus, at least three times a day, Chikkaveeragowda would suckle Mastamma's nipples in the backyard. Sometimes Mastamma would also bring her son Sumani in her armpit. In one, Sumani, in the other, Chikkaveeru, like twins, would cling to her breasts. Mastamma was like a good goddess, dark, tall, stout, and strong. She didn't wear a blouse. A simple cotton saree, a black bead necklace with a thali around her neck. Two silver rings on her feet, a pair of silver bangles on her forearms. Earrings! These were her only ornaments. But her mouth always had to be chewing a quid of betel nut and tobacco. Her thick black hair, which hung from her head to her knees, gave her a brave and dignified appearance. Whenever such a Mastamma came near the house, the boy Chikkaveeragowda would say, ‘Chikkavva has come’ and would run to her and hug her...

‘Oh! You fell! Come, my little parrot!’ Mastamma would hug him to her chest and give him her breast.

As days passed like this, when Sumani was three and going on four, Nagari, who had gone to play the drum at the Huchchuraya fair of Hunjankere, which happens once every twelve years, on the fourth day of the fair, when the pujas of the gods of all ten villages were being celebrated competitively, exhausted from playing the drum rhythmically with all his strength, he bit his forehead and fell to the ground, never to rise again! After this tragedy, the widowed Mastamma would come to Chikkaveeragowda's house with her son at dawn, and would only return home after dark!

What if someone dies? What if someone is born? What if someone rules a kingdom? The sun rises and sets, days become weeks, weeks become months, months become years, it is inevitable! Along with this, humans, trees, and animals grow, are born, die, and play a role in many events, is it inevitable? Similarly, the lives of Sumani and Chikkaveeragowda were full of many strange incidents. Even if people said something, both of them maintained a weak line between untouchable and upper caste in front of people's eyes; but in fields, farms, gardens, lakes, etc., they behaved like equal beings created by nature. One incident is enough to illustrate that Sumani never crossed the line drawn by Chikkaveeragowda. It was mango season. Next to Veeregowda's land was Peerukhan Sab's garden. The cumin mango tree, famous for making one's mouth water, had all its fruits ripe and shining brightly in the midday sun, hanging from the tree. Peerukhan Sab's children were mischievous. Everyone around them was a relative, Hindus. Besides, who doesn't want mangoes and fruits? If you ask, they can't say no. If they keep giving to everyone who asks, in the end, not a single fruit will be left in the garden for tradition. Why all this? Besides, they were Muslims. What if someone got angry that day, and there was a fight and they came to blows, what form would it take, so they had appointed a strong, about twenty-five-year-old fisherman, Jogi, to guard the garden. Jogi was short, dark, and strong. Plus, he had a 'girru' mustache that stood up! Hearing his name, even the red ants living on the mango tree were afraid to sniff the mangoes. Many young boys, when they came near the garden, would make a mess when he roared, ‘Who's there!’ Such a bronze, threatening voice. Besides, he was known as the village champion in the wrestling competition held on Ugadi festival day! When Chikkaveeragowda stood in front of the cumin mango tree in the garden, which was under the watchful eye of Jogi, who had such a background, he couldn't control his craving. Besides, Jogi had disappeared among the lush bushes towards the Kodihalli canal to 'relieve himself', so without hesitation, he ordered Sumani, ‘Pluck the fruits! They smell good. My mouth is watering’.

‘That short Jogi is somewhere here. He must have gone to the water’ Sumani said in a drawn-out voice....
‘Hey! What kind of man are you... If I say pluck, you should pluck! What kind of Jogi is he! Peerukhan Sab and my father are friends, I'll take care of whatever happens next’ Chikkaveeragowda twisted the corner of his mustache with his right hand - like Duryodhana twisting his mustache in the Kurukshetra play's court scene.

‘After you said this much, what is Jogi to me’ Sumani said, and like a nimble monkey, he climbed the tree and plucked ten to fifteen ripe fruits; seeing this, Jogi, having finished his work quickly, tied his loincloth and came like an arrow from the earth: ‘Who climbed the tree! Is the garden your father's... or your mother's lover's, wait, I'm coming... Ho! Ho! He must be sitting on the tree. If he comes down, I'll skin him’ he said, and appeared under the tree like lightning, and seeing the Gowda's son: ‘What is this, you, being the Gowda's son, are looting someone else's garden with this bastard; they say the monkey that gives justice to the village urinates in front of the stove’ he said, pointing his hand at Sumani, and then at Chikkaveeragowda - with disdain. Sumani, who was engrossed in searching for good fruits and asking for them, without caring that Jogi had come, sang loudly, challenging, ‘Brave, brave, Bhuvan; who is equal to me on this earth?’: hearing the words ‘born of adultery and the monkey urinates in front of the stove’, he jumped down from the tree and stood, glaring at Jogi:

‘Who is born of adultery? Who is the monkey...’ he said. ‘You are born of adultery! The monkey is standing right here’ Jogi said, pointing at Chikkaveeragowda...

Chikkaveeragowda felt like he had been cut with a knife! ‘What did you say?’ he said, and no sooner had he put his hand on Jogi's hair; Jogi used one of his wrestling moves like lightning, knocked him down, and sat on his chest. Sumani couldn't hold back anymore. He grabbed Jogi's topknot, lifted him up like a bunch of coconuts, then held both his forearms, pulled him towards him with force, threw him to the ground with a thud, sat on his chest, and said, ‘I'll show you your birthday. You bastard, you dared to sit on my Chikkagowda’ he said, grabbing his hair and squeezing it. Jogi's life was coming and going, his eyes rolled, and he struggled.

‘Hey! Sumani, I beg you, let him go... Let go of Jogi's hair... He might die, I swear on my life’ Chikkaveeragowda said, joining his hands and making a pleading command, only then did Sumani snort and stand up! Jogi's voice had died down. The one who was boasting as the wrestling champion of the entire village was now a mouse. He had won the championship title only because Sumani, being an untouchable, was not allowed to participate in the wrestling held on Ugadi. Immediately, an idea flashed in his mind like lightning. He looked around. Dozens of people had started gathering. Turning towards the village gate, he said, ‘This Gowda's son... made me, Sumani, a low-caste person, hit him and abuse him! Is there no one in this village to ask for justice! Isn't justice the same for everyone! Can a bastard make someone hit and abuse him with encouragement, why should I live anymore? All the high-caste people in the village, let's go and fall into the lake’ he said, pointing towards the lake, and acted as if he was ready to fall and die. Hearing Jogi's screams, ‘Sumani is low-caste; today he sat on Jogi's chest, tomorrow he will leave us’ everyone gathered and, afraid to touch Chikkaveeragowda: they decided to wait for Veeregowda, who had gone to the Amaldar's office in Maddur, to come and deliver justice, and then they went forward to catch Sumani, and when they hugged him, Sumani kicked once and they fell down; thinking, ‘This bastard, eating and eating at the Gowda's house, is like a strong gun, like a wolf!’ they got up and were about to try again, when Chikkaveeragowda, seeing that the situation was getting out of hand, himself took Sumani to the granary in front of the village and suggested that the inquiry be held there. They reluctantly agreed. But Jogi insisted, ‘The Gowdas must tie him to the coconut tree there with a bare rope’. Chikkaveeragowda looked at Sumani. ‘Agree! Let whatever happens, happen.... Can it cut me and eat me? Come on, why are you complaining about my Chikkagowda... Come... Do whatever you want to do...’ he said, and quickly walked towards the granary... They tied him to the coconut tree... Then began the display of people's bravery....

After dozens of ungrateful women's hands, who had grown up indebted to Mastamma's breast milk, started beating Sumani, and seeing that more people were gathering, an idea flashed in Chikkaveeragowda's mind, and he quickly walked towards the house, cut the bull's rope, and chased it towards the crowd, thus preventing the unfortunate turn that this incident could have taken!.
