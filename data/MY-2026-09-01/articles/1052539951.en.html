The Idea of India and Romila Thapar

Manjushree Kadakola

Just Being- Romila Thapar

Bharathidevi P.

It was St. Mary's High School, run by the Anglican Mission in the urban area of Pune. Romila Thapar was a student there. August 15, 1947, was the day the British politically freed India from their control. On that day, they asked Romila to give a speech. The words spoken by young Romila, after deep thought, were not only like a blueprint for our independent India but also a testament to her profound understanding. She spoke about how we, who had hitherto defined our identity as 'Indians' in opposition to 'British', would understand 'Indian' in independent India, and what form our country would take as an independent nation.

Later, Romila Thapar emerged as a voice that spoke with clarity and authority, backed by documents, about ancient India, not just in India but across the world, and subtly grasped and warned about the echoes of contemporary India. She has laid bare her long life and intellectual journey in her autobiography, 'Just Being'. Her personality, studies, and the ideas she championed throughout her life appear as a concrete symbol of what we consider the 'Idea of India'.

Born in Lucknow in 1931, Romila Thapar spent her childhood in various places because her father was a military doctor. She spent her early childhood years in Lahore and Rawalpindi. The nature of British India at the time, her interactions with various families and friends, and details of communal living are repeatedly found throughout her work. Later, after completing her initial education in Pune and Delhi, her father's accidental curiosity about Chola-era bronze artifacts drew her towards history. She then applied to Oxford University for higher education and pursued her studies with a keen interest in ancient Indian history at the SOAS (School of Oriental and African Studies) department there. Later, she taught for many years at Kurukshetra University and Delhi University, and her work at JNU's CHS (Centre for Historical Studies) since its inception, along with her colleagues, in building the department through dialogue and new perspectives, is remarkable.

When Romila Thapar began her studies, two perspectives on history were prevalent. One was the unique viewpoint of colonial scholars observing the countries under their rule. The history of Asia developed under the name 'Oriental', and studies on India under the name 'Indology'. This viewed the history of these regions in two ways. One was that these countries were extremely backward, lacking historical consciousness, and that they were exotic, different from the West. The second perspective was a nationalist interpretation that arose on the same basis, attempting to counter the colonial viewpoint. Both of these provided a blurred view of history.

Grasping these limitations, Romila Thapar realized the importance of studying history in its social and economic context, beyond mere binaries of 'us' and 'them'. She became interested in how social history could be constructed not just from texts, but also from artifacts of daily use found in excavations, the nature of settlements, and other sources. Her shift towards studying history through the theories and methodologies of social sciences, beyond the traditional methods of Indology, has helped us understand ancient and medieval India from a different perspective. Thus, Romila Thapar's insights related to the Shramana tradition beyond the Brahmanical perspective, social discrimination, the feudal conditions of the time, the status of women, and migration have influenced our way of thinking.

The history of ancient India is not monolithic. It is characterized by plurality, diversity, and contradictions. Therefore, her interest lies not just in identifying time and what happened when, but in why and how. She studies with questions about how history is written, its methodology, and why and how interpretations change from time to time (historiography). Her study of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka is a good example of this. She mentions in this work the interesting discussions that took place between teachers and students during her time at Delhi University and JNU, as asking the right questions is crucial during study.

At the time of independence, our leaders and educators believed that universities should be places of free thought and expression, and that education should be accessible to all social classes. It was on this basis that universities like JNU were established. CHS began its work on this foundation. The study of history requires extensive fieldwork. She herself undertook strenuous fieldwork in many areas like Harappa, Kalibangan, Chamba, and based on these sources, she explains ancient India. Furthermore, believing that knowledge of language is important for the study of history, she introduced the learning of Sanskrit, Prakrit, and ancient Tamil languages during her time as a professor at JNU. Similarly, the application of various analytical perspectives, the use of different research methods, and continuous discussions about them made JNU stand on par with global universities. The contrary phenomenon occurring in recent decades has betrayed the very purposes for which universities were built.

In applying the theories and methods of social sciences, the views of Max Weber, Emile Durkheim, and Karl Marx primarily influenced Thapar's studies. Here, her interest lies not in their political theories, but in exploring how their methods of study and their theories can help us understand our condition. Through these perspectives, Romila Thapar's exploration of social history revealed the monolithic perception of Brahmanical dominance, social inequality, and contradictions in our society. This led to the intolerance of those who made unsubstantiated, one-sided statements about history and used them for their political gain. This is why immature statements like "she injects Marxism into history" emerged. There is a difference between upholding Marx's theory as a political ideology and using Marx's method and classification for analysis.

In the last two centuries, our country has witnessed the tendency to use history as a crutch to seize power. This Hindutva ideology has no place for civil rights, the adoption of democratic values, or the principle of secularism. Furthermore, it has largely led our country towards anti-intellectualism. Thapar expresses concern about this, stating that it lowers the quality of our education system and prevents our children from becoming truly educated. The hidden chapters of history in NCERT textbooks lead to an incomplete understanding of Indian history. This is an attempt to close the Indian mind.

The tendency to reject intellectualism and political interests has led to attacks on many thinkers in our country. We can observe the repeated discords that arose when the committee, which included Thapar, formulated the NCERT textbooks. The reasons for this intolerance were how Shudras were discriminated against in Indian social history, and the lack of emphasis on the conflict between Hindus and Muslims in history. Here, emotionally provocative matters become more important than factual evidence based on sources. The resistance expressed when Thapar was invited as the keynote speaker for the convocation of Delhi University during the Emergency was of the same kind. This has continued today, reaching an extreme where historians are marginalized, and stories circulated on social media become history. Romila believes that this situation will not change unless people become aware.

Throughout her life, Romila Thapar has spoken and written in favor of a democratic, secular society with a socialist foundation. She has clearly advocated that secularism is not just the coexistence of multiple religions, but that religion should not control social institutions. The rights of citizens and the opportunity for dissent are the foundation of a democratic country. Whenever the state tries to snatch these away, citizens must stand up. The role of intellectuals is very important here. It is on this basis that Thapar has created the work 'Lokachintakaru' (Public Thinkers) and has herself raised her voice as a public thinker. Neither the present nor ancient India can be monolithic. She extensively discusses how we should perceive these different voices and dissents. She explains how post-independence India has slowly suppressed the autonomy of citizens and various institutions.

Romila Thapar is an autonomous woman who built her life without compromising with the patriarchal system in her personal life. Although she did not speak extensively about feminism, she demonstrated it through her way of living. Parents who supported their daughter's aspirations, breaking away from traditional paths, the progressive stance of her elder brother Romesh Thapar who was a constant support, and meaningful relationships that nurtured her life are the lifeblood of Romila's full life.

In her long life of 95 years, Thapar has witnessed many milestones of independent India. Seeing liberal India turn towards fascism, she has always yearned to know what citizens and researchers can do to keep the 'Idea of India' alive. Her life and research path, concerned about how history, long past, always influences the present, seem to awaken the education sector and society that have turned their faces towards intellectualism. l
