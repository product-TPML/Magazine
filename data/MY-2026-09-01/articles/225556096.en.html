Children who always get the first rank in class don't know the joy of it. They just wear that position like a shoe they wear every day. In that September month of 2002, my situation was different. The first term exams of ninth grade had just finished. Our eyes would sharpen every time the bell for each period rang, wondering if the teachers entering the class had a bundle of papers in their hands.

If a teacher brought a bundle of exam papers to class, it meant only one thing. That day, the teacher would call out roll numbers and hand out the papers. The abilities of those who scored more than twenty marks in a twenty-five mark test and those who scored less than ten marks would be announced loudly to the entire class, along with their scores. As we each got up from our benches and took the papers from the teacher, we would receive applause or reverse claps (i.e., forearm claps for those who scored low marks) according to our scores.

The last week of that September month was such a time. English, Kannada, Hindi, Mathematics, Science, Social Studies. Along with these, Music, Drawing, Moral Science, P.E. These last four subjects were graded not in marks, but in A, B, C, D grades. The school principal would come and give a 'star' badge with a star symbol to those who scored high marks in all six main subjects and also got an A-grade in the second group of subjects. Those who came first in that term could wear that badge pinned to their chest for the next three months.

I had never come first in class. The only rule in our house was 'stay within five; don't go beyond ten'. So, I never bothered about the first rank, but it was true that after the results of every term exam came out, when Karunalo, Sneha, or Ganesha would receive applause from everyone and get that medal from the principal, something inside me would sting a little. I used to dream that just once, just once, that badge should be on my chest, I should touch that coin-sized star symbol, press it to my neck, kiss it.

It was as if some power that controlled time had heard my plea, because that time, and only that time, in the first term exam of that September month of 2002, I was very close to realizing that dream.

Kannada – 22, Hindi – 20, Maths – 22, Science – 22, Social – 23. It wasn't a very high score. But that time, Karunala's grandmother had passed away at home, so she hadn't written the Maths paper well. Sneha, due to overconfidence, had wrongly identified the right and wrong answers in Social. Something else had happened to Ganesha. Anyway, it seemed as if the planets of the solar system had aligned somehow and were ready to give me that star-symbol medal just this once.

Only the English paper marks were yet to come.

***

The English teacher was Dr. Datturi himself, the principal. He had been appointed as the principal of our school from this academic year. Datturi had several years of experience in the military. He used to wear ironed shirts, striped ties, and polished brown shoes, which were rarely seen in that small town. On important days, he would come dressed in a suit. We first saw metal cuff-links and pocket-squares matching the tie on him.

None of us knew what rank this fifty-year-old, five-and-a-half-foot tall man held in the military. We also didn't know the origin of the 'Doctor' in his name. But it was clear that the face of our school was slowly changing since he became the principal.

Classical music, moral science, and yoga once a week – these started in the very first week Dr. Datturi arrived. Teachers were strictly instructed to wear sarees, and male teachers to wear safaris. Fine-boxes were placed in every classroom and corridor to indicate that no language other than English should be spoken while on campus. Even if we started a sentence in Kannada with 'su-', we had to pay a fine of eight annas into that box. For us, who were saving every penny to buy mints or chewing gums in the school staff canteen, this 'eight annas per word' fine was a very expensive affair. So, in the first few weeks after Datturi Sir arrived, we stopped talking about non-essential things. Moreover, during Kannada and Hindi periods, we would sit pale as if committing some crime.

The most important change of all was that Datturi had installed CCTV cameras in every classroom. Until then, we had never seen such cameras, which looked like they were wearing a square cap. It would not be wrong to say that these cameras, which would be seen on every street, in every shop, and in every house in later years, first came to our school in such a small town. One Monday morning, when we went to put our bags in the classroom before prayer, it was there. There, on that wall. Right behind us. An off-white square camera blending with the off-white wall.

That day, after the prayer, Datturi spoke about it. 'CCTV cameras have been fixed in every classroom to instill punctuality and discipline in the school. Ours is the only school in entire North Karnataka with such a facility,' he said in a loud voice. We all clapped.

It was only when the principal's voice, 'School, can you hear me?' came from the direction of the camera, that we realized there was a speaker attached to that camera. That voice came within ten minutes of the first period starting. Not only us, but even Revanna Sir, who was teaching us Maths that day, was startled.

We didn't know what to do next. How to deal with just a voice without a physical person in front of us? If it were a telephone, the situation would be different.

'School, can you hear me?'
Dr. Datturi asked again.

The entire school roared, 'Yes Sir!'

'Alright, now I'll check the speaker of each class,' said the voice.

We all sat still. Since the voice was coming from the back wall, we couldn't even look at the camera and talk.

'Eighth A class... Can you hear me?'

'Eighth B class... Can you hear me?'

Hearing both adjacent classes say 'Yes Sir!', we prepared for our turn.

'Ninth A class...?'

'Yes Sir!'

That call passed Ninth B, brushed past Tenth A, called Tenth B, and finally stopped.

***

As soon as the camera came to the classroom, changes began to appear in us at the cellular level. Since no one had any idea when the principal's voice might emerge from that wall camera, we were always vigilant. Some days, the camera would not make a sound even once in the entire eight hours. Sometimes, the principal's voice came from there four times in a period for different reasons.

'Mr. Revanna, the boy on the second bench from the right, wearing a red cap. Ask him why. Tell him to take it off.'

'Miss Vanamala, look at that girl on the last bench, she's sleeping with her head on the desk. Wake her up and send her to my cabin.'

'Miss Saroja... Control the class. Speak a little louder, it seems your voice isn't reaching the last bench.'

'Mr. Bhaskar... Take the class attendance again.'

It seemed as if the principal was showing the teachers, who acted like bosses in front of us, who the real boss was. The teachers also started coming to class with a somewhat bewildered expression.

Although all this seemed fun to us at first, as days passed, an indescribable tension began to be felt. Even if we sat with a slight hunch, at any moment a warning could come from the camera, 'Attention! Don't slouch!' As soon as prayer was over, boys were not allowed to unbutton their uniform collars, and girls were not allowed to untie their tightly braided hair and loosely tie a hair tie. God forbid, we couldn't forget our tie or belt at home, and as soon as we entered the classroom, we couldn't fold our long socks up to our knees. We also couldn't sit on the last bench and apply nail polish during free periods.

Even when leaving school, walking on the road, crossing the threshold of home, and even when doing homework or watching TV, it felt as if a pair of eyes were constantly behind our backs. The TV would quickly skip Kannada channels, switch to National Geographic, stay there for a few minutes, and then turn off just as quickly.

***

One day, in English class, a voice came. 'Miss Lalita, can you come to my cabin?'

Miss Lalita was teaching us at that time. Since she was only four feet tall, she would naturally get lost in any crowd. It seemed only in the classroom that we could see her so centrally. We had no such objection to her, who taught English in a kind of simple Kannada. Many times, we would mentally correct her pronunciation and forgive her. Miss Lalita would pronounce the words 'honest' and 'honor' with the letter 'h'. She would not only read the text haltingly and incorrectly in places but also add nothing new to it. After reading two lines of the English text, the Kannada expression 'Hagendare...' (meaning 'What does that mean...') would unconsciously emerge from her tongue.

On the day she was called to the principal's office, Miss Lalita was teaching us Kamala Surayya's poem 'The Introduction'. Although all of us girls had read and internalized it several times beforehand for the thrill the poem provided, Miss Lalita, who had been teaching the same text to ninth grade for ten years, had not come to terms with the poem. When pronouncing the words 'spouted hair' and 'breast' in the poem, she would lower her voice for a moment. What possessed the boys on the last bench, they started making a ruckus, saying 'Miss, can't hear Miss'. Miss Lalita shrieked in a shrill voice, 'Quiet! Quiet!' From the last bench, a faint 'Hagendare...' tune arose. The other students in the class, who were sitting seriously, now started giggling, making Miss Lalita cry out 'Quiet!' again pitifully.

It was then that she received that call from the principal's office through the camera.

Even after Miss Lalita left the class, we all remained seated in pin-drop silence. We were also curious to see how this incident would unfold.

Finally, after ten minutes, Miss Lalita returned. There were beads of sweat on her forehead. She started Kamala Surayya's poem again from the beginning. She read two lines at a time and paused there. Her voice trembled in places. When she said, 'I am Indian,
very brown... meaning...', not a single sound rose from the entire class. That silence was more terrifying than mischief.

Outside, the sun was pouring down. The fan was whirring. Occasionally, Miss Lalita's eyes would anxiously rest on the wall camera. 'That means... that means... she is brown because she is born in Malabar,' she stammered, and then Miss Lalita completely gave up, staring at the open book, surrendering. For a moment, everything was calm. She, we, and even the camera on the wall.

'Miss Lalita... wrap up the class and come to my cabin!'

There were still at least ten minutes left for the bell to ring. It surprised us that the principal had asked Miss Lalita to finish the class so soon. As Miss Lalita walked out of the class with heavy steps, holding her vanity bag in her right hand and the English textbook in her left, her eyes were moist, and she was wearing a light yellow saree with small blue flowers.

That was the last. We never saw Miss Lalita again.

The next day, it was Datturi himself who came to the English period holding the textbook. Our hands and feet immediately went cold. The first thing he did was to cross out Kamala Surayya's name and make it 'Kamala Das'.

'She is Kamala Das. She has gone crazy and changed it to Surayya now. But she is always Kamala Das,' he said. Alright. We also changed the latter half of the poet's name in the textbook.

The quota of oddities we were to experience that day was not yet over.

It continued when Dr. Datturi started teaching the same poem titled 'The Introduction'. It took us moments to adjust to his English pronunciation, its flow, its speed, its mastery. Could English be spoken like this? Could a language be taught like this? What had we seen, heard, and been taught until now then? That day, we all stretched our necks, straightened our backs, perked up our ears, widened our eyes, and surrendered to Dr. Datturi's style of recitation.

It was the time when the campaign 'Tie a Rakhi - or tie a Mangalsutra' had started, stopping young men and women walking together on the road. The reason I remember it is that before starting the first line of the poem that day, Datturi Sir gave a ten-minute introduction. That topic also came up in the introduction. Along with it, other national and international matters came and went. Godhra, which had been in the news repeatedly for the past five or six months, came and went. Kashmir also came. Love Jihad, Reverse Jihad, everything was mentioned. Kamala Das,
'I don't know politics, but I know the name,' a series of arguments from him also took place about why she said that. None of us had formed opinions on any of these matters. Only through his words, a stir would arise as if we had discovered something within ourselves. That feeling had the power of enchantment to which we succumbed.

When that forty-minute class ended that day, Datturi had taught only one stanza of that poem. The lines 'I am Indian, very brown, born in Malabar' seemed to reflect us. By the end of that class, like Kamala Das, we too could speak three languages. We could write in two. But dreams only came in one language.

Like the emerald cuff-links Dr. Datturi wore, like the horizontally striped silk ties, like the smooth pocket squares that matched them, like the gold-colored frame of his spectacles, an unspoken charm captivated all of us.

Datturi Sir was like the embodiment of something beyond us, growing up in that small town. If he gave us a little space, he would include us in that world... It was not easy for anyone to escape such an attraction.

So, gradually, it can be said that everyone, teachers, staff, and students alike, began to surrender to him.
Except for one.

***

One person, that is them. They, that is our Salma Miss.

It was evident from her walk that Salma Miss was the youngest among all the teachers who taught us. While the other teachers would always carry heavy vanity bags on their shoulders and walk like tortoises in the corridors, as if they had just woken up from an afternoon nap, Salma Miss was always so active, as if there was a spring in her step. Salma Miss, who came wearing colorful salwar kameez and a hijab of the same color as her dupatta on her hair, taught Social Studies.

Whether it was because of her age, her beauty, her teaching style, or because she brought a bundle of checked papers to class the very next day after the exam, the name Salma Miss would come to everyone's lips as their favorite teacher, not just some students.
Salma Miss, who had been on leave for two days, that day, for the first time, saw the camera on the classroom wall and very naturally took out a red handkerchief from her sling bag and covered the camera with it. And just as simply, she started teaching by writing on the blackboard with chalk.
We felt a tremor in our stomachs. How long would it take for Salma Miss to meet the same fate as Miss Lalita?

That day, Salma Miss was supposed to teach about the first war of independence, also known as the 'Sepoy Mutiny'.

Her first question was - why might the same rebellion have been called by two names?
From the beginning, Salma Miss's way of teaching was different. She never used words like 'recalcitrant' in everyday conversation like Datturi did.

That day, Salma Miss, through questions and answers, explained the intention of those who called the same rebellion a mutiny and the fervor of those who called it the first war of independence, and how that same fervor laid the foundation for the freedom struggle that would take place decades later.
Surprisingly, throughout that period, contrary to our expectations, no sound came from the camera.

As soon as the class ended, Salma Miss
took off the handkerchief she had covered the camera with,
folded it, put it in her bag, and left.

***

We realized Salma Miss was testing some invisible boundary of the school two days after she started teaching with her red handkerchief covering the camera.

That day, teaching about the Second World War, she was extensively using the book 'War Is a Racket' written by Smedley Butler in 1930. We, who had just emerged from the slogans of the Kargil War, were curious as to why this Butler person might have written that 'war is a racket' so many years ago.

Salma Miss read out a paragraph about how the money bags of politicians, businessmen, and investors grow when a war starts, and how steel factories, gun manufacturers, shipbuilding companies, bankers, uniform manufacturers; and even shoe and button manufacturing companies make immense profits as soldiers die in large numbers, and then asked for our opinions.

The entire class remained silent for fifteen seconds. After that, the fireworks began to fall.

'Not only that, the prices of daily necessities increase.'

'How many lives must be lost to satisfy the ego of a few people?'

'So, should we just give away our country to the enemies? This Butler is talking about some other era. In this era, such theories don't work.'

'Who are the enemies?'

'Do I need to spell it out? Everyone here knows who the enemy is.'

'Don't go off-topic, children.'

'War doesn't just start. It is started.'

'More important than whose profit it is, is whose loss it is. Whose money is wasted there? The taxpayer's money, isn't it? They should vote on whether they want war or not.'

'That's what Butler says in this book in another way - the only people who should vote for war are those who will fight it - meaning the children of the poor...'

'Miss Salma. Can you come to
my cabin?'

The sound that came from inside the speaker of the covered camera literally shook us that day. The entire class was stunned.

As Salma Miss left for the principal's room, we trembled, wondering if she would return to class.

We waited for her return, hardly breathing.

Within seven or eight minutes, Salma Miss came to class. As soon as she arrived, she smiled at all of us.

Immediately, she put Butler's book aside, opened the textbook, and came to the main lesson. As usual, she didn't read a single word from it. Instead, she explained what the situation in Europe was like before the Second World War began.

Having heard about the First World War just the day before, we started our discussion with the 'Treaty of Versailles'.

According to that treaty, most of the losses caused by the First World War were imposed on Germany. The entire Germany was drowned in debt. Its people were economically and psychologically depressed... all these fireworks exploded in the class that day.

'A mass hysteria, where people are willing to burn themselves to push others into the fire, is created under such pressure. The rise of people like Adolf Hitler, who intensely advocated nationalism...'

'Miss Salma, see me in my cabin after class.'

'Yes Sir. Will do.'

We were all so engrossed in the discussion that it seemed as if this conversation hadn't even happened. The Great Depression of 1929, the appeasement policy adopted by Britain and France to pacify Hitler in 1936-38, the Munich Agreement, all came and went in the class that day.

'That's true. The real war started in 1939 when Germany took over Poland. The roots of the Second World War had begun to sink into the ground much, much earlier than that.'

Just then, the bell rang, and Salma Teacher stopped speaking. Before leaving, she held up Butler's book 'War Is a Racket' and said, 'This is a small but equally important book. If anyone wants to read it, you know where to ask,' and then folded her handkerchief from the camera, put it in her bag, and walked out of the class.

In the following days, we learned very important lessons in history, geography, and civics in Salma Miss's class, in the form of discussions.

As soon as she came to class, she would cover the classroom camera with her red handkerchief and start teaching. The principal would also call her to his cabin occasionally. We never had the opportunity to find out what they talked about. But Salma Teacher, returning to class, would give a big, hearty laugh and continue her lesson without any hindrance.

However, a different kind of 'Cold War' began in Dr. Datturi's English class. In that war, Datturi was certainly in the position of the plaintiff. But none of us were clear about who all were in the position of the defendant.

Usually, Mathematics, Science, and Social Studies subjects were in the first session, that is, before the lunch bell. English, Kannada, Hindi, P.E., and other subjects were all in the second session.

Datturi would subtly and indirectly start refuting in his class in the evening the topics Salma Miss had raised in her class discussion in the morning.

On the day Salma Miss said, 'It's very difficult to live like Gandhi. The mere fact that such a person actually existed creates the illusion that such a life is easy,' two hours later, Datturi would interject into some topic, 'Father, they say father. What kind of father is that Gandhi?'

Following the Social Studies lesson that 'if ultra-nationalism is not identified at the first stage, the entire country will silently turn towards fascism,' we would have to hear the lesson in English class that 'controlling the people of a large country like India is an art. All the tools used for it are unimportant things.'

To the question, 'Truth has many dimensions. Immediate truth is one. But 'higher truth' is above that. Then which one do you choose?', we would have to hear the answer in English class, 'Truth and falsehood are like tossing a coin. Either it is this, or it is that. What is all this higher-lower nonsense?'

As days passed, our English class also turned into another Social Studies class. Datturi's struggle, determined to swiftly demolish the bricks Salma Miss had carefully laid, was beyond our comprehension.

***

That day in September 2002, a Saturday. During the sports period, as I stretched the badminton shuttlecock in my left hand, held it at eye level, and swung the racket with my right hand to serve, I knew it immediately. I quickly handed the racket to my classmate and carefully walked upstairs.

Although it had been two years since I started menstruating, it usually happened when girls wore white uniforms like this. No girl would keep a pad in her bag. We would feel shy, wondering where it might fall out from when taking out a notebook, and have to face the gaze of the boys in class.

I walked straight to the staffroom. What was never there, that day the door was secured from the inside. I gently knocked on the door with the latch and waited.

After four or five minutes, the person who opened the door was Salma Teacher. As usual, she gave her full smile. That day, her cheeks were even rosier and she looked more beautiful than ever. A strand of her silky shining forelock had escaped from her loosely tied hijab and curled beside her ear.

'Yes?'

'I needed a pad, Miss.'

Salma Miss was a little flustered.

'Oh God! Is this your first
time?'

It took me a moment to understand what she had asked.

'No, no,' I said.

Now she was a little relieved.

'Is your skirt stained? Shall I give you my sweater to tie around your waist?'

'No. All safe. I just need a pad.'

Still not satisfied, she said 'Just to be sure' and asked me to turn around once where I stood. Only after checking was she completely satisfied.

'So you just need a pad.'

'Yes.'

'Just a minute.'

I stood at the door, waiting for her to return. My eyes effortlessly turned towards the camera installed inside the staffroom. A red handkerchief was draped over that camera.
For some reason, that day I realized there was another person in the staffroom. Since that day was Nagara Panchami or Bheemana Amavasya, almost all the other lady teachers were absent. So, I was wondering who that other person inside was, when that person coughed a little and cleared their throat.

That was enough for me. To recognize that he was Bhaskar Sir, who taught us Kannada.

Bhaskar Sir and Salma Miss were friends who called each other by their first names. I had heard some students say that they were classmates in college or university. Another group would correct that news, saying that they were not classmates, but their families used to live next door to each other once. Whenever they stood and talked in the corridor, the fact that both of them were unmarried was the most thrilling thing for us, who were just entering adolescence.

That day, as I was removing the sticker from the back of the pad in the restroom, I thought that Salma Miss must have been showing her beautiful red hair to Bhaskar Sir by closing the door, and I just laughed to myself. Later, I forgot about it.

***

I had to face that memory again on Monday. That evening, as Dr. Datturi finished class and left, he called me by my name. Since he never seemed to remember anyone's name, it surprised all my classmates, including me. Even more surprising was that he was smiling when he called me.

It was that day we learned that Datturi Sir, who always looked as if 'a mustard seed would explode if it fell', could also smile.

He was waiting for me in the corridor, a little outside the classroom. I approached him with a slight jog.

'You wrote the English paper very well!'

'Thank you, Sir.'

'It means you listen to the lesson properly.'

I thought it was good that I had used the word 'ambiguous' while writing the answer in the paper. I felt like I was about to touch the ribbon in a hundred-meter race. A smile might have appeared on my face too.

After thanking him, as I turned to leave, he stopped me for a moment, as if he had forgotten something.

'By the way, you went to the staffroom on Saturday, didn't you?'

'Yes... I had to ask Salma Miss something.'

'Oh... good, good... Mr. Bhaskar must have been in the room too, right?'

Datturi had asked that question in such a natural voice, as if it was not a big deal at all. But at that very moment, some window or door, I don't know which, rattled loudly, making a thudding sound, shaking the fourteen-year-old girl to her roots.

Though I almost stumbled, I regained my balance. If a close friend had asked this question, I might have answered comfortably, and even gossiped a bit. But...

Almost stopping my breathing, I used all my life's energy to produce a sound from my throat, and said, 'I'm not sure, Sir.'

He said with a kind of indifference, 'Oh... good, good. Study well,' and smiled again, walking towards his cabin.

***

That entire evening, everything inside me was still, as if I hadn't taken a deep breath even once. Soumya, sitting next to me, also noticed my behavior and asked, 'Why are you sitting like you're possessed?'

Just ten minutes before the last period was about to end, while Bhaskar Sir was teaching Kannada, the school peon came and called my name, saying, 'The principal is calling.'

I got up and walked with a kind of weariness.

As I turned into the corridor, I saw Salma Miss standing in front of the principal's cabin. As soon as she saw me in the distance, she turned and faced the playground.

'May I come in, Sir?'

It was the same smiling Datturi Sir.

'Please come in.'

That 'cabin', which I had heard about a hundred and eight times only in conversation, was furnished like the district collector's office I had only seen in pictures. The swivel chair attached to the glass-covered table had a leather cover. Dr. Datturi's posture, the clothes he wore, the fingers holding the pen, were like sharp lines drawn by an artist. Only the pile of exam papers in front of him seemed to struggle greatly to fit into that environment.

'I was checking your class's paper. See... isn't this your paper?'

Immediately, my eyes turned to the right edge of the paper. How much would they give? To surpass Karuna, who always came first, I needed at least twenty-three.

He, with equal speed, wrote the number 'Two' in English on my paper. He looked up as if he had forgotten the next number.

'By the way...'

'Yes, Sir?'

'You said you went to the staffroom on Saturday, didn't you?'

'Yes, Sir.'

'Was Miss Salma there?'

'Yes.'

'Was she alone?'

'Might have been.'

'Was it latched?'

I'm not sure.'

'You knocked on the door, didn't you?'

'Yes.'

'So it was latched, wasn't it?'

'Might have been.'

'Was Miss Salma alone in the staffroom?'

'It seems so.'

'Mr. Bhaskar was also inside, wasn't he?'

'I don't think so, Sir.'

Datturi's smile faded from his face. His face without a smile gave a more secure feeling.

He closed the open pen. Now my eyes fell on my paper where the number 'Two' was written, and on the five or six 'star' badges placed next to it.

It was then that I first realized that not only when seeing a favorite food, but also when any highly desired object was in front of me, my mouth would literally water.

'Miss Kadham...'

By calling the corrupted form of my surname, he seemed to have dragged my village, my community, and my family into that room.

'I have installed CCTVs in every corner of this school. If there was video recording facility, I wouldn't have needed to call you here. I know they both were in that room. I know it was latched. All I need is for you to say - yes, they were there.'

He was speaking in a voice I had never heard before. No words came from me.

'What do you say? They were there, weren't they?'

'I'm sorry, Sir. But I don't think so.'

Something within me, whose origin was unclear, hidden somewhere inside, still struggling to form, was directing me throughout this incident, beyond my control. In all this, Salma Miss was merely a pretext, and the words formed as if this was just an argument between me and Datturi.

He suddenly stood up from where he was sitting. He didn't move for a whole minute, as if someone had tied him in that posture.

'Is that a no, Miss Kadham?'

'It is a no, Sir.'

He sat back heavily on the chair and opened his pen.

'Why did you go to the staffroom?'

'...I needed to ask for a pad.'

'What?'

'I had got my periods. I needed to ask for a sanitary pad.'

As if he hadn't heard that, he wrote a zero in front of the 'Two' on my paper. He circled that zero twice.

'You will never succeed in life. I'll write it down for you if you want!' he said.

I hadn't even exhaled completely, when a louder voice startled me even more.

'Get out!'

Like someone freed from a curse, I turned back.

'Not just you. Tell her, who is waiting outside, to go home too.'

As my back crossed the Dutch door of his room, he used a word. That word brushed past the ironed pleat of my uniform and slipped away.

Coming out, I told Salma Miss, 'You don't need to wait. He said you can go home.'

A faint smile appeared on Salma Miss's face.

With our backs to the camera installed above the principal's cabin, we both walked along the length of that pipe-like corridor.

***

After that, not much changed. Salma Miss did not stop covering the classroom camera with her red handkerchief when she taught. Datturi Sir continued to teach Social Science in his English class for a few days. After a few days, a new English teacher joined, and Datturi Sir stopped coming to class in any form other than his voice.

About a year and a half later, on the day of the tenth-grade farewell, Salma Miss gave me a small box.

When I opened it, there was a bronze locket inside. It had an image of two doves flying out of a long pipe carved on it. That locket, which makes me want to touch it again and again, and is so adorable that I want to press it to my cheek, is still with me.l
