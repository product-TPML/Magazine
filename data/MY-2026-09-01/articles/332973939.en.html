A.K. Ramanujan's poems: The incessant search for Svabhavokti

K. Phaniraj

This note can begin with three statements by A.K. Ramanujan. The notes are as follows:

1.Poems contain reality, change it, and give us back our reality; they expose us to reality obliquely.' (About poems
A.K. Ramanujan. Gilmaro Rodrigues' 'When Mirrors Are Windows:
A View of Ramanujan's Poetics' epigraph, p. vi)

2.'These are still nascent poems. Though there are feelings, they are not words. They must become introspective, take the form of images, manifest concretely; above all, they must successfully withstand the test of irony. Remove poetic conventions like 'heart', 'soul', 'love', 'horizon'. Untangle and bury obvious rhythms. Don't say what can be said in another way.'
(A.K. Ramanujan's advice to a young poet after reading the draft of his poems. See: Chirantan Kulshrestha, 'A.K. Ramanujan: A Profile', Journal of South Asian Literature, Vol. 16 Issue 2, Miscellany (Summer, Fall) 1981, pp. 181–184)

3.'I don't know if 'personal' is the right word; because what is expressed in poems is not the view of a (specific) 'person', but the view of a 'personality'. (In poems) it is not Ramanujan's words that are heard, ultimately it is an imagined voice; it may only partially represent my (words), but not fully.' (Conversation with Ayyappa Paniker. Sahitya Akademi's journal 'Indian Literature', Vol. 53 Issue 6 (254), November–December 2009, pp. 171–187)

Keeping the above references in mind, the three philosophical characteristics of A.K. Ramanujan's poems can be identified as follows:

1.Ramanujan is a poet with a rare 'modern' sensibility among Kannada poets. For life in South Asia, 'modern sensibility' means: critically examining traditions and heritage without considering them as universal truths, being loyal to autonomous independent thought while understanding pluralism with acceptance, advocating social equality secularly, and creating paths of knowledge by exposing oneself to intellectual and social changes.

2.As a condition for these special qualities of modernity, in Ramanujan's poetic sensibility – a strong doubt about 'individual originality',
'self-identity', and a unique existentialist sensibility is woven in, where an individual's life in social co-existence is intertwined with many lives, and one's 'personality' 'exists' as a 'blended personality' that emerges from the intertwining of many lives. This kind of 'modern existentialist' sensibility is also the uniqueness of Ramanujan's poetry.

3.For Ramanujan, like Bhartrhari, language is not a creation of God, nor a creation of man, but an eternally existing 'Shabda Brahma'; language is not merely a medium of communication, it is a visionary power that can explore the natural characteristics of objects and subjects in the world. However, Ramanujan is skeptical whether such visionary power of language can be obtained merely through language use. Whatever the language, the visionary knowledge of language certainly cannot be stimulated by rhetorical devices such as similes, metaphors, rasa-prasanga appropriateness; it is true that the poetic principle of 'Svabhavokti', which is the power to grasp and describe the 'nature' (their original natural characteristic) of events of objects, time, and space, brings us very close to the visionary quality of language; can language bring to our awareness the 'nature' of emotions and experiences that manifest in the complexities of human daily relationships? Does the language we speak have the power to express 'nature'? Can humans, who fail to truly communicate the 'nature' of experiences gained through the senses through language, express the nature of mind-related emotions and experiences in language? These problems are the foundation of Ramanujan's poems. To embody in language the nature of experiences accessible through the senses, and to create language in which mental emotions and experiences

manifest their nature in the form of sensory perceptions

– these are Ramanujan's

incessant tasks in poetry.

Now let's look at three of Ramanujan's poems:

Ramanujan does not use irony or adjectives in his poems; he breaks words, emotions, and rhythms; he separates the suffixes attached to nouns and verbs in grammatical usage and arranges them in the stanza as if they were separate words; he makes each stanza an indicator of everyday human actions; a concrete action design can be seen in all his poems; he seems not to have written a single poem (in Kannada or English) that does not manifest as a linguistic image. In his first two Kannada poetry collections, each poem has a consistent title, but he stopped giving titles to the poems in his third collection. These are incessant attempts to achieve Svabhavokti in the process of language.

Here, three poems published in Kannada have been selected as examples of Ramanujan's poetic creativity. The first and third poems are taken from the collection 'Mattū Itara Padyagaḷu' (And Other Poems), and the second poem is taken from the collection 'Kuṇṭōbille' (Hopscotch).

There is no objection to saying that the first untitled poem is the motto of Ramanujan's poetic work. The goal of all his poems is to 'touch the mind as if touching the body' in language creation, and to convey the 'nature of emotion'. It is clear that the 'God' indicated in this poem is not God in the conventional sense, but 'Shabda Brahma', the visionary power of language. The critical depiction of not being able to see the conventionally human-made imagined God in any human action can be found in 'Sandēhada Bhakti' (Devotion of Doubt) in the 'Kuṇṭōbille' collection. Whatever the nature of elemental nature, the knowledge and vision that human world can gain through the knowledge of natural sciences, the poem 'Vartamāna' (Present) from the 'Kuṇṭōbille' collection, which deals with the knowledge that is difficult to understand the nature of objects and emotions that are part of human daily life, can be read along with the first poem given here.

'One can cross the Amazon.
How to cross this daily inner Ganga?
How to touch this horizon within the eyes?'

The last stanza of this poem is famous.

The poem under the title 'Padyada Mātu Bēre' (The Word of the Poem is Different) also deals with the difficult relationship between language and human emotional comprehension. Here two human actions are described: one, reading received letters and finding it safer not to reply because the nature of the emotion in them cannot be understood; the second, after attempting to sift through linguistic archives to find the possible (meaning) or impossible (meaninglessness) emotions in abstract poems, and still not finding the nature of those emotions, the action of concretely applying the literal meaning of language to a concrete action and saying 'Look, this is the nature'. If a poem, where 'word is substance' (writing/reading), fails to express 'reality', and 'reality' can only be obtained by experimenting with 'substance' as a concrete action, then what is the purpose of a poem (writing/reading)?

Ramanujan was not just someone who tried to embody 'Shabda Brahma' in his own poems; he was skeptical about his own creations, and he sought and gathered the visionary power of language in the pluralistic literary traditions of India; he tried to see the life-giving roots of language in the work of translating Sangam literature, Bhakti literature, and folk literature into English. In the diversity of hundreds of Ramayanas that emerged beyond the Sanskrit epic of 'Ramayana', he sought the source of the nature of emotion in the relationship between language and emotion. That is why Ramanujan wrote the essay 'Three Hundred Ramayanas: Five Examples and Three Thoughts on Translation' in 1991. This essay was included in the syllabus for undergraduate classes at Delhi University in 2006. Against this, a right-wing student organization raised a ruckus, claiming 'this insults the religious beliefs of Hinduism', and insisted that the essay be removed from the syllabus; they even went to court; although the university's syllabus committee decided not to remove the essay, the university administration, bowing to pressure, removed the essay from the syllabus. Ramanujan was not alive to see this kind of 'reality' manifest as 'word is substance'. If he had been alive, what would he have felt seeing the sensibility of 'the word of the poem is different' become a concrete lesson of Tenali Rama, transcending the 'meaning' and 'meaninglessness' of language that could lead to such 'reality'?

Ramanujan did not hide his socio-political views in his poems; nor did he implicitly hold opposing views to validate his own perspective; is this an attempt to show the nature of Ramanujan's era, where he places his worldview alongside different perspectives and brings them face to face in language? This is left to the reader's discretion.

Readers may not agree with the poet's perspective. But, can one deny the dignity of a poem that, without expressing irony about differing views, constructs a concrete image of the inner relationships of past and present in the dynamic structure of words, and stimulates a vision of the nature of 'different light' emerging from the reflection of human skin's 'lampshade'? Can one deny it and understand the nature of social emotion that manifests in any time and place?
