Half-century 'Lotus' coin

This is a 'Lotus' coin from more than half a century ago, minted and put into circulation by the Reserve Bank of India in 1970-1971. This coin, made of nickel-brass alloy, was found in a temple's offering box. A devotee, who had preserved this coin for so many years, put it in the offering box of my acquaintance's temple. The coin was discovered during the counting of the offering box money.

This is a coin from when I was a child. It is still sturdy and seems like it will last for many years. I still remember jewelers clamoring for this coin back then. Perhaps they were mixing it with gold or adulterating it in jewelry making...!

–Nagara Gurudev Bhandarkar, Hosanagara

Mist-covered 'Grandma's Cloth'

The spider web, which looks like a white handkerchief spread on the grass, is also called 'Grandma's Cloth'. This is a sign that the rainy season has ended and winter is approaching in the hilly forests of Malenadu. The artistic skill of the spiders is hidden behind this web, which they weave overnight for insect food. They shine like a mirror reflecting the morning sun. The collection of spider webs spread on the ground is an indicator of seasonal change.

–Dattatreya Bhat Kannipal, Yellapur

The Vanished Comfortable Chair

At one time, the comfortable chairs seen in the picture were found in most homes. It would not be wrong to say that they have vanished today. The elders of the house used to sit in these chairs, read newspapers, listen to the radio, and rest for a while after lunch. In the evening, sitting in the front yard of the house and talking with neighbors was a delightful sight.

That chair was not just a tool for sitting. It was also a symbol of the elders' dignity and authority. If grandfather sat in that chair, there would be a disciplined atmosphere throughout the house. Now all that has disappeared. Modern sofas, chairs, divans, and recliners have found a place in homes. Chairs are available for sitting. To bring dignity to that chair, joint families have broken up in homes, or for various reasons, there are no grandfathers and grandmothers anymore.

These chairs, made of strong wood, were simple to look at but comfortable to sit on. They could be folded. They could be easily moved. They could be taken anywhere outside the house, in the courtyard, on the veranda, on the terrace, and set up to sit.

–Sharad Kulkarni, Bidar

Oleander Hawk-Moth

The image shows the Oleander Hawk-Moth (Daphnis nerii), which attracts attention with its unique color and design. Its wings have a design mixed with green, light green, brown, and pink, which reminds many nature lovers of a military uniform, hence it is also called 'Army Green Moth'.

It is a large moth belonging to the Sphingidae (hawk-moth) family, and its wingspan is usually 9 to 12 cm. The wavy green design on its wings easily camouflages it among the leaves. This color is not just for beauty; it is a boon given by nature to protect itself from enemies.

The Oleander Hawk-Moth is more active at night. It has the ability to hover in one place to suck nectar from flowers. This action resembles the flight of a hummingbird. With the help of a long needle-like proboscis, it sucks nectar even from deep tubular flowers. It is commonly found in India, as well as parts of Asia, Africa, and Europe.

–Dr. Maithri Bhat, Vittal

Closed Cattle Shed

When humans make mistakes, they are put in jail as punishment. Similarly, a 'cattle shed' was a place to confine cattle that committed mischief. When grazing cattle went into someone's field and destroyed crops, the field owner had the option of punishing them by confining them in a cattle shed. Such cattle sheds, usually located in the central places of large villages, have now permanently closed their doors due to the difficulty of their maintenance.

Naughty cattle that jumped fences, and mischievous cattle that destroyed crops in the village, were put into these sheds, given little water and fodder for a few days to bring them under control. There was also a system of collecting a maintenance fee from the owners as a fine when they came to take their cattle home. This entire process served as a lesson for the cattle owner to guard his cattle and prevent such mistakes in the future. It also benefited the farmers who grew crops. The sheds were maintained in such a way that there was no room for disagreement between the cattle owner and the farmers who suffered crop loss due to mischievous cattle. Now, most of them have closed their doors everywhere.

–Archana G. Bomnalli, Uttara Kannada

Siddharoodha Math's Cooking Utensils

Siddharoodha Math in Hubballi is a place of communal harmony. Its tradition of 'Dasoha' (community feeding) has a history of centuries. Siddharoodha himself started the Dasoha tradition so that devotees who came to the Math should not return hungry. Now, the Math provides 'Annaprasada' (food offering) three times a day, and it is the pride of the Math that it provides food to thousands of devotees daily. The Dasoha system here is very hygienic. Rice is cooked using boilers and gas. There is an automatic machine (dishwasher) for washing plates. Although the kitchen is ultra-modern, the Math committee has carefully preserved the copper, brass, and silver vessels, measuring pots, and large cooking pots that were used at the beginning of the Dasoha. Each vessel is given a number, and they are neatly arranged on shelves built in the vegetable room here. These narrate every stage of the Dasoha culture's journey here.

–Swaroop Kottur, Vijayanagara
