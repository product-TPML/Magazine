Craving is not just for eating, it is also possible to satisfy cravings by adding food items to the words we speak, and speaking them in between words is proof of this. Without our knowledge, we add so many food items to our conversations. Let's look at a small list of food items that come and go in such conversations or songs, one by one.

If someone doesn't know anything, what we say is, 'What does he know about brinjal?' Or if someone else has become intelligent just by reading books, we say, 'He is just a bookish brinjal.' If someone has become dark from walking in the sun, we say, 'Oh, look at his face, he looks like a burnt brinjal from walking in the sun.' Here, why brinjal is a question. It seems even brinjal doesn't know why no other vegetable is included instead.

If someone has too much arrogance, it is common to say, 'How much tamarind sourness does he have?' It is not known what the connection is between arrogance and tamarind. We have seen small children who are very active or smart being called 'small chili' or 'spicy chili'. To someone who is considered a bit dull, we say, 'If you study like this, you will get a pumpkin in the exam.' Here, pumpkin is a symbol of a big zero!

If we say, 'You should see his face when he gets angry, it becomes like a red, red tomato,' then if he is happy, which is the opposite emotion of anger, we say, 'He has become a ridge gourd from happiness.' Here, the comparison of a red face to a tomato is correct, but it is not known why it should be a ridge gourd when happy. And when those who were expecting something from someone finally get nothing, they say, 'You got the mango seed.'

When children are being mischievous, their father or mother says, 'If you make noise like this, I will give you two kadubu on your back.' 'Kadubu' here does not mean the edible kadubu, but two blows on the back, and the children understand this and become quiet.

When talking about thieves being caught by the police, if we say, 'The police fed the thieves kajjaya properly,' then the thieves feed the police 'challihannu'. It is not known in which village or market this challihannu is found.

When someone is about to threaten someone else loudly and raise their hand, it is common to say, 'He thinks he can hit me? Does he think I'll be eating groundnuts all that time?' and roll up their sleeves. Here, perhaps, if they are quiet, it means they are eating groundnuts!

If someone else's disliked person or enemy dies, we hear people say, 'He jumped and fell and became a gooseberry.'

The funny thing is, sometimes we go to ask someone for a loan. They themselves might have invested in some business and gone bankrupt. When we ask such people for money, they say, 'My life has become chitranna, where can I give it to you from?' Why should life become chitranna? Perhaps it's because chitranna is very simple? If that same business had been profitable, life would probably have been pulao or bisibele bath! Since these dishes are rich with many vegetables, cashews, and spices, it is my opinion that they could have said that life has become bisibele bath or pulao.

All the examples mentioned above apply not only to men but also to women. However, in movie songs, the comparison of fruits and vegetables mostly applies to women. In songs describing the beauty of the heroine, many fruits and vegetables can be compared to her eyes, nose, and cheeks.

'Look, this girl like a lemon has come...' and 'There's an apple on her cheek, a ivy gourd on her lips, pomegranate seeds in her mouth...' - poets have compared the heroine's lips to ivy gourd and her teeth to pomegranate seeds while describing her beauty.

'A young woman with sugarcane eyebrows...' Here, the heroine's eyebrows are compared to sugarcane. This comparison might be for rhyme or because the eyebrows are bent like sugarcane stalks. Overall, many fruits and vegetables can be seen on the heroine's face. In this way, for one reason or another, we keep feeding the food items we love to eat every day to the words we speak, don't we?g
