Dr. Brahmananda Nayak

A fifty-two-year-old man from R.T. Nagar came to my clinic. In his hand, a blood test report, a protruding belly, and self-confidence that surpassed both.‌

“Doctor, I have no problems,” he said.

His blood pressure disagreed. His blood sugar, triglycerides, waist circumference – none of them testified in his favor. Only his gasping breath, after walking just five hundred meters, had already revealed the truth.

He was not a negligent person. He renewed his car insurance on time. He got his AC serviced before summer. He changed the water purifier's filter. He even bought an extended warranty for his fridge. But he had forgotten to maintain his own body, which took care of all these things.

There was a reason he came to the clinic. A school friend had suddenly died at the age of fifty-three. Until then, death was a distant topic, like climate change or a solution to Bangalore's traffic problem. Now it had a familiar face. There was the name of the person standing next to him in an old group photo. There was a condolence message on WhatsApp.

“We met just last month,” he said softly.

Middle age doesn't begin at forty or fifty. It truly begins the day a familiar name appears in the news of death.

Lifestyle Bills

Middle age is not the prelude to old age. It's the time when the bills for past lifestyles start coming home. Late-night meals, postponed sleep, cigarettes smoked only for company, exercise postponed to every Monday, stress carried as a sign of great responsibility – all now demand an account. The body is still generous. It corrects. It adapts. It forgives. But it no longer silently erases every mistake as it used to.

The goal should not just be to live longer. Postponing the day when you need someone's hand to get up from a chair, not forgetting a familiar road, opening a medicine bottle, buttoning a shirt, going to and from the bathroom independently – these are the true measures of longevity. How many years we have lived is our lifespan. How long in those years our life was our own is healthy lifespan. The gap between these two is largely determined in middle age.

First, know your body's statistics. Blood pressure, HbA1c, cholesterol, triglycerides, weight, and waist circumference – these are not attractive subjects. The cardiac intensive care unit is also not an attractive place. Also, know who in your family had diabetes, heart disease, stroke, cancer, or memory loss, and at what age it appeared. The feeling that "I am fine" may give comfort; it cannot give a certificate of no disease. Diabetes and high blood pressure are very polite guests. They don't knock on the door for years. They don't make noise. They come straight in and occupy the best room in the house.

Don't just trust the weighing machine. Some people boast that their weight at fifty is the same as at forty. But a big transaction might have happened inside the body. Muscle might have melted away, and belly fat might have occupied its place. The scale doesn't know this deception. Is the old belt moving towards its last hole? Do you gasp for breath when climbing stairs? Can you get up from a chair without using your hands? The body gives its annual report like this even before the laboratory report.

Preserve your muscles

After forty-five, walking alone is not enough. A woman from Chamundinagar used to walk to the temple every day. Sometimes twice. But if she sat on the floor, she couldn't get up without holding a chair. Her heart was ready to walk; her thigh muscles had resigned.

With age, muscles silently waste away. The first sign of this doesn't have to appear in the gym. It can appear when carrying a vegetable bag, climbing bus steps, or getting up from the floor. Do muscle-strengthening exercises at least two days a week. You don't have to be a bodybuilder. You don't have to buy expensive equipment and then turn it into a clothes drying stand. Getting up repeatedly from a chair, squats, push-ups against a wall, step-ups, resistance bands, and lifting weights appropriate for your strength are enough. Muscles control sugar, protect bones and joints, and maintain balance. Longevity is not about adding more candles to the cake; it's about retaining enough strength to carry that cake ourselves.

Maintain balance

A healthy sixty-year-old man from Anandnagar knew his cholesterol level down to the decimal point. But he lost his balance while putting on his pants standing up and fell. Standing on one leg with support, walking with heel-to-toe steps, and doing exercises that strengthen the feet – these are simple but invaluable practices. Correct vision problems. Ensure good lighting in the bathroom. Don't leave objects scattered on the floor. One moment is enough to fall; but its consequences can change the lives of the entire family.

Don't complicate food. When it comes to this, every Indian household has at least four experts. Breakfast disappears in the stress of work. Lunch goes into the stomach in front of a laptop. In the evening, biscuits and mixtures come with tea; a serious declaration of "light dinner tonight" also accompanies them. But at ten o'clock at night, dinner enters like a wedding procession. We don't just eat more at meal times; we forget when to stop, starting from the morning.

A normal meal with vegetables, greens, lentils, grains, fruits, nuts, and plenty of protein is best. Depending on your diet and health, you can get protein from lentils, sprouts, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, or meat. Half the plate vegetables, a quarter protein food, and the remaining quarter rice, roti, or other grains – this is a simple rule to remember. Reduce sugary drinks, bakery snacks, fried foods, and packaged snacks.

No advertisement for guava

Good food looks very ordinary. There is no influencer behind guava. There is no brand ambassador for lentils. Greens don't advertise "awakens the youth of cells"; they are busy being greens. Those who bargain for five rupees for ten rupees worth of coriander leaves will buy "anti-aging" powder for ₹1,800 without a second thought. We don't know how much that powder will change health; but the monthly budget will definitely change. Longevity food not found in Sultanpalya's vegetable market also doesn't last long in daily life.

Ayurveda did not view old age as an event starting from a birthday. It described it as a gradual change in body strength, digestive power, tissues, senses, and recovery capacity. Its advice is not continuous fasting; nor aggressive purification. Preserving digestive power, avoiding excess, maintaining body strength, and eating only as much as can be digested – this is its essence. Middle age needs moderate nutrition; not starving all week and attacking a buffet on the weekend.

Stay away from addiction

Stay completely away from tobacco; don't be under any illusion about alcohol. I once saw a person in a tea shop seriously say no to sugar, and immediately light a cigarette. It was like throwing out a small thief and seating a robber in the chair. Cigarettes, beedis, gutka – in whatever form, tobacco steals not only lifespan but also the years of healthy living. No herb, antioxidant, or detox drink can successfully negotiate peace with daily tobacco.

Don't consider alcohol a heart medicine. In middle age, problems with sleep, blood pressure, liver, and weight may already exist. Adding alcohol to that and calling it relaxation is like celebrating that the ventilation has improved by loosening the roof tiles of the house.

Keep your brain active

Give your brain new tasks. Give life a purpose. Learn something – music, language, gardening, crafts, digital skills. Read a book that contradicts your opinion. See the place more than the photos during travel. A retired bank officer started learning music after sixty. He had no special talent. That was even better; his brain had to work harder.

Have a reason to wake up tomorrow morning. It doesn't have to be as grand as writing a biography. Watering six plants, mentoring one student, helping at a temple, calling lonely relatives – that's enough. It's a great tragedy to feel that someone who was needed by everyone for forty years is not needed by anyone after retirement. A long life without purpose can become a well-equipped waiting room.

Notice small problems while they are still small. Don't neglect eyes, ears, teeth, vaccinations, and age- and risk-appropriate health check-ups. Buying every laboratory package is not a check-up. A check-up is doing the right test for the right person at the right time and taking action based on the results. People get even a silently running car serviced on time. But they bring their body to the doctor only after it starts making noise.

Manage stress before it becomes your personality. Pranayama, meditation, prayer, walking, music, intimate conversations, and psychotherapy if needed – all are helpful. But don't use these only to tolerate a life that urgently needs change. Sometimes the cure is not another breathing exercise. It's saying "no" to the situation that is tormenting us. A difficult conversation. Less debt. Saying goodbye to some responsibilities. A job change. A boundary around our time and peace.

The longevity industry doesn't like such common advice. It wants to sell biological age tests, IV vitamins, detox powders, hormone shortcuts, and capsules that restore youth to cells. Some treatments may be necessary for suitable individuals. But no pill can fix lack of sleep, smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, declining body strength, and withered relationships. We want some medicine to fix everything without changing our lifestyle.

The man who came to me asked if there was any medicine to increase lifespan. Instead of a pill, I suggested three simple changes – controlling blood pressure and sugar, doing muscle-strengthening exercises two days a week, and going to bed half an hour earlier every night. I said, let these become part of your routine first; then you can adopt the remaining changes one by one.

If you try to change your life in a single Sunday, your body will ache on Monday. By Wednesday, the resolution will die. Longevity is not built from heroic Sundays. It is built from ordinary Tuesdays that come again and again.

Age keeps moving; no one has the power to stop it. The question middle age poses to us is different.

How should old age be?

Once old age comes, you cannot choose its health. That choice is happening now – in every meal, walk, sleep, and habit. The only person who can protect your health tomorrow is – you today.

Physical activity is needed

Walk regularly; be a little brisk in your step. One morning, hundreds of people were walking near Hebbal lake. Some walked briskly; their arms moved rhythmically, and their breathing was a little faster. Still others moved so slowly that their shadows seemed to overtake them. One person was engrossed in office talk on their mobile until they completed four rounds. It's true they walked around the lake; but it seemed more like a continuation of an office meeting than exercise.

At least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week is necessary. Your breath should increase a little; yet you should be able to talk to the person next to you. Brisk walking, cycling, swimming, dancing, climbing stairs – choose what you like. A workout plan that remains only in your mind offers no benefit to the heart. Walking becomes exercise only when the lungs also participate.

An hour's walk in the evening cannot completely erase ten hours of sitting. Get up occasionally. Use stairs if possible. Our ancestors considered it an insult to use a vehicle for a distance that could be reached by walking. Such small movements don't attract as much attention as gym selfies. But the body doesn't need publicity.

Respect sleep

Sleep is medicine that doesn't come in a bottle. Perhaps that's why we don't understand its value. Even at midnight, blue light can be seen in the windows of Kemapura apartments. Someone is replying to office emails. Someone is watching the fourth episode of a series whose first three episodes they didn't even like. Another is losing their health for tomorrow by looking at other people's vacation photos. In the morning, they wake up and say, 'I'm old, my energy has decreased.'

Sleep is not time stolen from productivity. It's the time when the brain, hormones, immune system, and metabolism do their repair work. Let bedtime be as regular as possible. Let the morning sun touch your body. Reduce late-night mobile use, heavy meals, and coffee. If you snore loudly, stop breathing in your sleep, or feel excessively sleepy during the day, get yourself checked. Wearing a smartwatch to measure sleep is good; losing sleep while looking at its graph is not so smart.

Stay away from loneliness

Maintain friendships. I see people who are in seven WhatsApp groups but have no one to call at midnight. A hundred birthday messages arrive; half of them are sent by an app. Loneliness is not just mental sadness. It can slowly weaken the body's health and memory capacity. The body counts friendship in ways that cannot be counted in our contact list.

An elder from R.T. Nagar used to walk to buy a newspaper every day. The same newspaper also came to his house. In reality, he wasn't buying the newspaper. Someone in the shop would call him by name. They would argue about cricket. They would curse politics. They would also praise the expertise of a TV anchor. If he didn't come one day, they would notice. In those ten minutes, he got walking, morning sun, conversation, and the feeling of "I am part of this world" – everything. Sometimes health is hidden in practices that seem medically insignificant.
